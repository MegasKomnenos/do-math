## 1. The style of the previous versions

The observations are numbered so that Part 2 can refer to them. Quotations are from `README_before.md` unless marked "running before".

**S1. The subject of a sentence is an actor of the system, and the verb says what it does.** The subjects are a session, the runner, a peer, a review, the user, the frontier, a record. "The runner then launches sessions as the work requires" (14). "It keeps all of its work, from definitions and computations to approaches that failed, in its own working files" (16). The passive appears where the actor is known or does not matter: "the candidate is published as a record with that assessment attached, and the record is never edited afterwards" (18). Abstract nouns are rarely subjects; where one is, it is announced and then unpacked at once: "The first is continuity. A run can last for days and pass through hundreds of conversations" (20).

**S2. Sentences are declarative, in the present tense, and of middle length, and a qualification is attached to its claim inside the same sentence.** The usual joint is a semicolon or "and": "It never changes while the instance runs; to work on a different statement, you start a different instance" (32). "A pending concern changes no status by itself" is followed at once by what does happen (145). The semicolon is the characteristic mark, at 8.4 per thousand words in the README and 16.9 in the running guide. A colon introduces what a thing consists of: "Each run of do-math is an **instance**: a directory with its own Git repository" (32).

**S3. Short sentences state a fact; they are not there for cadence.** They open a paragraph with its topic or state a limit flatly: "An attempt has no time cap." (83), "The result has limits." (159), "There is no Lean proof of the root." (155), "The record never changes afterwards." (95). Two short sentences in a row occur once in the whole README (20). A short sentence never restates the paragraph it closes.

**S4. A term is introduced once, in bold, in a sentence that says what the thing is or does, and often with an example straight after.** Three constructions recur. The thing is described first and named second: "What a producing session leaves behind is its **retained work**: the contents of its own directory when it ends" (71). The name is added in apposition: "the guide that each role receives, called its **role guide**" (79). Or the name is the subject and the definition the predicate, with the number of values given first when there are several: "It gives each claim one of three statuses. A claim is *supported* when ..." (91). Ordinary words that the reader may not know are glossed in passing and without bold: "Lean 4, a proof assistant" (16), "standing inputs, which are instructions that reach every session launched after them" (14), "the do-math skill, a folder of instructions that these CLIs load" (193), "a local MCP server (a tool server that AI CLIs can call)" (236). Examples follow definitions: "such as a lemma that would imply the root" (34); "Suppose a session working on a node P proves that some statement Q implies P" (36). A term needed before its section is pointed forward by section title: "two levels of assurance that the section "What counts as established" defines" (34).

**S5. A paragraph does one thing, runs to about a hundred words, and is built as rule, then mechanism, then exception or limit.** Line 83 is the model: the rule ("An attempt has no time cap"), the mechanism (the session host resumes the conversation), the count ("up to three times"), the condition for ending, and last the roles to which it does and does not apply. The last sentence of a paragraph is one more fact or the limit, for example "there is no cap on the number of such continuations" (117) and "the runner applies no such filter itself" (109).

**S6. Counts are exact and enumerations are announced.** "Two considerations shaped this design. The first is continuity. ... The second is independent judgment." (20). "Six roles share the work" (40). "Two routes lead to it." (151). A procedure in prose is marked "First ... Next ... Finally" (16; running before 105).

**S7. A reason is given in the sentence it explains, with "because", "so" or "so that".** "The section counts as occupied until the resulting record is published or the review decides there is nothing to publish, because until then the work exists only in the attempt's own files, where no other session can see it, and a second attempt on the same part would repeat the work without knowing" (77). "Each piece of work is assessed by a session that took no part in producing it, so that no session certifies its own work" (20). Running before: "so that the instance's own Git repository is not nested inside a tracked tree" (70); "which is why compiles go through the broker" (66). The reason is concrete: it names what would go wrong.

**S8. Limits are stated as plain facts about the system, in the same voice as the claims, and next to them.** "This is a technical term of the system, and it means less than the words might suggest" (22). "No person has refereed the records" (24). "This default is part of the independent judgment described at the start, and it has a limit:" (93). "Whether do-math's mechanisms changed the quality of the research has not been measured" (159). "Linux has not been verified in a live run, and macOS is not supported" (187). The reader is told what to do with a limit: "a reader who does not accept an assumed error model should treat the results that depend on it as unproved" (137). The tables give limits a column of their own, "What it does not mean" (125). There are no hedging adverbs and no intensifiers.

**S9. Contrast is used where the reader would otherwise assume the other thing, and the usual form is "rather than" inside the sentence.** "Sessions coordinate through information rather than enforcement" (79). "it is asked to report the gap rather than write the proof and certify its own work" (91). "not by a person and not by a proof checker" (133). "Their workspace boundaries are instructions, not an operating-system sandbox" (189). Flat negatives about the system also occur, about once a section: "No session plans the work of another." (18), "An attempt is handed no node and no task." (75), "no session writes a separate account of the current state" (95). Each is said once.

**S10. Tables hold parallel items that share attributes; prose holds mechanism and reasons; bullets hold short separate items.** The role table gives each role what it receives and what it leaves (42–49), the support table what each kind of support means and does not mean (125–129), the command table each command and its purpose (running before 88–101). Each table comes after prose that has already defined its terms. Bullets are used for the defaults (running before 44–48, one sentence each), the standalone tools and the placeholders. Table cells in the running guide are full sentences that include the consequence: "`stop` does not end the run: the runner stays alive and running sessions continue" (running before 93).

**S11. The vocabulary is plain and the verbs are everyday ones.** keep, hold, leave, take up, hand on, carry, read, find, start. Figurative use is confined to dead metaphor: "the records are the research's memory" (20), "the assessment travels with the work" (20). Programs and commands are called by their names: "`init` refuses to create an instance when that environment is not installed" (running before 9); "several CLI processes" (83); "new CLI conversations" (119). A run is "the first research run" (22).

**S12. The system is described in the third person, and the reader is addressed as "you" only where the reader acts.** Instructions are imperatives with the purpose or condition placed first: "To set up an instance by hand instead, write a UTF-8 `root.md` containing the exact statement" (195); "With only Claude Code installed, list Claude models only" (running before 50); "To check the result, open `index.md`, go to `frontier.md` ..." (157).

**S13. The old versions are not uniformly easy.** Line 153 is a single sentence of 99 words, and line 175 opens with a file description packed into one sentence with five semicolons. Length and density alone are therefore not departures; what the old versions avoid is listed in S3, S7, S9 and S11.

## 2. Where the new versions depart from that style

### 2.1 Where the new versions keep the style well

The new documents keep most of what Part 1 describes, and in a few respects they do it better than the old ones.

- **Structure and openings.** The README keeps the "At a glance" table, the seven-paragraph introduction that ends with a paragraph saying what the rest of the document contains (25), the role table (86–93), the diagram with a paragraph that explains its boxes and arrows (95), and the table of "What it means" against "What it does not mean" (177–184). Topic sentences are in the old manner: "An attempt is handed no statement and no task." (119), "A published element is not a certificate." (175), "Some arguments rest on computation." (212).
- **Reasons inside the sentence.** The new README gives more reasons than the old (15 uses of "because" against 4), and most are concrete in the way S7 describes: "a definition left open would give every statement that used it an unknown object to speak about" (59); "It cannot tell the session what another attempt is discovering at the same moment, because that work exists only in the other attempt's files" (121); "since a review that supplied the argument and then confirmed it would be certifying its own work" (141); "Publications are made one at a time, because each changes the one library that every session reads" (145).
- **Limits as plain facts.** "Lean cannot tell whether the statement as written says what the user meant." (23). "This has two limits. The peer and the review share their default model." (143). "It does not compare either with the user's intent." (206). "The rounds were ended after the second, which still found defects of substance, and the record gives that as a reason to expect that some remain." (290). "They do not show that the real compiler gives those outputs, a difference that has already hidden two defects until a run with the real compiler exposed them" (299). These are the best sentences in the new README and they are fully in the old voice.
- **Terms.** Bold at first use, a definition in the same sentence, a gloss in passing for outside words: "ends in `sorry`, Lean's marker for a proof that is left open" (57); "`sorryAx`, the axiom that stands for a `sorry`" (198).
- **The running guide.** Its new sections are written as the old guide was: an instruction, then the reason that makes the instruction memorable. "If the definitions were in the root's module, those modules and the root's module would have to import each other, which Lean does not allow." (54). "an `import Mathlib` in a module that everything imports is paid for at every compile of the run" (94). "a nonempty proof section would make the canon show it as having one" (110). "subtraction of natural numbers stops at zero, division by zero returns zero, and a recursion can start one index early or late" (132). "a command that selects processes by name ends all of them together" (270). The command table keeps the full-sentence cells of the old one.
- **Sections that follow the old text closely** are unchanged in style: README 125, 129 (apart from the phrase in D24), 133, 159, 163, 216 and the whole of 301–323.

### 2.2 The departures

Twenty-eight departures follow, grouped by the observation of Part 1 that they depart from. Each gives the document, the line, the words, what differs, and a rewriting. Where I am not certain that a rewriting keeps the meaning, I say so.

#### A. Short sentences and antithesis used for emphasis (against S2, S3, S9)

The old versions attach a qualification to its claim inside one sentence and use a short sentence to state a fact. In the passages below the new README breaks one thought into two or three short sentences so that the break carries the emphasis, or sets a denial against an assertion as a figure. The counts agree: semicolons per thousand words fall from 8.4 to 4.6, runs of two short sentences rise from 1 to 4, and sentences with a negation rise from 26.7% to 33.3%.

**D1. README 57.**
> A proof takes one of two forms. It can be complete in Lean. Or it can be written in words in the record, while the Lean source carries a proof that names, one by one, the statements the argument rests on and then ends in `sorry`, Lean's marker for a proof that is left open.

Three sentences, the third beginning "Or", for one alternative. The old README announces the number and then gives the cases in a sentence (91).
Rewriting:
> A proof takes one of two forms. Either it is complete in Lean, or it is written in words in the record while the Lean source carries a proof that names, one by one, the statements the argument rests on and then ends in `sorry`, Lean's marker for a proof that is left open.

**D2. README 192.**
> Lean has not checked the argument. A review session has, by reading the candidate against the producing session's retained work, with three sub-reviewers reading it independently first. A person has not.

A three-beat figure with the verb left out of the second and third sentences. The old README says the same kind of thing in one sentence: "it was checked by an AI review session reading the candidate against the producer's retained work, not by a person and not by a proof checker" (133).
Rewriting:
> The argument itself has been checked by a review session, which read the candidate against the producing session's retained work after three sub-reviewers had read it independently, and not by Lean and not by a person.

**D3. README 145.**
> A review judges and does not gate.

A slogan. "Gate" as a verb is programmers' jargon that nothing in the document prepares, and the sentence that follows immediately takes part of it back ("It can decide that a candidate holds nothing worth keeping, in which case nothing is published"). The same point has already been made in plain words at line 19.
Rewriting, with the next sentence left as it is:
> A review does not keep a candidate out of the canon until its proofs are confirmed.

**D4. README 139.**
> One reading, however careful, has one set of blind spots, and the three readings widen the examination; the judgment itself remains the reviewer's own, made from the evidence with the three reports beside it, and it is neither a vote nor a compromise between them.

The reason for the three readings is given as a maxim about reading and not as a "because" attached to the fact (S7). "Widen the examination" is vague. "Neither a vote nor a compromise" denies two things that the reader had not supposed. The session is here called "the reviewer", and twice in this line, while everywhere else it is "the review".
Rewriting:
> Three readings are used so that the examination is wider than one reading, which, however careful, has one set of blind spots. The judgment remains the review's own: the review makes it from the evidence, with the three reports beside it, and does not reach it by a vote among the reports or by a compromise between them.

The same change of name applies earlier in the line, in "none sees another's report or the reviewer's own first reading", which becomes "the review's own first reading".

**D5. README 78 and 80.**
> Nobody writes it. (78)
> No session and no review admits a statement to the frontier. (80)

One fact, that the frontier is computed and not authored, is denied twice within two paragraphs, each time in a short sentence of its own. The old README makes such a denial once and inside the sentence that gives the positive fact: "The frontier, the index, the board and the shared glossary are rendered from the repository; no session writes a separate account of the current state" (95).
Rewriting of 78:
> Nobody writes it; the runner computes it from the canon each time it is shown. It starts at the root and lists the root's proofs, lists under each proof the statements that the proof names, leaves out those that are settled, and opens each unsettled statement in the same way.

Rewriting of 80:
> A statement appears in the frontier because some proof that leads to the root names it; no session and no review admits it. The quality of the frontier therefore depends on how the sessions state what they leave open.

**D6. README 157.**
> The canon holds no account of a session. What a session learns about the course of the work, as distinct from its results, is kept with the statement or the question that the work was aimed at.

The paragraph opens by denying something that has not come up. The reader does not yet know what "an account of a session" would be, so the denial has nothing to push against.
Rewriting:
> What a session learns about the course of the work, as distinct from its results, is kept with the statement or the question that the work was aimed at; the canon holds no account of a session as such.

**D7. running 21.**
> Any further tool server named in the settings does what it does.

A tautology with a shrug in it. The old guide says "together with that of any tool servers named in `mcp_servers`" (running before 17).
Rewriting:
> Any further tool server named in the settings adds whatever traffic it makes.

#### B. Definitions (against S4, S6)

**D8. README 39.**
> A declaration of the library together with its record is an **element**, and everything the research keeps as a result is an element. A definition is an element. A **statement** is an element too: in Lean it is a `theorem`, whatever a mathematician would call it, and it can stand with or without a proof, so that a conjecture and an open step of the root's proof are both statements.

All six sentences of this paragraph have "is" as their main verb, and "an element" closes three clauses running. The old README defines a term by a sentence about what happens ("As the sessions work they find further statements worth settling, such as a lemma that would imply the root, and each of these becomes a node of its own", 34). Line 15 of the new README already gives the division in one phrase: "a Lean declaration, which is either a definition or a statement".
Rewriting:
> A declaration of the library together with its record is an **element**, and everything the research keeps as a result is an element. An element is either a definition or a **statement**. In Lean a statement is a `theorem`, whatever a mathematician would call it, and it can stand with or without a proof, so that a conjecture and an open step of the root's proof are both statements.

**D9. README 55.**
> A record also gives the element's **weight**. A **unit** is a result that someone describing how the proof goes would name. A **step** is a result that lives inside one proof and exists because that proof was written in pieces.

"Weight" is named and never defined; the reader has to infer that unit and step are its two values. The old README gives the number of values first (S4, S6). The running guide says the same thing outright: "`unit` or `step`" (running 102).
Rewriting:
> A record also gives the element's **weight**, which is one of two. A **unit** is a result that someone describing how the proof goes would name. A **step** is a result that lives inside one proof and exists because that proof was written in pieces.

**D10. README 74.**
> The first three standings are the **work that remains**.

A standing is not work. The sentence equates two things of different kinds, which the old README's definitions do not do.
Rewriting:
> The statements that have one of the first three standings are the **work that remains**.

**D11. README 21.**
> Several considerations shaped this design. One is continuity. ... Another is independent judgment. ... A third is that a result should be one thing that later work can name.

The old sentence was "Two considerations shaped this design. The first is ... The second is ..." (20). The new one counts three and calls them "several", and the third is a clause where the first two are nouns.
Rewriting:
> Three considerations shaped this design. The first is continuity. ... The second is independent judgment. ... The third is that a result should be one thing that later work can name.

I cannot tell whether "several" was chosen to leave room for considerations that are not listed. If it was, "three" would add a claim, and the only change to make is "One ... Another ... A third" to "The first ... The second ... The third" after a sentence such as "Three of the considerations that shaped this design are these."

#### C. Reasons and abstract subjects (against S1, S7)

**D12. README 41.**
> A proof may use the imported library and the elements, and it can use nothing else. This is why sessions write what they find in Lean as they find it. Only what has a Lean statement can be named by a later proof, so a result that was found and left in prose can be read by the session that takes the work over and can be used by no proof.

"This is why" points back to the sentence before it, and the reason proper comes in the sentence after it, so the reader is told that a reason has been given before it has. "Can be used by no proof" puts the negative where English does not put it.
Rewriting:
> A proof may use the imported library and the elements, and it can use nothing else. Sessions therefore write what they find in Lean as they find it: only what has a Lean statement can be named by a later proof, so a result that was found and left in prose can be read by the session that takes the work over, but no proof can use it.

**D13. README 157.**
> The reason for keeping this by approach is that sessions keep coming while what is learned about the course of the work does not grow with their number: sessions approach the same statement in the same ways and stop in the same places.

The subject is a noun phrase ("The reason for keeping this by approach"), and the concrete fact, which is the real reason, comes last after an abstract restatement of it.
Rewriting:
> This is kept by approach because sessions approach the same statement in the same ways and stop in the same places, so that what is learned about the course of the work does not grow with the number of sessions.

"Sessions keep coming" is carried by "the number of sessions"; if the owner wants the growth said outright, "as more sessions come" can be added at the end.

**D14. README 188.**
> In both cases the element is published and other proofs may use it. What changes is that the statement keeps the standing "correct, held back", stays among the work that remains, and settles nothing that rests on it. The reason is that a statement with an accepted proof appears finished, and no session would then go on to find the simpler proof or the explanation.

"What changes is that the statement keeps" says that the change is that something stays. "The reason is that" stands apart from the thing it explains, and the reader has to work out that it explains why the proof is not accepted.
Rewriting:
> In both cases the element is published and other proofs may use it, but the statement keeps the standing "correct, held back", stays among the work that remains, and settles nothing that rests on it. The proof is not accepted because a statement with an accepted proof appears finished, and no session would then go on to find the simpler proof or the explanation.

**D15. README 123.**
> Choosing is serialized by the statement's **lease**.

A gerund as subject, a passive, and a technical verb. The old README introduced the same term with the session as subject and the effect in a "so that" clause: "While an attempt is choosing, it holds the node's **lease**, so that only one session at a time can be choosing a section on that node" (77).
Rewriting:
> While a session is choosing, it holds the statement's **lease**, so that only one session at a time can be choosing a section on that statement.

#### D. Wording that is elevated or inverted (against S11)

**D16. README 65.**
> The second is made only of a correct proof: whether it is accepted.

"Made of" reads first as "composed of".
Rewriting:
> The second, which is made only when the proof is correct, is whether to accept it.

**D17. README 188.**
> Of a correct proof, the review decides separately whether to accept it.

The fronted "Of a correct proof" is a construction of older formal English.
Rewriting:
> When a proof is correct, the review decides separately whether to accept it.

**D18. README 208.**
> For both, Lean's check of a complete proof stands where a review's judgment of the proof would stand, and the meaning of the statement rests on whoever wrote it.

"Stands where ... would stand" is a figure, and "rests on whoever wrote it" does not say what the reader should conclude.
Rewriting:
> For both, Lean's check of a complete proof takes the place of a review's judgment of the proof, and whether the statement says what was meant has been judged only by whoever wrote it.

I am fairly but not fully sure that this is what "rests on whoever wrote it" means; it agrees with "Two kinds of element have received no such judgment" in the same paragraph.

**D19. README 222.**
> Its first lines give the state of the root, and below them stands the frontier: the statements beneath the root that are not settled

Inverted word order.
Rewriting:
> Its first lines give the state of the root, and the frontier follows: the statements beneath the root that are not settled

**D20. README 147.**
> because statements stay as they are, whatever a waiting candidate rests on still stands as it stood

Rewriting:
> because statements stay as they are, every statement that a waiting candidate rests on is still the statement it was

**D21. README 141.**
> whether the record describes the element truly

Rewriting:
> whether the record describes the element accurately

The verb "stand" runs through the whole of the new README and is the clearest single mark of this register. The old README uses it as a verb four times in prose (101, 105, 137, 175). The new one uses it at 15 ("can stand without a proof"), 17 ("already stand there"), 39 ("can stand with or without a proof"), 53 ("where the statement stands in what was read"), 137 ("already stands in the canon"), 147 three times ("stands behind publication", "stands as it stood", "stands where the old proof stood"), 169 ("can stand under the name"), 179 ("stands alone"), 200 ("stand behind a formal proof"), 208 twice and 222. I do not count the idiom "stands for" at 21 and 198. Each use taken alone is acceptable English. Taken together they are a habit, and the repair is to keep the verb where a statement is said to be in the library without a proof (15, 39, 179) and to use "is", "is in", "replaces" or "lie behind" elsewhere.

## 3. The four things the owner asked about

### 3.1 How I understand the four

*Writing quality* I take to be whether each sentence says one definite thing, whether each paragraph does one job, and whether the order of the whole serves a reader who starts at the top. *Avoidance of AI writing* I take to be the absence of the habits by which machine-written prose is recognised: emphasis added by structure where the content did not call for it (a denial set up to be corrected, a short sentence that lands a point, items in threes by reflex, a closing line that restates), stock vocabulary, signposting, and formatting laid over what should be prose. *Likeness to human writing* I take to be the presence of what a particular knowledgeable person brings: reasons that come from having done the thing, exact detail where it matters, and one consistent voice. *Natural reading for human readers* I take to be whether a mathematician, a builder of AI systems or a person about to run the software can read each passage once, in order, and know what it says.
