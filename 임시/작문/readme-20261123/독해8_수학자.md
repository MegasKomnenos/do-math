# Reading 8: a research mathematician reads `README.md`

**What was read.** `C:\math\README.md`, lines 1 to 361, once, from the top, in order. I went back to earlier lines only when a later passage sent me there, and I say so where it happened. I opened no other file, followed no link, and did not see the page as GitHub renders it, so the Mermaid diagram at lines 114 to 130 was read as source and its appearance is not judged.

**Who is reading.** A research mathematician who has used Lean a little, knows nothing of how AI command-line tools or multi-agent systems work, and wants to know two things: what a result of this system can be trusted for, and whether the design is sound enough to try on a problem of their own.

Line numbers are those of the file as it stood when I read it (361 lines, 11,662 words by `wc -w`).

## 1. What I understood

This part was written before the others and has not been adjusted afterwards. The points are in the order of importance that the document seemed to me to give them, which is roughly the order of the table at the top followed by the order of the opening paragraphs.

1. **What the system is.** do-math sets many AI conversations, called sessions, to work on one mathematical statement, which they try to prove or to refute. No session remembers any other. They pass work to one another as files, and what is kept goes into a single Lean 4 library in which each entry has a prose record beside it. The user supplies the statement. An AI conversation called the operator writes the Lean form of the statement with the user, the user confirms it, and a program called the runner then launches sessions for as long as the research lasts. (Lines 5, 14.)

2. **When a run is complete.** Only under a strict condition: the statement fixed at the start, or its negation, has a proof that Lean has checked in full, that depends only on a permitted list of axioms (by default `propext`, `Classical.choice` and `Quot.sound`), and that carries the standing "accepted", as does a proof of everything beneath it. A proof that Lean accepts but that a review has not accepted does not complete the run. (Lines 6, 24, 86, 207, 217, 249.)

3. **What completion does not show.** It does not show that the Lean statement says what the user meant. The user settles that once, before the run, reading the Lean statement with the operator's explanation, and nothing checks it again. No person referees anything; every review is an AI session. (Lines 7, 24, 225.)

4. **How much has been shown to work.** Little, and the document says so plainly. One toy statement, that the sum of the first n odd numbers is n squared, was carried to a formal proof by six real sessions, all on one model, with no formalizer, organizer or comprehensive review launched. No problem is listed as solved. A run that imports Mathlib was started on 22 November 2026 and showed faults on its first day. The automated tests put stand-ins in place of both the AI sessions and the Lean compiler, the suite has not been run as a whole since the code last changed, that substitution has already hidden two defects, and two rounds of review of the software ended while defects of substance were still being found. (Lines 8, 9, 30, 317 to 322, 329.)

5. **What an entry of the library is.** An element is a Lean declaration, a definition or a theorem statement, together with its record. A statement may be in the library with no proof. If it has a proof, the proof is either complete in Lean, or it is written in words in the record while the Lean source cites, one by one, the statements the argument uses and then ends in `sorry`. In both cases Lean checks that the cited statements exist and are applied to objects of the right type. In the second case that is all Lean checks; the argument is judged by an AI review. (Lines 16, 40, 46 to 67.)

6. **How work enters the library.** Through three sessions in turn. A producing session (an attempt, an explorer or a formalizer) does the mathematics and keeps all its working. A peer takes that work over, continues it where it can, and writes the candidate, which is the proposed change to the library. A review judges each element of the candidate after three examinations of it and a compile of its own. If the review finds anything worth keeping, the whole candidate is published, and an element whose proof was not confirmed goes in labelled as such with the review's reason. A review never sends work back. A published statement is never altered; proofs and records can be replaced, and a later review can withdraw a proof's confirmation. (Lines 18, 20, 154 to 164.)

7. **Nobody plans.** Sessions read the library and choose their own work. The runner assigns only the follow-up work that the state of the research makes necessary. (Lines 20, 136.)

8. **Why it is designed so.** Three reasons: the library has to be the memory of a research effort whose workers forget; a candidate should be judged by a session that had no part in producing it, and the judgment should travel with the work so that imperfect work can still be published; and every result should have one name, which Lean enforces. (Line 22.)

9. **Standings, settled, formal.** A statement's proof has one of four standings: no proof; proof not confirmed; correct, held back; accepted. A statement is settled when one of its proofs is accepted and the same holds for everything that proof cites, all the way down. Settled is a matter of the reviews; formal is a matter of Lean and the axiom list. The two are independent, and completion needs both. The frontier is a computed view of what the root rests on that is not yet settled. (Lines 75 to 97.)

10. **What each standing can be trusted for.** A published element is not a certificate. The table at lines 196 to 203 says what each standing means and does not mean. A correct proof can be held back for two reasons that the author chose and a user can switch off: it can clearly be made simpler, or it gets part of its statement from a large computation that nobody has explained. A settled statement whose proof is in words is "a claim with a reviewed argument attached". A formal proof has been checked in up to three ways, the strongest being a program called the judge that re-derives the axioms and replays the proof through Lean's kernel. The judge's verdict depends on the library having been compiled honestly, and the sandbox that might guard that is not in effect. The judge's check of a finished root has never reached a verdict on a real instance. (Lines 194 to 221, 318.)

11. **What is enforced and what is merely asked.** A program enforces compilation, the existence and fit of cited names, the axiom list, the immutability of statements, and a few rules about who may replace a proof. Nothing enforces that a review made its three examinations, that sessions read the library first, that they kept off one another's work, or that the user confirmed the root. Sessions run with full access to the user's files and with no cap on time, sessions or usage. (Lines 229 to 233, 273.)

12. **The remaining machinery.** Questions and their "understanding so far"; the list of approaches tried on each statement; the board, sections and leases by which attempts avoid duplicating one another; the session host that resumes a conversation; the organizer that joins duplicates by Lean-proved statements of agreement; the broker through which all compilation passes; concerns and findings, by which a confirmation is withdrawn. (Lines 134 to 190, 166 to 176, 241 to 243.)

13. **How to read a run.** Begin with `knowledge/frontier.md`; the root is in one of six states, of which "informally proved" means that AI reviews have accepted everything and Lean has not checked all of it. A table gives the paths. (Lines 247 to 265.)

14. **How to run it.** Requirements, what a run does on the machine, a quick start through the operator, and the same by hand. (Lines 271 to 307.)

15. **The rest.** Repository layout, four unrelated programs, development skills, language, citation, licence. (Lines 311 to 361.)

## 2. Where I had difficulty

### 2a. Difficulties that bear on what a result can be trusted for, or on what the system does

For each item I say whether I came away with a belief that the document later corrected, or with a guess that the document never resolves. Both kinds are here because either can leave this reader wrong.

**A. Line 5 (and line 14): "written in Lean 4" is easily heard as "proved in Lean 4".**
Quoted: "Every result they keep is written in Lean 4, a proof assistant, as part of one library, with a written record beside it." And at line 14: "the results the research keeps are added to one library written in Lean 4, a proof assistant".
Reason: to someone who has used Lean, a result "written in Lean, as part of one library" is a result Lean has checked. On first reading I took the row to say that everything kept is machine-checked. Line 16 corrects this ("A statement can be in the library without a proof"; a proof may be "written in the record"), and line 20 adds that an element with an unconfirmed proof also enters. But the table is the part a reader is most likely to stop after, and no row of it says that a kept result may have only a prose argument that an AI reviewed, or a proof that a review could not confirm. Row 6 implies it only by contrast. This is a belief the document corrects later, for a reader who reads on.

**B. Line 18 (with lines 156, 233): "examined three times, independently" promises more independence than the body delivers.**
Quoted, line 18: "Before it judges, it has the candidate examined three times, independently, against the producing session's files". Line 156: "A review session on Claude Code delegates the three examinations to sub-agents, none of which sees another's report or the review's own first reading; a review session on Codex, which cannot delegate, makes them itself as three separate passes." Line 233: "No program checks that a review made its three examinations".
Reason: three things. First, line 18 does not say who examines, and "independently" suggested three separate examiners to me. Second, at line 156 one of the two tools has the same conversation make all three passes, which is not independence in the sense a mathematician gives the word when speaking of referees; line 156 nevertheless opens with "each time independently" for both tools. Third, "sub-agents" is never explained: I do not know whether a sub-agent is another AI conversation, whether it is the same model, or what it is given. I guessed that it is a fresh conversation of the same model started by the review. A reader who stops after the opening believes in three independent checks; a reader who reads on finds the claim weakened in three places that are far apart.

**C. "Statement" names two things, and it is therefore not said whether a published definition can change (lines 20, 40, 51, 67, 162, 164).**
Quoted, line 40: "An element is either a definition or a **statement**." Line 20: "The statement of a published element never changes afterwards. A proof or a record can be replaced by a better one". Line 164: "the statement of an element is never changed in place". Line 162: "no statement changed in place". Line 67: "it gives for each declaration the text of its statement". Line 51: "The statement, or the definition, in ordinary mathematical language."
Reason: at line 40 a statement is one of two kinds of element, the other being a definition. In the immutability rule, "the statement of an element" must then mean something else, the Lean text of a declaration, or else the rule says nothing about definitions. The meaning of the root, and of every theorem, rests on the bodies of the definitions it mentions, so whether a definition's body is fixed after publication matters as much as whether a theorem's statement is. I guessed that it is fixed. The document does not say so in words that cover a definition, and the only passage that speaks of definitions being compared is the judge's check of the root at line 221 ("identical together with every definition they depend on"), which an instance may not have. This is an unresolved guess.

**D. Line 84 (with lines 88, 186, 221): what makes two declarations "the same statement" is not said, and completion seems to depend on it.**
Quoted, line 84: "A statement can have more than one proof, because a further proof is a further declaration that states the same thing, and each proof has a standing of its own." Line 88: "It starts at the root and lists the root's proofs". Line 221: "the judge compares the root that was proved with the root as the instance's first commit fixed it". Against these, line 164: "A proof that replaces another takes its place, under the same name and statement."
Reason: I read line 84 three times. Since Lean rejects a second declaration of a name (line 40), a "further declaration that states the same thing" has another name. Then a "statement" in the sense of line 84 is no longer one declaration but a class of declarations, and something must decide membership: identical Lean text, a proof of equivalence, or a session's say-so. The document does not tell me which. It matters because completion is defined for "the Lean statement fixed at the start" (line 24), while line 221 speaks of "the root that was proved" as something that has to be compared with the fixed root, which suggests that the declaration carrying the formal proof may not be the original root declaration. I also could not reconcile "each proof has a standing of its own" with line 75, "Every statement of the canon has one of four **standings**", and with the frontier line, which shows one standing beside a statement's name; and I could not tell when a new proof is a replacement under the same name (line 164) and when it is a further declaration (line 84). This is an unresolved guess on a point that the definition of completion rests on.

**E. Lines 86, 219, 221, 249, 271, 318: I cannot tell whether the judge's verdict on the root is part of what "complete" means.**
Quoted, line 86: "The instance is complete when the root, or a statement that Lean has confirmed to be the negation of the root, is settled through a proof that is formal." Line 221: "When the root has become formally settled and the instance has a judge, the runner starts the judge on the root itself." Line 249: "The instance is complete in the two formal states." Line 271: "with an earlier Lean a run proceeds without the judge". Line 318: "the command that judges a settled root had not been carried to a verdict on a real instance when the document was written".
Reason: by lines 86 and 249 the instance is complete as soon as the root is settled through a formal proof, and the judge runs afterwards. Nothing says what follows if the judge then rejects. A run on Lean older than 4.35 has no judge at all, and on such a run I do not know what stands in for the comparison of "the root that was proved" with the root as first committed. Reading line 219 alone, I had formed the belief that every formal proof has been replayed through the kernel by an independent program; "up to three ways" and "where the Lean installation provides the tools" qualify that, and line 318 says the root-level check has never completed on a real instance. A reader could easily come away thinking that a complete run always carries the judge's verdict. The document does not say that it does, nor that it does not.

**F. Line 221: "compiled honestly" names a weakness in the strongest check without saying how large it is.**
Quoted: "The judge's verdict holds only if the library was compiled honestly, because Lean code that a session wrote runs when the library is compiled. The comparator has a sandbox in which it can build what it judges, but do-math gives it modules that its own broker has already compiled, so that sandbox does not cover those builds on any system, and on Windows, where it cannot run, it is switched off."
Reason: I read this passage three times and still cannot say what it tells me to believe. "Compiled honestly" is not explained. I guessed the meaning to be that a Lean file can contain code that executes during compilation, so a session could, in principle, write a file that interferes with what gets compiled or recorded. But line 219 says the comparator "works out the axioms again from that export alone and replays every declaration through Lean's kernel", which I had taken to be immune to whatever happened during compilation. The two together leave me unable to tell whether the exposure is that a false proof could be passed, that the statement judged could differ from the statement shown, or something narrower. In the second sentence I also had to work out that both "it"s refer to the sandbox and not to the comparator or to do-math. This is the one passage that qualifies the document's highest level of assurance, and it is the one I understood least.

**G. Line 71: what standing a result taken from the literature receives is not said.**
Quoted: "Its statement is written in Lean in terms of the instance's own definitions, its record names the source and says how much of the source was read, and the review judges the element on what was read. If the root comes to rest on such a result, the result needs a complete Lean proof before the run is complete, as every other statement does."
Reason: "judges the element on what was read" does not say what is judged or with what outcome. I cannot tell whether a citation counts as a proof written in words, and so whether a cited theorem can be "accepted" and then "settled" on the strength of a source that an AI session read in part. If it can, then "settled", and with it the root state "informally proved" (line 249), may rest on an AI's report of what a paper says, which is a different thing from "a claim with a reviewed argument attached" (line 211). If it cannot, a cited result is simply an open statement, and the sentence about needing a Lean proof before completion would be true of it but would not be the important thing to say. Formal completion is unaffected either way; what the informal standings can be trusted for is not clear.

## 3. The writing

### Findings on the four criteria

**Writing quality.** High, and highest where it matters most to me. The document defines nearly every term at first use in bold and then holds to it; it says who does what in almost every sentence; and it states its own limits with the same precision as its claims. I count four weaknesses. Two words are overloaded: "statement" (item 2a C) and the role names "review" and "attempt", which name an actor in one sentence ("the review judges the candidate", line 18) and are distinguished from the actor in another ("An attempt has no time cap. Its session is a conversation", line 146). The document says the same thing at several depths, which is right for the table and the opening but means that by line 67 I was reading for the third or fourth time that Lean checks the cited names and a review checks the prose (lines 16, 58, 67, and again at 211). The third weakness is proportion, discussed under natural reading. The fourth is the passage at line 221, where the writing is least clear exactly where the reader's trust is at stake.

**Avoidance of AI writing.** Largely achieved. I found no promotional vocabulary, no hedging of every claim alike, no rhetorical questions, no summary sentence closing each paragraph, no three-item lists made for rhythm, and no announcements of what is about to be said beyond the one at line 26. The numbers and dates are specific (22 modules, 763 test functions, six sessions, 22 November 2026), and the admissions are concrete rather than gestural. What remains that I associate with machine drafting is slight. One sentence is a slogan built from two abstract nouns: "Sessions coordinate through information rather than enforcement" (line 142). And the document has a habit of announcing a count before every enumeration: "Three considerations shaped this design" (22), "in three stages" (18), "on three points" (18), "Six roles share the work" (101), "there are two weights" (56), "one of four **standings**" (75), "This has two limits" (160), "Two considerations can keep" (207), "in up to three ways" (219), "Two kinds of element" (227), "it is one of six" (249), "Two further commands" (265). Each is useful where it stands; together they give the prose a mechanical regularity.

**Likeness to human writing.** It reads like a careful person writing a specification, nearer to the drafting of a standard, or to a well-made translation of a technical text, than to conversational prose. Several passages sound like someone who has done mathematics: the examples of questions at line 168, the third consideration at line 22, the reason for refusing an open definition at line 69, and the remark on accepted and settled at line 213. Two things read less like a person. One is evenness: every paragraph has the same measured cadence and the same degree of emphasis, so that a sentence I would underline ("A published element is not a certificate", line 194) sounds no different from a sentence about module caching. The verification section, where the author evidently speaks from experience, is the only place where the voice changes. The other is a set of constructions that read as though the writer were steering round a forbidden phrase: "to show the structure of a proof and not every lemma" (56), "one for each approach, and not one for each session" (174), "it is their instructions, and no barrier, that keep them" (233). A native reader notices these as slightly off.

**Natural reading for a human reader.** Sentence by sentence, yes: I rarely had to reparse a sentence, and the items in part 2 are almost all questions of what is meant rather than of syntax. As a whole, the document is long for a front page (11,662 words), and its middle reads as a manual. The opening, to line 26, is 1,466 words and gave me a fair picture, subject to items 2a A and B. From "The choice of work" to the broker (lines 134 to 190, 3,162 words) the subject is leases, session hosts, resumption limits, launch order and compile budgets, which answer an operator's questions and neither of mine. The section that answers my first question, "What counts as established", begins at line 192, after 7,096 words. It cannot simply be moved up, since it needs the terms of "Standings and the frontier"; but nothing at the head of "How the research is organized" tells a reader with my questions which subsections bear on trust and which can be passed over, and I read the operational ones with flagging attention. My attention returned at line 192 and held to line 243, which I thought the best part of the document.

### Passages I would change

Each rewriting keeps the meaning as I understood it. Where my understanding rests on a guess, I say so, and the author should check the rewriting against the facts.

1. **Line 5.** "Every result they keep is written in Lean 4, a proof assistant, as part of one library, with a written record beside it."
   What is wrong: "written in Lean" is heard as "proved in Lean" (2a A).
   Rewriting: "Every result they keep is stated in Lean 4, a proof assistant, as part of one library, with a written record beside it. Its proof may be complete in Lean, or it may be an argument in words that only an AI review has checked."

2. **Line 6.** "and when that proof and a proof of every statement it rests on have been accepted, a term defined below."
   What is wrong: the criterion turns on a word left blank (2b 1).
   Rewriting: "and when that proof and a proof of every statement it rests on have been accepted, which means that a review found the proof correct and saw no reason to hold it back for a simpler or better-understood one." (This leaves out the case at line 84 in which a Lean-complete proof that came with the root or from an organizer counts as accepted without a review; the author must decide whether the table can bear that omission.)

3. **Line 8.** "One small statement has been carried to a formal proof by real AI sessions and the real Lean compiler; the tests replace both by stand-ins."
   What is wrong: "the tests" are not yet known to the reader, and the clause seems to qualify the first (2b 2).
   Rewriting: "One small statement has been carried to a formal proof by real AI sessions and the real Lean compiler. The software's automated tests use neither; they put stand-ins in place of both."

4. **Line 14.** "The operator sets up the run and operates it on the user's behalf"
   What is wrong: "the operator ... operates it" says nothing about what the operator does.
   Rewriting: "The operator sets up the run and looks after it on the user's behalf"

5. **Line 18.** "Before it judges, it has the candidate examined three times, independently, against the producing session's files, and it compiles the candidate itself."
   What is wrong: who examines is not said, and "independently" overstates what line 156 describes (2a B).
   Rewriting: "Before it judges, it has the candidate examined three times against the producing session's files, each examination made without sight of the others where the review's tool allows that, and it compiles the candidate itself."

6. **Line 26.** "The rest of this document lists the solved problems, and then explains"
   What is wrong: it promises a list, and the section it points to says "None is listed yet".
   Rewriting: "The rest of this document has a section for solved problems, which is still empty, and then explains"

7. **Line 56.** "The views described below use the weight to show the structure of a proof and not every lemma; a step remains an element, and proofs name it."
   What is wrong: "and not every lemma" is not idiomatic and has to be read twice.
   Rewriting: "The views described below use the weight to show the structure of a proof without listing every lemma; a step is still an element, and proofs name it."

8. **Lines 80 and 199.** "A proof was offered, and a review found a gap in it, could not complete its judgment, or has not judged it."
   What is wrong: "a review ... has not judged it" makes a review the subject of a case in which there has been no review. Line 211 already has the right form.
   Rewriting: "A proof was offered, and a review found a gap in it or could not complete its judgment, or no review has judged it yet."

9. **Line 142.** "Sessions coordinate through information rather than enforcement."
   What is wrong: an abstract slogan in a document that otherwise says things directly; the next sentence already says it concretely.
   Rewriting: "Sessions are kept out of one another's way by what they are told, and only in a few cases by a program that refuses them."

10. **Line 164.** "A statement that supersedes another enters under a new name, and the old element keeps its name and statement; where the old statement follows from the new one, the old element's proof becomes a one-line derivation from it, at once if the old statement was not settled and otherwise once the new statement is settled as well."
    What is wrong: too much in one sentence, with the condition trailing (2b 7).
    Rewriting: "A statement that supersedes another enters under a new name, and the old element keeps its name and statement. Where the old statement follows from the new one, the old element's proof is replaced by a one-line derivation from the new statement. If the old statement was not settled, this is done at once; if it was settled, it is done only when the new statement is settled too."

11. **Line 207.** "Such a proof is held back because the research should have the simpler proof, which is the more concise one."
    What is wrong: the appended clause reads as an afterthought, and I could not tell whether it defines "simpler" or adds a second property.
    Rewriting, if it defines: "Such a proof is held back so that the run ends with the simpler proof, simpler here meaning more concise."

12. **Line 221.** "The judge's verdict holds only if the library was compiled honestly, because Lean code that a session wrote runs when the library is compiled. The comparator has a sandbox in which it can build what it judges, but do-math gives it modules that its own broker has already compiled, so that sandbox does not cover those builds on any system, and on Windows, where it cannot run, it is switched off."
    What is wrong: "compiled honestly" is unexplained, the consequence for the reader is not drawn, and the pronouns of the second sentence are hard to assign (2a F).

## 4. What I could not determine

### What I would still want to know

1. **How often an AI review is wrong.** The whole of "settled" and "informally proved" rests on reviews, and the document gives no observation of a review accepting a faulty prose proof, nor says that none has been looked for. The one completed run was a toy with all six sessions on one model. I have no basis for judging how much weight "accepted" carries on real mathematics.
2. **Whether the system has yet done anything on a problem of research interest**, and how the Mathlib run that began on 22 November 2026 stands. The document says only that it began and showed faults.
3. **What a run costs.** Line 273 says there is no cap on time, sessions or usage, and line 319 says the quantities that decide the cost have not been measured. A reader deciding whether to try it on a problem has no estimate of any kind, not even for the toy run.
4. **How the ways in which Lean itself can be bent are handled.** `axiom`, `sorry` in a definition and `native_decide` are covered. I would want to know about `opaque` declarations, `unsafe` and `implemented_by`, instances added to change what a later statement elaborates to, and notation or macros defined by a session that make a statement read differently from what it says. Line 227 gives the review the job of judging what a statement says; I do not know whether anything mechanical supports it.
5. **How "a statement that Lean has confirmed to be the negation of the root" (line 86) is confirmed**: whether the statement must be literally `¬ root`, or whether an equivalent form with a Lean proof of equivalence is admitted.
6. **Whether the list of allowed axioms can change during a run**, and who can change it.
7. **Whether a person can act on the record of a run**: for instance whether a human referee who finds an error in a prose proof can enter a finding, or only start a comprehensive review through the operator (line 241).
8. **What happens to the work of a run when the root's Lean statement proves to be wrong.** Line 38 says a new instance is required; whether the canon of the old one can be carried over is not said.
9. **What the paper command (line 265) produces and whether anything checks it**, since that paper is presumably what another mathematician would be shown.
10. **Whether the six states of the root hide a formal proof that is not accepted.** By lines 207 and 249, a root with a complete Lean proof on the allowed axioms that a review has held back is shown as "unresolved". I would want that case to be visible to someone reading `knowledge/frontier.md`, and I cannot tell whether it is.
