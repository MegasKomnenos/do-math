# Reading 7: the writing of the new README and running guide

This report reads two documents as writing: `C:\math\README.md` and `C:\math\docs\do-math\running.md`. It compares them with the versions they replace, `README_before.md` and `running_before.md` in this folder. I read those four files in full and nothing else. I did not check any fact, and where a rewriting below depends on a fact I could not see in the four files, I say so beside it.

Line numbers refer to the files as they stood on 23 November 2026. "Before" means the replaced versions, and "new" means the current ones.

The finding in short: the new documents keep the style of the old ones closely, and the faults are local. Three kinds recur. Some sentences carry more than one sentence can hold. Some words are used as terms before they are introduced, or in two senses. And about a dozen phrases are stiff where a plain phrase was available. The running guide is the better written of the two.

## 1. The style of the previous versions

**The subject of a sentence is the thing that acts.** Sessions, the runner, the review, the user and the record do things, in the present tense and with plain verbs such as holds, reads, gives, keeps and decides.

- "No session plans the work of another." (README_before L18)
- "It gives each claim one of three statuses." (L91)
- "The **runner** starts all of these as new CLI conversations under the user's existing authentication." (L119)

The passive appears where the agent does not matter ("the candidate is published as a record", L18). What happened in a run is told in the past tense with its date and its number ("On 12 November 2026 the suite had 316 tests and all passed.", L230).

**A term is introduced once, in bold, and defined in the sentence that introduces it.** The definition comes after a colon, in an apposition, or with "called".

- "Each run of do-math is an **instance**: a directory with its own Git repository." (L32)
- "standing inputs, which are instructions that reach every session launched after them" (L14)
- "Lean 4, a proof assistant" (L16)
- "the guide that each role receives, called its **role guide**" (L79)

The bold terms are ordinary nouns: instance, root, node, relation, pipeline, retained work, lease, finding. After its introduction a term is used unchanged, and no bold term is used in a second sense. Even the summary table at the top glosses what it uses: "*Informally proved* on 11 November 2026, a status defined below. In brief, ..." (L7).

**A paragraph does one job and usually opens with a short sentence that says what the job is.**

- "An attempt is handed no node and no task." (L75)
- "An attempt has no time cap." (L83)
- "A published record is not a certificate for everything it says." (L123)
- "The result has limits." (L159)

Paragraphs run from three to eight sentences. Sentences of six to ten words stand between sentences of twenty-five to forty-five, and a long sentence usually holds one statement with one condition or one reason.

**A reason is given in the sentence it explains.** The connectives are "so", "because", "so that" and "which is why".

- "A run can last for days and pass through hundreds of conversations, none of which remembers the one before, so the records are the research's memory from one session to the next" (L20)
- "because until then the work exists only in the attempt's own files, where no other session can see it" (L77)
- "which is why compiles go through the broker" (running_before L66)

**A limit is stated as a flat fact, beside the claim it limits.** Nothing softens it.

- "No person has refereed the work." (L7)
- "This is a technical term of the system, and it means less than the words might suggest" (L22)
- "This default is part of the independent judgment described at the start, and it has a limit:" (L93)
- "Whether do-math's mechanisms changed the quality of the research has not been measured" (L159)
- "a reader who does not accept an assumed error model should treat the results that depend on it as unproved" (L137)

The tables do the same with a column headed "What it does not mean" (L125).

**Tables and lists hold parallel items, and prose holds reasoning.** A table is used where each item has the same attributes: the roles with what each receives and leaves (L42 to L49), the kinds of support (L125 to L129), the commands (running_before L88 to L101). A bulleted list is used for discrete items: the defaults (running_before L44 to L48), the standalone tools (L236 to L239). Code blocks are introduced by a sentence that ends in a colon.

**Punctuation and connection are plain.** Semicolons join clauses that belong together ("The board shows which sections are occupied; the guide ... explains how to respect that; and the sessions are trusted to follow the guide.", L79). Colons open an expansion. There are no dashes. Contrast is made with "rather than" ("Sessions coordinate through information rather than enforcement.", L79). A sentence may begin with "And" (L141).

**The reader is addressed only where the reader acts.** The text is in the third person. "You" and the imperative appear in the parts about running the software ("Clone this repository.", L193; "under your account", L189) and in the instructions for checking a result (L157). There is no "we".

**The reader is told the plan once.** There is one paragraph of that kind at the end of the introduction (L26) and one at the head of the running guide (running_before L3). Sections otherwise open with their content. A reference to another section names it in quotation marks ('the section "What counts as established" defines', L34).

## 2. Where the new versions depart from that style

### Where the style is kept well

Most of both documents is written as the old ones were. These passages show it best.

- README L14 carries the old opening over and adds the glosses the new content needs ("two AI command-line tools", "a proof assistant").
- README L38, on the root, uses short declaratives with a named actor and puts the consequence at the end ("requires a new instance").
- README L136 to L142, on the choice of work, opens with "An attempt is handed no statement and no task." and gives each reason in its own sentence ("because that work exists only in the other attempt's files").
- README L160 states the limit as the old text did: "This has two limits."
- README L194 with the table at L196 to L203 keeps the opening "A published element is not a certificate." and the column "What it does not mean".
- README L211 keeps the old instruction to the sceptical reader: "A reader who wants more assurance than an AI review gives should treat a settled statement whose proof is not formal as a claim with a reviewed argument attached."
- README L311 to L316 states what was and was not verified in plain negatives with numbers: "It launched no formalizer, organizer or comprehensive review."
- running L54 gives the reason for an instruction the old way: "which Lean does not allow".
- running L94: "an `import Mathlib` in a module that everything imports is paid for at every compile of the run".
- running L132 gives three concrete cases as its reason: "subtraction of natural numbers stops at zero, division by zero returns zero, and a recursion can start one index early or late".
- running L196: "that is a setting of your machine and yours to decide".
- running L300: "End each of these processes by its ID and never by its name."

### Departures

**2.1 README L207. The reason stands in a sentence of its own, framed "The reason is that".**

> "The reason is that the simpler proof is the more concise one, and the research should have it."
>
> "The reason is that a statement with an accepted proof appears finished, and no session would then go on to explain why that part is true."

The old text attaches a reason to its sentence with "because" or "so". Here the frame is repeated twice in one paragraph ("The first is that ... The reason is that ... The second is that ... The reason is that ..."). The first reason also says little, because "simpler" and "more concise" are nearly the same word. Rewriting, from "The first is" to the end of the second reason:

> The first is that the proof can still clearly be made simpler: the review can point to a place in the proof and say how it could be simplified there. Such a proof is held back because the research should have the simpler proof, which is the more concise one. The second is that the proof establishes part of its statement only by a large computation carried out without an understanding of why that part holds. Such a proof is held back because a statement with an accepted proof appears finished, and no session would then go on to explain why that part is true.

**2.2 README L146. A passive hides the actor that the old sentence named.**

> "or when the limit is reached, in which case the end is authorized without a decision from the session"

The old sentence read "or when the limit is reached and the runner authorizes the end" (README_before L83). Rewriting:

> or when the limit is reached, in which case the runner authorizes the end without a decision from the session

I cannot tell from the four files whether the runner is still the one that authorizes the end. If it is the session host, the rewriting should name the host.

**2.3 README L170, L172 and L174. Two bold terms are phrases that do not behave as nouns.**

> "Each question carries its **understanding so far**, a text that says what is now understood of the matter the question asks about, and what is not." (L170)
>
> "A question is resolved when a review judges that its understanding so far answers what its statement asks" (L172)
>
> "A statement whose proof is not accepted carries its **approaches tried**, one entry for each approach, saying what the idea was, what the approach established and where it stops, with the review's assessment of what that work contributed added beneath." (L174)

The old bold terms were nouns that take an article and a plural in the ordinary way. "Its understanding so far answers" and "carries its approaches tried" make the reader stop to see that a name is meant. I take the names to be the system's own, so the rewriting keeps them and gives each a noun to lean on.

> Each question carries a text called its **understanding so far**, which says what is now understood of the matter the question asks about, and what is not. (L170)
>
> A question is resolved when a review judges that the text of its understanding so far answers what the question asks (L172)
>
> A statement whose proof is not accepted carries a list of the **approaches tried** on it, with one entry for each approach. An entry says what the idea was, what the approach established and where it stops, and the review's assessment of what that work contributed is added beneath it. (L174)

**2.4 README L5 and L6. The summary table uses terms that it does not gloss.**

> "Every result the research keeps is a declaration in one Lean 4 library, with a written record beside it." (L5)
>
> "Only when the statement or its negation has a proof that is complete in Lean, uses only the allowed axioms and has been accepted, together with a proof of everything it rests on." (L6)

The old table glossed its terms where it used them. It wrote "a status defined below. In brief, ..." (README_before L7) and "formalization, the writing of machine-checked proofs in Lean 4" (L8). Here "declaration" is a Lean word that the reader has not met, Lean itself is not explained until L14, and "accepted" is a term of the system that reads as an ordinary word. L6 is also hard to parse, because "together with a proof of everything it rests on" can attach to "has a proof" or to "has been accepted". Rewriting:

> Every result the research keeps is written in Lean 4, a proof assistant, as part of one library, with a written record beside it. (L5)
>
> Only when the statement or its negation has a proof that is complete in Lean and uses only the allowed axioms, and when that proof and a proof of every statement it rests on have been accepted, a term defined below. (L6)

In L5 the rewriting drops the word "declaration". I believe that this changes no fact, since L16 introduces the word with its meaning.

**2.5 README L30 and L36. A section says what it is about to do.**

> "This section lists the problems that do-math has formally proved and that have been organized into publishable form. None is listed yet." (L30)
>
> "and two folders that the following paragraphs describe. `canon/` holds the mathematics of the run. `knowledge/` holds the views that the runner derives from `canon/`, together with the published judgments of the reviews." (L36)

The old sections open with their content. In L30 the wording is also off: one proves a statement, and a problem is solved. In L36 the pointer is inaccurate, because the next two sentences of the same paragraph describe the folders. Rewriting:

> The problems whose statements do-math has formally proved, and whose results have been put into a form that can be published, will be listed here. None is listed yet. (L30)
>
> a directory for every session that has run, and two folders: `canon/`, which holds the mathematics of the run, and `knowledge/`, which holds the views that the runner derives from `canon/` together with the published judgments of the reviews. (L36)

I cannot tell what "organized into publishable form" refers to, so the L30 rewriting keeps it as general as the original.

**2.6 running L208. Nine parallel items stand in one sentence, inside a paragraph that does four jobs.**

> "It refuses, among other things, a library that does not compile, a value check that fails, a declaration without a record or a record without a declaration, a record without a weight or a description, an `axiom`, a definition that contains `sorry`, a root that is not a theorem or that already has a proof, a settings file that is not valid, and a tool server whose command is not found."

The old guide put parallel discrete items in a list (running_before L44 to L48), and the new guide does so itself for the defaults (L153 to L162). The paragraph at L208 explains the flags, says what `init` does, lists what it refuses, and says what to do after a refusal. Rewriting of the whole paragraph:

> `--root` is the root file, `--canon` the folder with the Lean library, and `--root-declaration` the full Lean name of the root; all three are required. `--settings` is optional, and without it every default applies.
>
> `init` copies the files into the instance, compiles the library, extracts the structural index and makes the first Git commit. Nothing in do-math compiles the folder before this, so `init` is where you learn whether it compiles; with files that import Mathlib it takes minutes.
>
> `init` refuses, among other things:
>
> - a library that does not compile, or a value check that fails;
> - a declaration without a record, or a record without a declaration;
> - a record without a weight or a description;
> - an `axiom`, or a definition that contains `sorry`;
> - a root that is not a theorem, or that already has a proof;
> - a settings file that is not valid, or a tool server whose command is not found.
>
> A refusal names the cause. If `init` refuses after it has created the directory, the partial directory is not an instance; correct the files and run `init` again on a new path.

## 3. The four things the owner asked about

I went through both documents from beginning to end. The passages below are the ones I would change if the documents were mine. They are grouped by fault, because three faults recur and each is repaired by going through all of its places together. Passages already treated in part 2 are not repeated.

### Fault A: a sentence that carries more than it can hold

The sentence holds a list whose items bring their own qualifications, or relative clauses stacked on one another, or a qualification wedged between a verb and its object. The reader has to read it twice. It occurs at README L18, L22, L67, L107, L168, L219, L267, L307 and L323, and at running L9 and L13. README L6 and running L208, treated in part 2, are of the same kind.

**A1. README L18.**

> "Finally, a third session, the review, has the candidate examined three times, independently, against the producing session's files, compiles the candidate itself, and then judges each element: whether its Lean statement says what its record says, whether its proof is correct, and whether a correct proof should also be accepted, a second judgment that is explained below."

Three actions, a list of three questions, and a pointer hang on one subject. Rewriting:

> Finally, a third session, the review, judges the candidate. Before it judges, it has the candidate examined three times, independently, against the producing session's files, and it compiles the candidate itself. It then judges each element on three points: whether its Lean statement says what its record says, whether its proof is correct, and whether a correct proof should also be accepted. The last of these is a second judgment, which is explained below.

**A2. README L22.**

> "A candidate is judged by a session that took no part in producing it, and because the judgment travels with the work, useful work that is not yet perfect can still be published, and a later session can tell an established result from one that still needs work."
>
> "A result stated in Lean has one name, a proof that uses it must cite that name, and Lean checks the citation."

The first sentence joins a fact and a two-part consequence with three "and"s. The old text made two sentences of the same material (README_before L20). The second sentence runs three clauses together with commas. Rewriting:

> A candidate is judged by a session that took no part in producing it. Because the judgment travels with the work, useful work that is not yet perfect can still be published, and a later session can tell an established result from one that still needs work.
>
> A result stated in Lean has one name; a proof that uses it must cite that name, and Lean checks the citation.

**A3. README L67.**

> "Whenever a publication changes the Lean files, the runner extracts again from the compiled library a **structural index**, the file `canon/index.json`: for each declaration, the text of its statement, what the statement and the proof use, whether the Lean proof is complete, and the axioms the proof depends on."

The object of "extracts" comes after two adverbial phrases, and the new term arrives with an apposition and a colon behind it. Rewriting:

> Whenever a publication changes the Lean files, the runner extracts the **structural index** again from the compiled library. The index is the file `canon/index.json`, and it gives for each declaration the text of its statement, what the statement and the proof use, whether the Lean proof is complete, and the axioms the proof depends on.

**A4. README L107, the formalizer's cell in the table of roles.**

> "One settled statement that the root, or a settled refutation of the root, rests on, whose accepted proof is not yet formal and names only statements that already have formal proofs"

Two relative clauses hang on "statement", and the first is interrupted by an alternative. Rewriting:

> One settled statement whose accepted proof is not yet formal and names only statements that already have formal proofs. The statement is one that the root rests on, or one that a settled refutation of the root rests on.

**A5. README L168.**

> "An explorer takes one question, and a question is with one explorer at a time, except that two explorers whose questions an organizer merges both keep the merged question; the explorer works on it with no predetermined theorem to produce."

The exception separates "takes one question" from "works on it", and after the exception "the explorer" has no clear referent. Rewriting:

> An explorer takes one question and works on it with no predetermined theorem to produce. A question is with one explorer at a time, with one exception: when an organizer merges two questions that each have an explorer, both explorers keep the merged question.

**A6. README L219.**

> "And where the Lean installation provides the tools, which Lean 4.35 and later do, a program called the **judge** checked the proof from outside the structural index: Lean's exporter writes the theorem out together with everything it rests on, and a second program works out the axioms again from that export alone and replays every declaration through Lean's kernel, the small program that checks every proof."

The sentence introduces a term, gives a condition, and describes two programs and three actions. "From outside the structural index" is also unclear. Rewriting:

> And where the Lean installation provides the tools, which Lean 4.35 and later do, a program called the **judge** checked the proof without using the structural index. Lean's exporter writes the theorem out together with everything it rests on. A second program then works out the axioms again from that export alone and replays every declaration through Lean's kernel, the small program that checks every proof.

I read "from outside the structural index" as "without using it". I cannot confirm that reading from the four files.

**A7. README L267.**

> "Outbound traffic comes from the programs they start: the CLIs, the literature server, which queries public literature services, any further tool servers you configure, and, when you run the command that writes a paper and its example papers are not yet on the machine, a script that downloads their sources from arXiv."

The items are separated by commas, and two of them contain commas of their own, so "which queries public literature services" reads at first as a third item. The running guide gives the same content in separate sentences (running L21) and reads well. Rewriting:

> Outbound traffic comes from the programs they start. These are the CLIs; the literature server, which queries public literature services; any further tool servers you configure; and a script that downloads the sources of the example papers from arXiv, which runs when you run the command that writes a paper and those papers are not yet on the machine.

**A8. README L307.**

> "The checks made on the implementation that the earlier design document describes are in [docs/do-math/rebuild-verification.md]"

"That the earlier design document describes" can attach to "the checks" or to "the implementation". Rewriting, on the reading that it attaches to the implementation:

> The earlier design document describes an earlier implementation, and the checks made on that implementation are in [docs/do-math/rebuild-verification.md]

If the other reading is meant, the rewriting is wrong.

**A9. README L323.**

> "They do not show that the real compiler gives those outputs, a difference that has already hidden two defects, each of which came to light only with the real compiler, and they do not evaluate the mathematics that real sessions produce."

Two trailing clauses separate the first "do not" from the second. Rewriting:

> They do not show that the real compiler gives those outputs. That difference has already hidden two defects, each of which came to light only with the real compiler. Nor do the tests evaluate the mathematics that real sessions produce.

**A10. running L9.**

> "`init`, the command that creates an instance, compiles the Lean form of the root, so Lean has to be installed, and named in the settings unless `lean` is on your PATH, before the instance exists."

The time phrase "before the instance exists" belongs to "installed" and comes after an inserted clause with its own condition. Rewriting:

> `init`, the command that creates an instance, compiles the Lean form of the root. Lean therefore has to be installed before the instance exists, and it has to be named in the settings unless `lean` is on your PATH.

**A11. running L13.**

> "Two commands that are used once the research has results have requirements of their own. `paper.py`, which writes the paper, needs a TeX program, `tectonic`, `latexmk` or `pdflatex`, to produce the PDF; without one it still writes the manuscript."

In the first sentence the verbs collide at "results have". In the second, "a TeX program, `tectonic`, `latexmk` or `pdflatex`" reads as a list of four things. Rewriting:

> Two commands have requirements of their own; both are used once the research has results. `paper.py`, which writes the paper, needs one of the TeX programs `tectonic`, `latexmk` and `pdflatex` to produce the PDF; without one it still writes the manuscript.

### Fault B: a word used as a term before it is introduced, or in two senses

The old text used a bold term in one sense only. In the new README several ordinary words are terms: statement, standing, settled, formal, accepted. At a few places the ordinary sense and the technical sense meet, or a term is used ahead of its definition where a small reordering would avoid it. It occurs at README L26, L56, L84 with L168, L172 and L241, L75 with L84 and L237, L86 with L101, L148, L221 and L312. README L5 and L6, treated in 2.4, are of the same kind.

**B1. README L26.**

> "what each standing of a result does and does not certify"

"Standing" is defined at L75. Here, in the paragraph that tells the reader what is coming, it is an unknown word. The old sentence was "what a published result does and does not certify" (README_before L26), and it still describes the section. Rewriting:

> what a published result does and does not certify

**B2. README L56.**

> "A record also gives the element's **weight**, which is one of two."
>
> "The frontier, a view described below, shows units; where a proof rests on a step whose own proof is accepted, the frontier shows in its place what the step rests on, so that a reader sees the structure of the proof and not every lemma."

The second sentence explains a behaviour of the frontier thirty lines before the frontier is defined (L88), and it uses "accepted" before L75 defines it. It belongs with the frontier. "Which is one of two" is also abrupt. Rewriting of the first sentence:

> A record also gives the element's **weight**, and there are two weights.

I would move the second sentence into L88, after the sentence that ends "and opens each unsettled statement in the same way", in this form:

> The frontier shows units. Where a proof rests on a step whose own proof is accepted, the frontier shows in the step's place what the step rests on, so that a reader sees the structure of the proof and not every lemma.

**B3. README L84, L168, L172 and L241. "Statement" names three things.**

> "An element is either a definition or a **statement**. In Lean a statement is a `theorem`" (L40)
>
> "a further proof is a further declaration with the same statement" (L84)
>
> "A question has a statement of what is to be understood and the grounds on which it was asked." (L168)
>
> "answers what its statement asks" (L172)
>
> "the questions, each with its statement, its grounds, whether it is open or resolved, and its source" (L241)

At L40 a statement is an element of the canon, which is a Lean theorem with its record. At L84 it is what such a declaration states. From L168 on it is the prose text of a question, which L168 itself says "no statement can hold". The old README also spoke of a question's "statements" (README_before L175), but "statement" was not a bold term there. Rewriting:

> a further proof is a further declaration that states the same thing (L84)
>
> A question consists of a text that says what is to be understood and of the grounds on which it was asked. (L168)
>
> answers what the question asks (L172)
>
> the questions, each with its text, its grounds, whether it is open or resolved, and its source (L241)

> "every statement of the canon has one of four **standings**" (L75)
>
> "A statement can have more than one proof: a further proof is a further declaration with the same statement, and each has a standing of its own." (L84)
>
> "that proof's standing becomes "proof not confirmed"" (L237)

**B5. README L86, L101 and L107. "Formal" and "settled" are defined for statements and then applied to proofs.**

> "a statement is formal when its Lean proof is complete and depends, through everything in the canon that it uses, only on the axioms the instance allows. The instance is complete when the root ... is settled through a proof that is formal." (L86)
>
> "a **formalizer** makes formal a proof that is settled and not yet formal" (L101)

"Makes formal a proof" is also an unnatural order of words. A mathematician expects "formal proof" to be the primary phrase, and the document already uses it that way at L24. Rewriting:

> Being **formal** is a matter of Lean. A proof is formal when it is complete in Lean and depends, through everything in the canon that it uses, only on the axioms the instance allows, and a statement is formal when it has such a proof. (L86)
>
> a **formalizer** gives a formal proof to a statement that is settled and not yet formal, usually by completing in Lean a proof that was accepted in words (L101)

The L86 rewriting defines the word for a proof first. I believe that this changes no fact, since L24, L86 and L107 already speak of a formal proof.

**B6. README L148. "Turn" and "sub-agents" are used before the reader has met them.**

No program has been given that name. The reader has met the judge, Lean's exporter and "a second program" (L219), and cannot tell which is meant. If it is the second program, the rewriting is:

> The second of the judge's programs, which makes the comparison, can run inside a sandbox as a guard against this

I cannot tell from the four files whether that identification is right.

The item before it mentions one run and this item mentions another, so "the first of these runs" has to be worked out. Rewriting, on the reading that the run of the first item is meant:

> The judge was run with the real Lean tools on the small library of the run described in the first item

### Finding on each document as a whole

**README.md**

*Writing quality.* The writing is good. Paragraphs have one subject and open on it. Terms are defined where they are introduced, with few exceptions. Reasons and limits are stated. The verification section (L309 to L323) says what was not shown as plainly as what was. The weaknesses are the three recurring faults, and they gather in the passages that define the new vocabulary: L73 to L97, L166 to L176 and L205 to L227. In those passages the reader must keep "accepted", "settled" and "formal" apart, and the sentences that carry those words are the ones that are overloaded or that use the words loosely.

*Avoidance of AI writing.* I found almost none of the habits by which generated prose is recognized. The prose has no dashes used as asides, no stock vocabulary, no rhetorical questions, no closing sentence that restates its paragraph, and no contrast made against a position that nobody holds. Where the text says "does not", it states a real limit. What remains is slight: the repeated frame "The reason is that" (2.1) and the three verbs without an object at L170 (D4). I would not change "Three readings are used because one reading, however careful, has one set of blind spots" at L156, although it is shaped like a maxim, because it gives a reason.

*Likeness to human writing.* The document reads as the work of one careful writer with settled habits: "rests on", "says what was meant", "by itself", "is with one explorer". The stiff phrases of fault C read as over-careful or translated English and do not read as machine prose. What makes the new README less like a person's account than the old one is what its content lacks. The old README told of a run that had happened, with cars and parking spots, dates, record numbers and what went wrong. The new one consists of general statements about a mechanism for some three hundred lines, relieved by one small example (L60 to L65 and L91 to L95) and by the verification list. That is a matter of what there is to tell, and no rewriting of sentences will supply it.

*Natural reading.* A reader can go through most sections once and understand them. The reading breaks at the sentences of fault A, which need a second pass, and at the terms of fault B, where the reader has to look ahead or work out which sense is meant. A mathematician reading for what can be trusted will find the summary table, the introduction and the table at L196 to L203 clear, and "Standings and the frontier" the hardest part.

**docs/do-math/running.md**

*Writing quality.* This is the better written of the two. It tells the reader what to do, in the order in which it is done, and gives the reason for each instruction that has one (L54, L94, L134, L196, L300). The split of "Starting by hand" into subsections suits the growth of the content. I would change eight passages (2.6, A10, A11, C8, C10, C12, C13, D5), and all are small.

*Avoidance of AI writing.* I found nothing to report.

*Likeness to human writing.* The guide reads as written from experience of running the software. Examples are the seven minutes that a file importing Mathlib took to load (L192), the Windows commit limit (L196), and the warning that a command selecting processes by name ends all of them (L300).

*Natural reading.* A person who intends to run the software can follow the guide from top to bottom. The paragraph at L208 is the one place where I lost the thread.
