# Reading 2: a researcher who builds AI systems

Reader stood in for: a researcher or engineer who builds AI systems and wants to evaluate the design of this one (how the sessions are organized, what is checked by whom, what has been verified about the implementation). The reader knows large language models and agent tooling and knows little about Lean or research mathematics.

Conditions of the reading: I read `C:\math\README.md` once, from line 1 to line 331, in order. I opened no other file, followed no link, and consulted no memory node, guide, source file or Git history. Line numbers are those of the file as I read it. Quotations are exact.

## 1. What I understood

This part was written first, from the one reading, and was not adjusted afterwards. The points are in the order of importance that the document itself gives them: the table at the top, then the prose before the first heading, then the body.

1. **What do-math is (lines 5, 13).** It is a system in which many AI sessions try to prove or refute one mathematical statement. Each session is a fresh Claude Code or Codex conversation that sees no other session's conversation. Sessions pass work to each other as files, and whatever the research decides to keep goes into one library written in Lean 4, which later sessions read. The user fixes the statement before the start, in words and as a Lean theorem with its proof left open, and may add "standing inputs" that reach all later sessions. An AI conversation called the operator sets the run up with the user. A program called the runner then launches sessions, several at a time.

2. **When a run is complete (lines 6, 23, 76, 224).** A run is complete only when the fixed Lean statement, or its negation, has a proof that is complete in Lean, uses only the allowed axioms, and has been accepted by a review. The body says this a little differently: the root must be "settled through a proof that is formal".

3. **What completion does not show (lines 7, 23, 206, 208).** Lean guarantees that the proof proves the statement as written. Nothing guarantees that the statement as written is what the user meant. The user confirms that once, before the run, with the operator's explanation, and no later step checks it. Every review is an AI session, and no person referees anything.

4. **Nothing is listed as solved (lines 8, 29).** The list of solved problems is empty.

5. **How one addition to the library is made (lines 5, 17, 84 to 95, 137 to 145).** Three sessions take part. A first session does mathematics and leaves all of its work in its own files. A second session, the peer, takes that work over, continues the mathematics where it can, and writes the candidate, which is the proposed change to the library. A third session, the review, has three sub-reviewers examine the candidate, compiles it, and judges each element on three things: whether the Lean statement says what was meant, whether the proof is correct, and whether a correct proof should be accepted. The body adds that the first session has one of three roles (attempt, explorer, formalizer), that a sixth role, the organizer, tidies the library after every few publications, and that the peer and review default to a different model from the producing roles.

6. **A review grades work and does not send it back (lines 19, 145, 175).** If the review finds anything worth keeping, the runner applies the candidate, compiles the library and commits. An element whose proof was not confirmed is published anyway, marked as such, with the review's reason, so that a later session can repair it. Statements never change after publication. Proofs and records can be replaced, and a later review can withdraw a proof's confirmation with a "finding". A published element is therefore not a certified one.

7. **What the library holds (lines 15, 39 to 61).** Each kept item is an "element": a Lean declaration (a definition or a statement) plus a Markdown record that holds a description, the statement in ordinary mathematical language, an explanation and possibly a proof in words. A proof has one of two forms. It is either complete in Lean, or it is written in words while the Lean source names the statements the argument rests on and leaves the proof open with `sorry`. In both forms Lean checks that the named statements exist and are used in a way that fits them, so a program can read the dependency graph from the compiled library. In the second form Lean does not check the argument; a review does.

8. **Nobody plans the work (lines 19, 119 to 125).** A producing session reads the library and decides for itself what to work on. Sessions avoid collisions through a board that shows who is working on what, through "sections" that they register on statements, and through a per-statement lease that serializes registration. The tools refuse only what a program can decide mechanically. Whether two sections overlap is left to the registering session, and the sessions are trusted to follow their guides.

9. **Why it is designed this way (line 21).** Three reasons are given. The library is the only memory across hundreds of conversations that remember nothing. Judgment comes from a session that did not produce the work and is published with the work, so imperfect work can be published and still be told apart from established work. And a result stated in Lean has one name that later proofs must cite and that Lean checks, which prose cannot provide.

10. **The vocabulary of trust (lines 65 to 80, 175 to 218).** Every statement has one of four standings: no proof, proof not confirmed, correct but held back, accepted. "Settled" means accepted with everything beneath it settled as well. "Formal" means that the Lean proof is complete and depends only on allowed axioms. Settled is a property that the AI reviews confer, and formal is a property that Lean confers. The frontier is a computed view of what the root currently rests on that is not yet settled. A correct proof can be held back because it can clearly be simplified or because it rests on a large computation that nobody understands; both are settings and are on by default.

11. **The checks behind a formal proof, and their limits (lines 198 to 208).** The review recompiles the candidate and reads its axioms. The runner recompiles the whole library at publication. With Lean 4.35 or later, a separate program called the judge recomputes the axioms from an export and replays the declarations through Lean's kernel. When the root is formally settled, the judge also checks that the proved root is identical to the root fixed at the first commit. The judge depends on the compilation having been honest, and its sandbox is off on Windows. Elements supplied by the user at the start, and the agreement statements that organizers add, have received no review of their meaning.

12. **Session lifecycle and infrastructure (lines 129 to 133, 171).** Attempts have no time cap. A "session host" process resumes a conversation when its CLI process exits and asks the session, up to three times, whether it really means to end. Reviews and organizers are resumed only when their output lacks the required structure. All Lean compilation goes through one broker per instance that enforces memory and thread budgets and caches results. By default eight sessions run at a time. Sessions inherit none of the user's personal instructions, hooks or memories.

13. **Questions, approaches and the two documents (lines 151 to 163).** Beside statements, the research keeps questions, each with a running "understanding so far", and for each unproved statement a list of the approaches tried and where each stopped. Most roles also receive an essay on what mathematical research is for and a document on the author's preferred writing.

14. **Reading results and running (lines 222 to 277).** Results are read from `knowledge/frontier.md`, `knowledge/index.md` and the `canon/` folders. A run needs Python 3.12, Git, Lean 4, and one or both CLIs. Sessions run unattended with permission prompts disabled and full file and command access, with no cap on time, sessions or usage. The quick start is to ask the assistant, inside this repository, to start research with the do-math skill.

15. **What has been verified (lines 283 to 299).** One toy research (the sum of the first n odd numbers is n squared) ran end to end with six real sessions on one Claude model and reached a formally proved root; it used no formalizer, organizer or comprehensive review. No real Codex session is recorded for the current implementation. A scripted research ran once against the real compiler. A research that imports Mathlib started on 22 November 2026 and only its first day is reported. Two rounds of review and repair were made, and the second still found substantial defects. There are 763 test functions in 22 modules, which use scripted sessions and a stand-in compiler; each module passed when last run, but the suite has not been run as a whole since the last change to the runtime. The design document was written before the implementation and has not been brought up to date.

16. **The rest (lines 301 to 331).** The repository also holds four unrelated tools with Korean READMEs and a set of supporting skills. do-math is in English. The license is AGPL v3.

What I carried away as this reader: the architecture and the division of trust are stated with unusual care, and the limits are stated candidly. Of the three things I came for, the third (what has been verified about the implementation) is the last thing the document tells me, at line 285, and nothing in the opening prepares me for how little has been run.

## 3. The writing

The owner asked the independent readers to "also check for writing quality, avoidance of ai writing, and likeness to human writing, and natural reading for human readers". I judge each by my own understanding of it, as the reader described above.

### 3.1 The document as a whole

**Writing quality.** The prose is accurate in its grammar, consistent in most of its core terms ("rests on", "says what was meant", "settled", "formal"), and unusually exact about agency: nearly every sentence says who does what. It makes no claim that it does not bound. Those are real strengths for a reader who evaluates a design. The weaknesses are of three kinds. First, the document says its main points three times: in the table (lines 3 to 11), in the prose before the first heading (lines 13 to 25), and in the body. The first sentence of line 13 repeats the first sentence of line 5 word for word, the first sentence of line 15 repeats the second sentence of line 5, and line 23 repeats lines 6 and 7. A reader who starts at the top reads the same sentences twice within one screen. Second, the body introduces about forty bolded terms and gives almost no examples of the things they name. There is no sample of a Lean declaration with its record, no sample of a proof in the second form, and no sample line of the frontier. For a reader with little Lean, one six-line example of a proof that "names the statements and ends in `sorry`" would do more than the four paragraphs that describe it. Third, many paragraphs are very long for a page read on GitHub. Lines 13, 17, 19, 21, 125, 147, 171, 246 and 248 are each a single paragraph of 150 to 250 words.

**Avoidance of AI writing.** The text is free of the most recognizable marks: there is no promotional vocabulary, no "not just X but Y", no reflexive triplets of adjectives, no bulleted fragments in place of reasoning, no closing summaries, and no emphasis by bold beyond defined terms. What remains are four habits that a reader used to model-written text will notice.
(a) Definition by denial. The document tells me repeatedly what does not happen before or in place of what does: "The sessions share no conversation." (13), "The user does not launch sessions by hand." (13), "No session plans the work of another." (19), "so the canon keeps no separate glossary" (41), "and no session writes it down" (57), "Nobody writes it." (78), "No session and no review admits a statement to the frontier." (80), "An attempt is handed no statement and no task." (119), "it is neither a vote nor a compromise between them" (139), "A review judges and does not gate." (145), "The canon holds no account of a session." (157), "A published element is not a certificate." (175). Several of these carry real design information (no central planner, no hand-written dependency list) and should stay. At this density they become a tic, and some answer questions that only someone who knew an earlier design would ask.
(b) Slogans. A few short sentences compress a paragraph into a motto and are then explained anyway: "Sessions coordinate through information rather than enforcement." (125), "A review judges and does not gate." (145), "One reading, however careful, has one set of blind spots" (139).
(c) Labelled enumeration. "Several considerations shaped this design. One is continuity. ... Another is independent judgment. ... A third is that ..." (21) announces a list, gives two items abstract-noun labels, and then breaks its own pattern on the third.
(d) A recurring verb. "Stand" carries too much: "statements that already stand there" (17), "A statement can stand without a proof" (15), "stands where the old proof stood" (147), "still stands as it stood" (147), "One invariant stands behind publication" (147), "Three checks stand behind a formal proof" (200), "stands where a review's judgment of the proof would stand" (208), "below them stands the frontier" (222), "can stand under the name" (169), "The statement stands alone" (179).

**Likeness to human writing.** The document reads less like a model's default output than like a carefully drafted specification, or like prose translated from another language by a careful hand. The sentence shapes are uniform: most are declarative, of middle length, and built as "A X is a Y that Z". There is almost no "you" before line 244, no example taken from an actual run before line 287, and no place where the author says what surprised them or what went wrong, except in the verification list, which is also where the text is most alive. A human author of a front page would, I think, show one concrete thing early (an element, a frontier, a published change) and let some definitions wait for the guide. A few phrases are unidiomatic English: "One small research was run" (287), "A research that imports Mathlib" (289), "an organization" as an output (131), "what later became of it" (19), "survives as its difference from the new text" (147).

**Natural reading for human readers.** Line by line the document is easy to follow; I rarely lost the grammar of a sentence. The difficulty is cumulative. By line 125 I was holding instance, root, canon, record, element, statement, unit, step, structural index, standing, settled, formal, frontier, index, six roles, pipeline, retained work, candidate, section, board, lease and role guide, and several ordinary words had acquired second meanings (part 2, items 14, 19, 21, 29, 41). The order also works against the reader I am. The mechanism comes first and at length, and the evidence (lines 285 to 299) comes last. The opening tells me candidly that nothing is listed as solved and that no person referees the work, and it does not tell me that one toy problem has been run end to end and that the test suite uses a stand-in compiler, which are the facts I would most want if I stopped after the opening.

### 3.2 Passages I find wanting, with rewritings

Each rewriting keeps the meaning as I understood it. Where I had to guess the meaning, I say so.

1. **Lines 5 and 13.** "A system for autonomous mathematical research in which many AI sessions work together to prove or refute a single mathematical statement." (5) and "do-math is a system for autonomous mathematical research in which many AI sessions work together to prove or refute a single mathematical statement." (13). The same sentence appears twice, eight lines apart, and "Everything the research keeps is ..." is likewise repeated at lines 5 and 15. Rewriting: let the table row be short and let the prose carry the detail. Row: "Many AI sessions work on one mathematical statement until it is proved or refuted in Lean 4, a proof assistant. See the first paragraphs below." Line 13 can then keep its present first sentence, and line 15 its own.

2. **Line 13.** "The user does not launch sessions by hand. An AI conversation called the operator sets up the run together with the user and operates it on the user's behalf. A program called the runner then launches sessions as the work requires, several at a time, for as long as the research goes on." The first sentence denies something and the next two then say it positively. Rewriting: "An AI conversation called the operator sets up the run together with the user and operates it on the user's behalf. From then on a program called the runner launches the sessions, several at a time, as the work requires and for as long as the research goes on; the user launches none by hand."

3. **Line 15.** "In both cases Lean checks that each named statement exists and fits the use made of it". "Fits the use made of it" is vague. Rewriting (my guess at the meaning): "In both cases Lean checks that each named statement exists and that the proof applies it to objects of the kind the statement is about". A two-line Lean example here would remove the need for the phrase.

4. **Line 17.** "and whether a correct proof should be accepted as the proof the research stops at." Rewriting: "and, when the proof is correct, whether the research should be content with it or should keep the statement open for a better proof."

5. **Line 19.** "A review judges work and does not hold it back for repair, so an element whose proof the review could not confirm enters the library marked as such, and a later session can start from it and from the review's reason." Rewriting: "The review does not send work back to be repaired. An element whose proof it could not confirm enters the library with that mark and with the review's reason, and a later session can start from both." This also frees "hold back" for the standing (part 2, item 10).

6. **Line 19.** "so that the element and what later became of it remain visible together." Rewriting: "so that the element stays in the library with the whole history of the judgments made of it."

7. **Line 21, whole paragraph.** "Several considerations shaped this design. One is continuity. ... Another is independent judgment. ... A third is that a result should be one thing that later work can name. A result kept in prose restates its notation wherever it is written down, and the same fact written in two accounts is two assertions with nothing that stands for the fact itself." The announcing sentence and the labels add nothing, and the third item is the hardest sentence in the opening. Rewriting: "Three needs shaped this design. A run is meant to last for days and to pass through hundreds of conversations, none of which remembers the one before, so the library has to carry everything that later sessions will need. A candidate is judged by a session that took no part in producing it, and the judgment is published with the work; useful work that is not yet perfect can therefore be published, and a later session can still tell an established result from one that needs work. And a result has to be something that later work can cite. When a result is kept in prose, each report that uses it writes it out again in its own notation, and nothing ties the copies together. A result stated in Lean has one name, a proof that uses it must cite that name, and Lean checks the citation." ("Is meant to" reflects part 2, item 13, and should be changed back if runs of that length have been observed.)

8. **Line 25.** "The rest of this document lists the solved problems, and then explains how the sessions cooperate, what each state of a result does and does not certify, how to read the results of a run, and the shortest path to running do-math on a statement of your own." The sentence recites the headings that follow, and GitHub shows an outline of them. Rewriting: delete the first sentence and keep the second ("The [running guide] holds the detail of the settings, the commands and the procedures."), or shorten to "The sections below go into each of these in turn; the [running guide] holds the settings, commands and procedures."

9. **Line 41.** "A definition is referred to by its Lean name, and Lean rejects a second declaration of a name, so the canon keeps no separate glossary." Rewriting: "A definition is referred to by its Lean name, and Lean rejects a second declaration of a name, so the Lean names themselves serve as the research's glossary."

10. **Line 55.** "The views show units and pass through a step whose proof is accepted, so that a reader sees the structure of the proof and not every lemma". Rewriting (my guess at the meaning): "The views list units. A step whose proof is accepted is left out, and the statements it rests on are shown in its place, so that a reader sees the structure of the proof and not every lemma".

## 4. What I could not determine

**What I would still want to know, as this reader.**

1. Which models, by name and version, the defaults use for each role, and which model the three sub-reviewers run on. Line 265 says only "Codex first and Claude second".
2. How independent the three sub-reviewers are in practice: whether they receive the same prompt and the same material, and what besides separate contexts makes their readings differ.
3. Whether the reviews have been evaluated at all: how often a review has confirmed a wrong proof or rejected a right one, and whether any review has yet caught a real error. The document says that reviews are AI sessions and gives no evidence about their reliability.
4. Whether coordination by trust (sections, board, role guides) has been observed to work when many sessions run at once. The only completed run had six sessions.
5. What a run costs. The document says there is no cap and that no usage figures are collected (248), and that the quantities that decide cost at scale have not been measured (289). I do not know the token or session count even of the toy run.
6. What is in a session's starting context and how large it is, and how a session with no time cap deals with the limit of its context window.
7. How a run ends when the statement is neither proved nor refuted. Line 277 mentions "how to end a run" as a topic of the guide.
8. What the system does about material that sessions read from the web, and about Lean code written by one session that runs when another session or the runner compiles the library (202).
9. What the "governing principles" of the earlier design record are (283).
10. What the "paper" and the "dependency map" are (248, 277, 281), and what "organized into publishable form" (29) requires.
11. How a refutation is handled: who writes the negation, and how Lean confirms that it is the negation (76, 224).
12. Whether the design document linked from the first table (10) can be relied on for evaluating the design, given line 283, and how large the departures are.
13. Whether the root judge ran in the toy run (part 2, item 51), and which Lean version that run used.
14. Whether the runs "for days" and through "hundreds of conversations" (21) have happened.

**What I had to guess.**

1. That the "written account" of line 5 is the record of line 15.
2. What a proof in the second form looks like in Lean, and what "fits the use made of it" means (15, 57).
3. That the review, and nothing else, checks that a proof in words relies only on the statements its Lean source names.
4. That "pass through a step" (55) means the view omits the step and shows what is beneath it.
5. That a statement can carry at most one Lean proof and one proof in words, which would explain "one of its proofs" (74).
6. That a CLI process exits when the model ends its turn, which is why a session spans several processes (129).
7. That additional allowed axioms come from the imported library or from tactics, since the canon itself may declare none (59, 198).
8. That "survives as its difference from the new text" (147) means a stored diff.
9. That "compiled honestly" (202) refers to session-written Lean code interfering with compilation, and that "the comparing program" is the second program of line 200.
10. That "the guide that every role receives" (159) is a shared guide distinct from the six role guides.
11. That the toy problem of line 287 is absent from "Solved problems" because it was not "organized into publishable form", whatever that requires.
12. That "the user who runs the research" (188) means the person who runs an instance and can change its settings.
