do-math is a system for autonomous mathematical research in which many AI sessions work together to prove or refute a single mathematical statement. The sessions share nothing but a knowledge base: each is a new Claude Code or Codex conversation that knows nothing of the others, and what one session proves, writes up or assesses is published there as a record for later sessions to read. The user supplies the statement to be proved or refuted, written out exactly, and may add standing inputs, which are instructions that reach every session launched after them. The user does not launch sessions by hand. An AI conversation called the operator prepares the statement and the settings on the user's behalf, and a program called the runner then launches sessions as the work requires, several at a time, for as long as the research goes on.

Every record is produced in three stages, each by a different session. First, a session works on the problem: it tries to prove a statement or understand some feature of the problem, and it keeps all of its work, from definitions and computations to approaches that failed, in its own working files. Next, a second session, the peer, takes over from those files. It writes the account that other sessions will read, completes arguments where it can, looks for what the first session missed, and decides what the record should propose to the research, such as new statements to pursue. Finally, a third session, the review, checks the peer's draft against the first session's files and assesses whether each claim in it is supported. The record is then published with that assessment attached, and it is never edited afterwards. If a later session finds a mistake, it appends a finding to the record, so that the original claim and what later became of it remain visible together. No session hands out work to another: a session in the first stage reads the knowledge base and decides for itself what to work on.

Two concerns shaped this design. The first is continuity. A run lasts for days and passes through hundreds of conversations, none of which remembers the one before, so the records are the only memory the research has, and they must carry everything that later sessions will need. The second is independent judgment. The session that has just produced an argument is poorly placed to notice what it has missed, so each piece of work is assessed by a session that took no part in producing it. Useful work that is not yet perfect is still published, together with its assessment, so that a later session can tell an established result from a claim that still needs more.

The first full run of this implementation began on 5 November 2026. Its statement was Conjecture 6.5 of a 2025 paper by Celano, Elder, Hadaway, Harris, Martin, Priestley and Udell ([arXiv:2507.07243](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07243)): for every length n, the numbers of parking functions of length n with maximum displacement 0, 1, …, n−1 form a unimodal sequence. On 11 November the conjecture reached the state that do-math calls *informally proved*. This is a technical term of the system, and it means less than the words might suggest: along two routes leading from statements already proved to the conjecture itself, every record and every reviewed implication had been accepted by an AI review session. No person has refereed the records. Several steps depend on computer-assisted inputs. And there is no Lean proof of the conjecture yet: do-math considers a run complete only when a Lean proof of the statement, or of its negation, has been recompiled by a review session and matched against the exact statement, and the user paused formalization on 9 November so that the research could finish first. The knowledge base, with every record and every assessment, is public on Zenodo.
