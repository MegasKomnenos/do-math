# do-math as it stands on 25 November 2026: context for the verification run

## 1. Why this document exists and how to read it

On 25 November 2026 the user asked for a multi-round verification run over the skill kept at `.claude/skills/do-math/`. In such a run several agents (repairers) each read a part of the skill in full and propose concrete corrections, other agents (judges) judge each proposal against the actual files, the accepted corrections are applied, and the round repeats. One session, here called the driving session, sends the agents out and applies what they accept. The agents who do this work start with nothing: they have not seen the conversations in which the skill was designed, they do not know what the user has decided, and they do not know what else is running on the machine. This document was written to give them that knowledge. It is the result of an investigation, made before the first round, of the commit history, of the memory notes that accompany each commit, of the transcripts of the sessions in which the user and several AI sessions worked on the skill, of the earlier verification runs of the same skill, and of the research instances that the skill is running on this machine.

The reason the run was asked for is stated in the user's request, which section 3 quotes in full. In short: the skill was rebuilt on 22 November, and in the three days that followed it was changed thirty-two more times by several sessions working at once in one working tree. Mechanisms were added and taken out again, many changes were committed without the tests being run, and nobody has read the result as a whole. The user expects that this left defects behind and wants them found and repaired.

Three things in this document carry the most weight for someone who is about to judge whether something is a defect and whether a proposed correction is allowed, and they are treated at the greatest length. The first is what the user asked of this run and has decided about the skill, in the user's own words, and what those decisions permit and forbid (sections 3 and 4). The second is what happened to the skill during the week: what was added, what was reversed, what was removed, how the work was done, and what remains in the tree as a result (section 5). The third is that a research instance is running from this very tree while the run edits it, and that another session is preparing a large change to some of its files (section 6). The remaining sections are reference material: a map of the skill, the state of its tests, the matters that earlier work recorded as open, what earlier verification runs of this skill learned, and the repository's criteria for judging tests.

### What kind of statement each sentence is

The document mixes statements of different kinds, and a reader who is about to rely on one of them needs to know which kind it is.

A **user decision** is something the user said, or something put to the user that the user answered or approved. Where the user's own words exist they are given, in the language the user used, with an English gloss where the words are Korean, and with their source: the session and the time of the message, or the memory note that records it. Times of messages are UTC. Times of commits and of events on the machine are KST, nine hours ahead, and are marked so. An answer of one word to a particular question or item is a decision on that item. An approval of a long proposal as a whole is a decision too: the agreement of twenty-five decisions for the fourth generation and the forty criteria of 7 November were worded by an assistant and approved by the user, and they bind. Where an approved text and the user's own words differ on one matter the user's words govern, and a later decision governs an earlier one. A user decision binds the run.

An **assistant's choice** is something an AI session decided that the user was not asked about, or was told of and did not answer. Much of the detail of the skill is of this kind: the particular way in which a decision of the user was carried out in code or in wording. Such a choice does not bind the run. It may be revisited where there is a reason, within everything the user did decide. In section 4 a statement is a user decision when it quotes the user, gives the time of the user's message or the memory note that records it, cites one of the forty criteria by its letter and number, or names the agreement as its source; where the text says that a session chose or added something, that is an assistant's choice. Where an approved passage of a guide is concerned, what binds is what the passage says and not its wording: it can be reworded, moved or tidied like any other passage so long as nothing it says is lost. Sentences of sections 3 and 4 that draw a consequence for corrections from the user's words ("so", "therefore", "a correction should not") are this document's reading of those words. The consequences that bind are the ones the freeze document states as invariants. Section 4.10 lists the choices most easily mistaken for decisions, because past sessions more than once took their own earlier wording for the user's intent.

An **observed fact** is something an investigator checked in a file, in the Git history or on disk on 25 November 2026, when the skill stood at commit `9729443f`. Line numbers refer to that commit and will drift as the rounds apply changes.

A **recorded claim** is something written down earlier that was not checked against the present tree for this document: what a session wrote in a commit message, a memory note or a conversation (that a test passed, that a defect exists, that something was not verified), or what an investigator inferred without checking. It is evidence of what its author believed.

Section 9 collects what earlier work, and the investigation itself, noted as defects and doubts left open. Most of its entries are recorded claims; some are observations made on 25 November, and the entry says so. They are included because the user asked that the investigation remove blind spots, and a repairer who knows where earlier sessions stumbled can look there with care. They are not conclusions of this run. Even an observation of 25 November is only a place to look, because the tree changes as rounds are applied and because nobody has judged whether what was observed is a defect. A repairer who wishes to rely on an entry must first establish the fact in the actual file, and a judge must check it in the same way, as if it had come from nowhere.

## 2. What do-math is

This section is a summary for orientation, drawn from `SKILL.md` and the guides as they stood on 25 November. It is not a standard against which a guide is judged.

do-math is a skill by which an AI agent, at a user's request, sets up and runs autonomous mathematical research on one proposition. The agent who talks with the user is called the **operator**. It fixes the proposition with the user, prepares a research **instance** (a directory with its own Git repository), and starts a detached program, the **runner**, which carries the research forward after the conversation ends. The proposition the instance exists to settle is its **root**.

The knowledge of an instance is its **canon**: one Lean 4 library, with a written **record** beside each declaration. A declaration together with its record is an **element**. A definition is an element; so is a statement, whether or not it yet has a proof. A proof is either complete in Lean, or it is written in words in the record while its Lean proof names the elements it rests on and ends in `sorry`. Review judges each proof twice, whether it is correct and whether a correct proof is accepted, and a statement accordingly has one of four **standings**: no proof; proof not confirmed; correct but held back; accepted. A statement is **settled** when one of its proofs is accepted and every statement that proof names is settled. An element is **formal** when its proof is complete in Lean and depends, through everything it uses, only on the allowed axioms (`propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound`; never `sorryAx`). The **frontier** is the view computed from the root downward along what each proof names, showing every statement that is not settled. The instance is complete when the root, or its negation, is settled by a formal proof.

The runner launches fresh AI conversations called **sessions**, each in one of six roles. An **attempt** reads the frontier and chooses work that remains. An **explore** session holds a **question**, something the research wants to understand, and develops understanding of it; question `q0` is the root problem as a whole. A **formalizer** writes the Lean proof that a settled statement's written proof still lacks. A **peer** takes over the work of one of those three, continues it, and writes the **candidate**, the proposed change to the canon. A **review** session has three independent sub-reviewers examine the candidate, compiles it itself, and judges each element. An **organizer** joins what is the same by proving agreement in Lean, rearranges modules and merges questions. To avoid two sessions doing the same work, a session takes the **lease** of a statement and registers the **section** of it that it will work on, which is the whole statement or an exact part of what it asserts; an explorer takes a question in the same way. A registered section holds until the work on it has been published. Review judges and does not gate: useful imperfect work is published with review's judgments.

Each session runs under a **session host**, a process that stays alive across the session's turns and resumes the same conversation when a turn ends before the work is done. All Lean compilation in an instance goes through that instance's **Lean broker**, a service that schedules compiles within memory and thread budgets and keeps a cache of compiled modules. Two further commands work from a finished or running canon: `depmap.py` draws the **dependency map**, and `paper.py` (the **paper command**) has sessions write a mathematical paper from the canon and typesets it.

Some further terms recur in this document. A session is one conversation of an AI command-line client, either Claude Code (`claude`) or the Codex CLI (`codex`); the guides and notes call that client the CLI and a session's CLI process a worker. What a session is given to read at its start is its **pack**. The check that the runner makes of a finished session's output, of its form and its references and not of its mathematics, is the **transport check**. The **index** is the table the program extracts from the compiled library after each publication: for every declaration its kind, its statement, what it uses and the axioms it depends on. Besides its record, a statement carries its **approaches tried**, one entry for each line of attack pursued on it with where that line stopped, and a question carries its **understanding so far**. A record gives its element a **weight**, unit or step, by which the dependency map and the paper decide what to show as a result in its own right. A **concern** is a challenge that a session reports against an element's proof; a **finding** is a judgment of review that withdraws a proof's confirmation; a **comprehensive review** is an assignment of the review role to examine questioned proofs and their uses. A statement whose negation is settled is **refuted**, and a statement and its negation that are both settled are **contested**. A **standing input** is a text that the operator submits with the `input` command and that every session launched afterwards receives. It is how the user's conditions reach a running instance, and the notes also call it a standing condition. The roles that produce work for a peer to take over (attempt, explore, formalizer) are the producing roles; when a candidate cannot be published, the runner assigns a new peer to the same work, a replacement peer. The **literature server** is scholarly-mcp, a server through which sessions search for and fetch mathematical literature; settings and status output call it `scholarly`.

Two research instances are named throughout. The **parking** instance (주차함수: that a certain count attached to parking functions is unimodal) belonged to the third generation, which also required a formal Lean proof of the root, kept in Lean files beside its prose records. It ran from 19 to 23 November, proved its root formally, and was the instance through which the user gave most of the conditions that the guides now carry. Its proof was afterwards put into the form of a fourth-generation canon with `init`, and it is those packaged instances that the paper and map commands have been run on. The **sunflower** instance (해바라기: the Erdős–Rado sunflower conjecture) is the first instance of the fourth generation to import Mathlib. It has run since 22 November and is running now (section 6).

The skill consists of an entry point for the operator (`SKILL.md`), reference documents (`reference/`), one guide per role (`roles/`), short texts shown to a session when it is resumed (`resume/`), the program (`runtime/`) with its tests (`runtime/tests/`), and a collection of real mathematical papers used as models by the paper command (`exemplars/`). Section 7 maps these in detail.

The skill has been rebuilt several times, and the notes speak of **generations**. In the third generation, which ran until 21 November, knowledge was kept as prose records, "nodes" for exact statements, "relations" between them and a glossary. The **fourth generation**, committed on 22 November as `ceb38646`, replaced all of that with the Lean library described above. The fourth generation is what this run covers. The directory `.agents/skills/do-math/` is a copy of the skill, generated by a script from `.claude/skills/do-math/` for another AI runtime; the notes call it the **mirror** and call the original the **canonical tree**.

### What "doc bloat" means here

On 21 November, after a session had added several passages to the guides, the user asked for a tidying and said what kind (session `1f8bb240`, 16:00:21Z):

> "전반적으로 내용에서 지금의 스타일과 전달하는 내용 및 디테일들은 유지하면서도 중복이라던지 과하게 장황한 것 등은 정리해보고 싶어. 그러니까, 내용을 압축하는 것이 아니라 내용을 유지하면서 그에 더해 불필요한 군더더기를 덜어내는"

(Keep the present style, the content conveyed and the details, and tidy away duplication and excessive verbosity; that is, do not compress the content, but keep the content and remove the unnecessary padding on top of it.) The session then removed three kinds of text: an explanation restated in one document when its main statement stood in another document that the same reader receives, repetition within one document, and connecting sentences that carried no content (memory note `M-0734`, correction 5). It applied this only to the passages it had itself written; the rest of the guides were never tidied in this way.

On 22 November, starting a revision of the guides, the user set the aim in one sentence (session `20ae7c1d`, 05:36:20Z): "Let's focus on improving the guidance in both effectiveness, efficiency, while retaining delicate and accurate details".

On 24 November an audit of the whole skill for over-engineering (below, the over-engineering audit) reported to the user that the guides each session receives are large (a starting pack of 430 to 730 KB once the state of the research is included) and that "Twelve rules are stated in two to five documents each". The user answered that item with one word, "intended" (session `20538f79`, 13:46:18Z). The memory note of that day records the item among the things the user kept: the composition of the guides that sessions receive (`M-0772`).

These statements pull in two directions, and the run has to hold both. On 21 November the user wanted duplication and verbosity tidied away, with the content kept. On 24 November, told that the same rules stand in several documents and that the packs are large, the user called the guides as each session receives them intended. On 25 November the user asked this run to reduce bloat in the documents.

The reading this run adopts is the following, and the freeze document makes it the rule of the run. It is the driving session's reading of the three statements together, and not a further statement of the user's. The guides are meant to be rich and detailed, and they are not to be cut down for their size. Which guides each role receives is the intended design. So is the statement of one rule in more than one guide: that is exactly what the audit put to the user, and the user called it intended. A rule stated twice is therefore not bloat, and is left. What counts as bloat is text that gives its reader nothing: an explanation, an account of a case or an argument that the same reader has just been given in the same pack and that is given again with nothing added; repetition within one document; sentences that only connect; and, after this week, text about something that no longer exists. Removing bloat never means leaving out a condition, an exception, a step of an argument, a criterion of judgment or a case, and a correction that removes or merges text has to show that the reader of the removed text still receives what it gave. `runtime/context.py` decides what each reader receives (section 7.2). The audit's notes (section 12) say which rules it counted.

Repetition across guides is judged by what is repeated. Words that a session follows or judges by in that place (what to do, what not to do, what to look at in judging) are left wherever they stand, however often they are stated. An explanation of where something is or how it works, an account of how something came about, or an argument for a rule, carried with the same content in two guides of one pack, is bloat where the later place adds nothing for that role; one place is kept and the other is removed or shortened to a pointer. The facts that bear on it are who reads each of the two places, whether they are read together in one pack or at different moments (a resume text is read hours after the guides), and whether the second place adds anything. Where it is not clear which kind a passage is, the passage is left as it is. One case needs no weighing: two statements of one matter that no longer say the same thing are a defect, whatever view is taken of repetition.

### What the user asks of tests

The user's requirements for the tests of this skill were given for an earlier verification run and recorded in English in its freeze document (`임시/do-math_검증_20261105/동결.md:56`):

> "(d) tests of the actual intended runtime behavior rather than a divergent imitation, assertions capable of failing when behavior deviates, and simple, cost-effective tests without unnecessary machinery; (e) complete coverage within each test module and across the suite, without needless overlap, redundancy or combined complexity."

The three test-related goals of the present request are these same requirements seen from the side of their failure: tests that overlap or carry machinery they do not need ("test bloat"), behaviour that no test would notice breaking ("test coverage"), and tests that cost more time than what they check requires ("test efficiency"). Section 7 gives the measured cost of the suite, about twenty-nine minutes for one sequential run, and where the time goes; the user asked on 22 November "why do the tests take so long" (session `3750b5d4`, 19:20:27Z) and nothing was changed in response. Section 11 gives the repository's standing criteria for judging a test.

These goals can pull against one another, and one way of trading them is closed: merging two tests into one so that an assertion which only one of them made is lost. The judges of an earlier run rejected such a proposal, and the freeze document of this run makes it a rule. Reducing a test's cost has to preserve the starting state and the real path it exercises, and not only its assertions.

### What the user asks about running tests and other checks

During this week the user stated a principle about checks, after watching sessions repeat them (session `20538f79`, 24 November 12:13:09Z):

> "a lot of the procedures are inefficient and repetitive. for example, subagent running the tests before reporting, then the receiving agent running the same ... tests all over again, running expensive builds only to run builds again like seconds later, running entire-scope verification while changing pieces of it which incur running entire-scope verification again afterwards, running whole tests after changing some doc that PHYSICALLY CANNOT CHANGE TEST RESULTS, a lot of the procedures done ad-hoc are more like a facade of work"

### 4.1 What the fourth generation is for

The user's brief for the rebuild (session `d778f997`, 21 November 15:14:03Z; the same text in session `e13e3c64`) asked for five things: that the problem and every proposition and relation around it be expressed in Lean and laid out, not only a few key statements; that the record of mathematical content, one per Lean element and native to Lean's structure, be separated from the record of process; that sessions be able to traverse the canon mechanically and deterministically, and be told to, so that repeated work drops; that a script calling AI sessions produce a paper and its PDF from the canon; and that the use of Lean be fused into the whole of the work, "다만 자원 소비가 효율적인 방식으로" (but in a way that consumes resources efficiently). The brief ends with a principle that governs how old and new are to be weighed:

> "만약 지금 기존의 절차와 이 새로운 제안이 서로 충돌한다 느낀다면 애시당초 기존의 절차를 개선하는 것이 목적이기 때문이야. 그러니 어떻게 하면 기존의 절차를 이 새로운 제안에도 불구하고 유지할 수 있을까, 는 애시당초 방향이 틀린 고민인거지."

(If the existing procedure and this proposal seem to conflict, that is because improving the existing procedure is the purpose; asking how to keep the old procedure in spite of the proposal is the wrong question from the start.)

Two requirements from the design conversation bear directly on bloat inside an instance. The user wanted records not to keep accumulating, "런타임이 길어져도 bloat이 최소화되도록" (so that bloat stays minimal even as the run grows long; 16:30:52Z), and wanted what sessions learn to accumulate by the object it concerns and not one record per session (16:34:29Z). And on unformalized results the user set a direction (17:37:23Z): the rule is not "discard what is not expressed in Lean" but "express it in Lean and keep it", and expressing things in Lean is to be a central step of the work from its start, "자연어 처리의 위에 더해지는 chore가 아니라" (not a chore added on top of work done in natural language).

The agreement, and the design record after it, list what the fourth generation left as it was, and the user approved that list with the rest: the six roles and the order in which the runner launches them; the way questions are proposed and admitted; concerns, findings and the comprehensive review; the two acceptance settings (section 4.5); that a session's lease and section hold until its work is published; the reconsideration of a session's ending, by which a session that ends is resumed to judge again whether it should; that the original material of a session is kept, and the limit on who reads it; English inside an instance; and the governing principles of the earlier design record `docs/do-math/rebuild-design.md`, which remains in force for whatever the new record does not change.

### 4.2 The sessions judge; the machine does not

The oldest and most often repeated of the user's requirements is that judgments of mathematical meaning belong to the sessions and that the program records and carries out what they decide (criteria B1 to B4). The program refuses only what a machine can decide: that Lean confirms a statement is the same as another or its negation, that something compiles, which axioms a proof depends on, names and hashes, the form of files, who holds a lease. A correction that makes the program judge mathematical content, the faithfulness of a statement, the sufficiency of an argument or the meaning of an overlap between sections goes against this.

There is no planning role and no assignment of work: exactly six roles, and attempts choose their own work (criterion A2).

The limits placed on sessions rest on their cooperation, and this is deliberate. When a comparison of the skill against outside standards reported that sessions can write to every file the checks rely on, the user answered (session `20ae7c1d`, 22 November 05:36:20Z): "restrictions are intentionally reliant on session's cooperation for effectiveness, efficiency, and simplicity." The absence of an enforcing mechanism is therefore not a defect, and a correction should not add enforcing wording or machinery.

The same holds for the operator. During a verification run on 10 November the user gave this instruction, which later freeze documents have carried forward (original in `임시/do-math_검증_20261110-2/동결.md:76-80`):

> "there is no need to consider a case of intentionally destructive and hostile operator or issues that would arise only in such a circumstance. on the contrary, if such considerations lead to rule-ification of what should be sessions' semantic judgement, restrict valuable degree of freedom, or overengineer the system into unnecessary complexity, or create rules that does not improve the effectiveness of research and/or decrease its effectiveness, such considerations are to be actively addressed"

So a problem that arises only when the operator deliberately breaks the system is not a defect, and code, text or tests that exist only to guard against such an operator, and that cost freedom or simplicity, are themselves defects.

On orchestration the user said (session `3171182f`, 23 November 00:34:54Z) that an orchestrating agent either knows the details of every area it delegates, in which case it need not delegate, or does not, in which case it gives directives that constrain the worker who meets the actual detail; "So, it is better to just set governance rules and tools that help agents orchestrate themselves, in my opinion." A correction that adds central direction of the sessions' work runs against this.

When sessions evaluated do-math this week the user corrected them more than once for judging by proxies and for treating deliberate positions as failures: evaluate "without mistaking any of its key qualities as failures or evaluating trade-offs as mistakes" (session `5172428b`, 22 November 14:18:33Z), and "Don't rely on proxies as sole basis of your evaluation; actually read what is there, what is implemented, and judge based on what actually is" (15:35:11Z). This document takes the same standard to apply to what a repairer calls a defect.

### 4.3 How the guides are written

**Knowledge for judgment, not rules.** The guides that worker sessions read (`roles/`, `resume/`, and the documents of `reference/` in their packs) are meant to give a session the understanding from which it judges, not conditions and commands (criteria C1 to C3, F1 to F5, G2). The operator's documents (`SKILL.md`, `reference/operations.md`) describe procedures and commands and are not under this limit. A correction does not add if-then rules, lists of things to refuse, closed lists of considerations, mandatory fields for justification, or gates. A choice left to the session is written as reasons, costs and open examples. This limit does not apply to the contracts that a machine or another role consumes: the form of an output file, the arguments of a command.

On 21 November the user decided that the way of working and review's criteria for accepting a proof should nonetheless be written down explicitly, and in what manner (session `1f8bb240`, 14:37:22Z):

### 4.7 How a defect is to be repaired

The user has said a good deal this week about what a good repair is, mostly in response to repairs that were not.

**Find the cause and repair that.** Dispatching an agent on an incident, the user asked for "the real, root cause and full solution, not something shallow and surface, something that is simple, effective, and robust, and accurate" (session `0860eb60`, 24 November 11:07:50Z).

**Do not build a safeguard for a state the system should not reach; prevent the state.** The agent's repair for that incident included a path that recovered when a folder the runner needed had been deleted. The user refused it (11:49:12Z):

> "so the issue is that it may be removed, right? and it is a case that should not happen in the first place. I think building a failsafe for that exception is the wrong path to take. the better path is to just avoid it, plain and simple, I think"

The only thing that could delete the folder was the system's own clean-up rule, so the rule was changed and the recovery path, its test and its sentence in the guide were taken out. The note of that session states the lesson as a question to ask of any failure state: who creates this state, and if the system does, repair the rule first.

**Simplicity.** "Simplicity is best." and "If a solution for any problem can be implemented before any runs, at this time now, then just be repeated reliably without much thoughts, that is the best." (session `20538f79`, 24 November 14:32:28Z). That is: a solution that can be put in place once, ahead of time, and then simply relied on is better than one that needs judgment each time it is used. The formal judge was removed on the same ground.

**Delete what is not used.** "delete what is not used" (session `20538f79`, 24 November 11:53:14Z); "delete leftovers." (the same session, 10:40:29Z). The earlier verification runs of this skill, on the user's instruction, treated unused elements and copies of one rule in the program and its tests as damage to remove, and held that "the behaviour is the same" is not by itself a reason to leave them; for repetition in the guides section 3 applies. A file that the program writes into an instance and that nothing reads is a different matter, because removing it changes what a live instance holds: the audit raised three such files, and the user has not answered (section 4.11).

**Repair the instruction, not the agent's freedom.** When agents duplicated work because their briefs told them to, and a session then began building a guard that forbade it, the user said (session `20538f79`, 23 November 01:43:46Z): "you told duplicate workers to do duplicate work, then when they began doing it you tried to add a new ban that would forbid it, when you could simply have told them to just do what they should do in the first place?"

**A failure ends the runner; it is not isolated, and nothing restarts the runner.** The program does not pass through an undefined state. A refusal becomes a bounded wait; a failure of the runner's own operation ends the runner with the error recorded, and the operator starts it again after the repair. ## 6. What is running on this machine

### 6.1 The sunflower instance runs from the files this run edits

The instance at `.skill-workspace/sunflower-20261122` is live. On the morning of 25 November it had six sessions running, had published 105 revisions, and its root was unresolved. It is a fourth-generation instance, and it does not run from a copy of the skill: its sessions' commands point at `C:\math\.claude\skills\do-math\runtime\`, and its resume texts are read from `C:\math\.claude\skills\do-math\resume`. The run edits those same files.

How a change reaches the instance depends on what kind of file it is, and `reference/operations.md` states the rules:

- A **guide** (`reference/`, `roles/`, `SKILL.md`) is copied into a session's folder when the session is launched. A session already running keeps the text it was given; every session launched after a change receives the changed text.
- A **resume text** (`resume/`) is read afresh each time a session is resumed. A change reaches sessions that are already running.
- A **command a session calls** (`coordination.py`, `canon_tool.py`, `check.py`, the client side of `lean_broker.py`, and everything they import, such as `canon.py` and `knowledge.py`) starts as a new process each time. A change takes effect at the next call.
- The **runner**, each **session host** and the **broker** keep the program they loaded when they started. A change to their code does not reach them until they are restarted.

So after a round is applied, new processes run the new program while the runner and the broker run the old one, and both read and write the same files of the instance. At present the runner was started just after the code of commit 35 was installed and very probably runs that code. The broker has not been restarted since 24 November 17:54 KST, before commits 31, 33 and 35 were made; it does carry the change of commit 31, which was put in place before it was committed.

The user asked for this run knowing that the instance runs from these files, and the run goes ahead on them; what the lesson quoted at the end of section 5.5 warns against is contained as follows. A change inside the program that alters neither what is written into the instance's files nor what one process passes to another is an ordinary correction. It takes effect for new processes, and the worst it can do is what any mistaken correction does. So is a correction that makes the program do what the guides already promise: sessions were told to expect that. Three kinds of change, however, touch what the running instance depends on. The freeze document sends the first to the user as a proposal and rules out the other two.

- A change to something that a process already running, or a session already launched, relies on: the form of a file of the instance or of the runner's or the broker's state, a settings key or its default, an environment variable given to sessions, and those arguments, output keys and exit codes of a command that another process of the program reads or that the guides promise to a session.
- A resume frame without exactly one of each of its placeholders. `resume/common.md` must contain `{{role_guidance}}`, `{{previous_error}}` and `{{request_id}}` once each, and `resume/output.md` the first two; otherwise the session host stops at its next resumption.
- A link in a guide that leaves the set of files a session is given. When a pack is assembled, a relative link in a guide embedded in it that resolves outside the session's `context/` folder raises an error, and the session cannot be launched. A session's `context/` holds `reference/` with a copy of every file of the skill's `reference/`, `roles/` with the session's own role guide alone, and `SKILL.md`; nothing else.

The driving session checks the last two, and that every Python file still parses, after each round is applied, with a script that only reads files.

### 7.2 Who reads which guide, and when

The **operator** receives `SKILL.md`, and reaches everything else by following its links; `SKILL.md` links only to `reference/operations.md`.

A **worker session** receives, at its launch, one file called its pack (`runtime/context.py`). The pack begins with the root statement, the root as fixed in Lean, and the session's assignment. Then come the guides for its role, embedded whole and in a fixed order. Then the state of the research at launch: the frontier, the understanding so far of the whole problem (question `q0`), the board of who holds what, the standing inputs, and the commands the session can run. The embedded guides are, for every role, `reference/common.md`, the role's own guide, `reference/writing.md`, `reference/canon.md`, `reference/lean.md`, `reference/tools.md`, and then:

| Role | Further embedded guides, in order |
|---|---|
| attempt | `what-research-is-for.md`, `user-writing-preferences.md`, `literature.md`, `sections.md`, `retained-work.md` |
| explore | `what-research-is-for.md`, `user-writing-preferences.md`, `literature.md`, `retained-work.md` |
| formalizer | `retained-work.md` |
| peer | `what-research-is-for.md`, `user-writing-preferences.md`, `literature.md`, `sections.md`, `retained-work.md`, `records.md`, `change-format.md` |
| review | `what-research-is-for.md`, `user-writing-preferences.md`, `literature.md`, `records.md`, `change-format.md`, `review-output.md` |
| organizer | `what-research-is-for.md`, `user-writing-preferences.md`, `records.md`, `change-format.md`, `organizer-output.md` |

Besides the pack, every file of `reference/`, the session's own role guide and `SKILL.md` are copied into the session's `context/` folder, where the session can open them by following a link. `reference/evidence.md`, `reference/lean-broker.md`, `reference/operations.md` and `reference/paper-method.md` are in no pack and reach a worker only in that way. This composition is the user's intended design (section 4.6). The guides embedded in a pack came to roughly 210 to 340 KB per role on 22 November, before the state of the research is added.

When a session's turn ends and the session host **resumes** it, the session is shown a text made from two files: a frame, with the role's own short text (`resume/<role>.md`) inserted into it. The frame is `resume/common.md` for attempt, explore, formalizer and peer, and `resume/output.md` for review and organizer (`runtime/ending.py`). `resume/output.md` is read by the program and named in no document.

The three **sub-reviewers** that a review session delegates to receive nothing from the program. What they know is what the review session writes into its brief to them; `roles/review.md` lists what the brief must carry and `reference/tools.md` what every delegation must carry.

The sessions of the **paper command** receive `reference/paper-method.md`, whole or one named section of it, and `reference/user-writing-preferences.md`.

`exemplars/README.md` is read by no program and named by no document.

The program starts five kinds of process of its own: the runner (`runner.py run`, detached), one session host per session (`runner.py _session`), an archiver (`runner.py _archive`), the broker (`lean_broker.py serve`), and one job host per compile (`lean_broker.py _job`). A session's commands are separate short processes whose command lines are written into the session's `input.json`.

The operator's commands are subcommands of `runner.py`: `init`, `start`, `status`, `stop`, `resume`, `input`, `render`, `review`, `organize`, `reconsider-attempts`, `questions`, `restart-broker`, `kill-session`. An investigator compared every command and flag that the operator's documents name with what the program defines and found none that the program lacks; a few flags that the program defines are named in no document (`lean_broker.py run` and `build` accept `--timeout`; `fetch.py` accepts `--only` and `--to`, described only in `exemplars/README.md`).

An instance on disk has two parts. What is kept in its Git repository: `root.md`, `instance.json`, `settings.json`, `canon/` (Lean sources, records, approaches, questions, history, the index) and `knowledge/` (published events and the rendered views `frontier.md`, `index.md`, `board.md`). What is outside Git: `runner/` (the runner's state, locks, leases, and under `runner/lean/` everything of the broker), `sessions/` (one folder per session) and `control/` (the stop file, standing inputs, operator requests). The complete table of files with the module that writes each and the modules that read it is in the investigator's report (section 12).

Two of the module-to-module dependencies form cycles that work only because one side imports the other inside a function: `canon` and `lean_broker` (through `coordination` and `knowledge`), and `runtime_support` and `ending`.

The program supports Windows and Linux and not other systems; it uses Windows Job Objects, process creation times and sharing-violation retries on the one side and `/proc` and `pidfd_open` on the other.

About half of the tests stand on one of two fixtures defined in `tests/support.py`, and those tests account for nearly all the time. An **instance** test (`InstanceCase`) receives a copy of a small compiled instance: once per test process the fixture writes a two-module canon and calls the real `init`, which starts the instance's broker and builds the library with a stand-in compiler; then each test copies that instance, a Git repository, into a temporary folder, together with a copy of the skill's guides (about 780 KB). A **scenario** test (`ScenarioCase`) goes further and starts from a state reached through the real publication path, built once per process and copied; scenarios build on one another, so the first test that asks for the furthest state pays for three publications. Each publication starts a broker as a detached process and runs the stand-in compiler as subprocesses. Because each module is run in its own process, each of the thirteen modules that use these fixtures builds them again (all modules except the four on the broker fixture, `test_waiting`, `test_cached_reads`, `test_cli_adapter`, `test_containment` and `test_retention`). A **broker** test (`BrokerFixture`, defined in `test_lean_broker.py` and imported by three other modules) runs a broker in the test process on a bare instance folder, with a second stand-in compiler.

The stand-ins are these. `tests/canon_lean.py` takes the place of `lean` for the instance and scenario tests: it parses a subset of Lean, writes the output files, and answers the index extraction with rows computed from the text. `tests/fake_lean.py` takes its place for the broker tests, with a controlled use of memory and time; every compile it runs sleeps 0.3 seconds, and many broker tests script longer sleeps. `tests/fake_lake.py` stands for `lake env`. `tests/pipeline_cli.py` stands for the AI CLI in every role for the single test of `test_pipeline`, which runs the whole runner. `test_ending` carries its own scripted CLI.

Only the six tests of `test_formal.LeanItselfTests` run the real Lean. They are skipped unless Lean 4.36.0 is found under `.skill-workspace/do-math-toolchains` or named in the environment variable `DO_MATH_TEST_LEAN`; on this machine it is found, so they run. Other tests are skipped off Windows, without Git Bash, without Playwright, or where hard links cannot be made. The stand-in `canon_lean.py` is known to differ from Lean in ways its own comments and the verification record state: it produces no generated axioms, writes four output files where Lean 4.36 writes five, takes a definition's whole text as its statement, and counts any textual occurrence of a known name as a use. A defect of the index extraction was once hidden by exactly this difference. Whether the suite should rest on a hand-written imitation of Lean at all is among the questions the user has not answered (section 4.11): a correction can make a stand-in more faithful or its description accurate, and cannot replace it by the real compiler or remove it.

### 9.1 The runner and publication

- **A provider's refusal is not recognised as a refusal.** On 24 November two peers of the sunflower instance finished their work, prepared a candidate that passed the check, and were then resumed for the reconsideration of their ending. In that turn the provider refused (the CLI's stream carried `model_refusal_no_fallback` and a message with `stop_reason: "refusal"`). The program recorded an ordinary failure with no refusal, and the runner retried each peer although a prepared candidate lay in its output. An investigator read `runtime/retention.py` (`refusal`, lines 189-211) and reports that it recognises usage limits, a message about usage credits and one message of the Codex CLI, and not this case. Whether the replacement peer reused the candidate was not checked.
- **`status` and an invalid settings file.** Two sessions reported independently, on 22 and 23 November, that `status` raises an error while `settings.json` is invalid, although `reference/operations.md` says a rejected edit appears as `settings_error` in what `status` returns (`runtime/runner.py`, near line 1463 and later 1547).
- **The allowed axioms at replay.** A session reported on 23 November that the research state, which the program recomputes from the published events (it calls this the replay), is computed from `lean.allowed_axioms` read straight from `settings.json` (`runtime/knowledge.py`), while the rejection of `sorryAx` lives in the validation of settings; on a synthetic instance, after `sorryAx` was added to the file, loading the settings refused the edit but the replayed state called the root formally proved. The same line was said to make the replay raise when the file is not valid JSON. This predates commit 35, which changed how formality is computed.
- **A failure of the CLI that carries no type** (an expired login, for instance) was reported, at second hand, to set no block on the model for peer, review and organizer, so that retries never move to the second model (`runtime/runner.py`, near lines 493-498).
- **The runner ends itself on an error.** Any error of four common kinds raised in the runner's loop is saved as `last_error` and ends the runner; nothing restarts it. This is the user's decision (section 4.7) and is listed here only because sessions keep reporting it.
- **The second step of a replacement has no owner.** When a new statement supersedes a settled one, the old statement keeps its proof until the new one is settled, after which a one-line derivation may replace it (section 4.6). The runner dispatches no session to do that; a peer that happens to pass may.
- **`init` and a failure of Git.** `reference/operations.md` says a refusal after the directory exists leaves no `instance.json`. An investigator infers from the order of the code that a Git failure during `init` would come after `instance.json` is written. Not run.
- **Things operators did by hand because the skill offers no way.** They ended and restarted a live broker directly, because `restart-broker` requires that no session's turn be running. They froze an instance's compiles by setting `lean.min_free_memory_mb` to a value no machine has, because `stop` halts the launching of sessions and neither publication nor compiling. They corrected the text of a published record through a patch that was never committed (section 5.5). They reused another instance's compile cache for `init` with a script of their own. Each of these is a gap in what the skill offers, not necessarily a defect; the freeze document does not count the addition of a capability as a repair.

## 10. What earlier verification runs of this skill learned

The skill has been through seven verification runs of this kind since 5 November. Their freeze documents, ledgers and final reports are under `임시/` in the folders named `do-math_검증_*`, `do-math-question_검증_20261109` and `do-math-lean-canon_검증_20261122`. The freeze document of the present run turns into rules those of their lessons that it means to keep; the rest of this section is history, given so that the same mistakes are recognised. The records of the present run itself, that is its ledger and the proposals and verdicts of its earlier rounds, are closed to repairers, mergers and judges by the run's rules. The earlier runs' folders and the investigation reports of section 12 can be read.

**The kinds of defect that kept returning.** In every run the largest class was disagreement between what a guide says and what the program does, and in the fourth generation it was found in the same place by several repairers at once: the conditions under which a formalizer is dispatched were described wrongly in four guides. The same rule written in two places that had begun to differ was found in every run, in guides (a table of folders duplicated between two references) and in tests (the same refusal asserted in two modules, copies of a helper). Code that nothing used and branches kept for an earlier format were found in every run. In the broker and the session hosts, defects of ordering between processes were uncovered one layer per round. Use of a term before its definition, one word in two senses, and unclear referents were found in prose in every round. And more than once a stand-in used by the tests hid a real defect that surfaced only in a real run.

**Why proposals were rejected.** In the run of 22 November the judges rejected three proposals, all for the same reason: the defect was real, and the replacement text introduced a new one. One changed how a set was computed and dropped a case the original had handled; one moved information out of a place where a later session still needed it; one, rewording a sentence, used "section" in two senses in it. Two were raised again in the next round with a narrower text and accepted; the third was repaired after the run. The lesson the reports draw: when changing a rule that counts or collects, list the cases the present text handles; when rewording, check the new sentence against the shared vocabulary. Other rejections: a proposal that merged tests and lost an assertion; a proposal resting on a false premise about what a guide lacked; proposals that narrowed something the user had decided, which went to the user instead; and a new mechanism to protect against an operator's mistake, which was closed as needless complexity.

**What repairers got wrong about their own task.** Repairers escalated to the user matters that the files in scope and the sources of the run already decided; that a sentence stands in a document does not make it a user decision, and that several repairs are possible is not a reason to ask. Repairers left a defect unreported because they took it to belong to another repairer's area; areas divide the duty to search, not the right to report. And repairers who found one defect did not look for the same pattern elsewhere; an earlier freeze asked that whoever finds a defect search its own area and the whole tree for the same wrong assumption, order of checks or turn of phrase, and report each place.

**What judging without running cannot see.** Judges do not execute anything. In one run three judges unanimously accepted a repair whose premise about a helper was false, and only a run afterwards showed it. Proposals that are each valid can conflict once applied together. This is why the driving session runs the tests at intervals and reads the result, and why a proposal that changes behaviour should carry, in the same proposal, the change to the test that asserts that behaviour.

## 11. The repository's criteria for judging a test

The repository keeps a distillation of what its earlier work learned about tests (memory notes `L-0001` and `L-0023`). It was written by AI sessions from past experience and is not a set of user decisions, but the earlier freezes used it and it is the measure this run applies together with the user's requirements of section 3. The points that bear most often on this suite:

- **A passing test testifies only to what it actually exercised.** A stand-in, a fixture or a patched name lets through every defect outside what it models. The broker passed all its tests with stand-ins and then failed on the real compiler at three boundaries the stand-ins did not model: state left by an earlier version, the file semantics of the platform, and how an executable is looked up.
- **A stand-in belongs only at the boundary with something outside the program or with time.** The program's own receiving, storing and judging run as real code. A stand-in does not answer the unknown with success, does not infer what the real producer would compute, and does not fill in what is missing.
- **A test of a refusal fixes the reason for the refusal**, not only that something was refused. Its input starts from what the real producer makes and changes the one thing the test is about, so that the failure can be attributed.
- **Passing does not show that a check is strong.** A check that goes over all members is tried by removing a member and seeing that it fails and names the member. Expected values are computed independently of the code under test.
- **A test tied to a past state is a defect.** A branch that tolerates a known old failure counts the failure's return as a pass; an assertion on a line number fails when correct code moves.
- **Right after tests are reworked, compare the number collected**, and confirm that the test guarding a particular repair is still collected. A test once ended up after the block that runs the module and never ran.
- **Cost.** A time budget means something only where overruns are reported. When cutting a test's cost, keep its starting state and the real path it goes down, not only its assertions; a method without an assertion of its own may still check through a helper or through the absence of an exception. A test that prepares and restores real stored state is not cheap whatever it is called.
- **A test that inherits the program's defaults inherits what is right for operation.** On a loaded machine, tests that inherited the default below-normal priority starved; the fixture was given normal priority and the default was kept.
- **A test that failed once under load** is examined for a race in the program before it is called unreliable; one such failure was a real race, reproduced by constructing the state.
- **A test made from an incident asserts the property the contract must keep**, not the incident's particulars; the user asked for exactly this when tests were written for an incident fix.
- **Counts.** Results of runs of different versions are not added together into "all pass". Skipped and not-run are kept apart from passed.
