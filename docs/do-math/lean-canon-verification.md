# do-math Lean canon: verification

do-math runs unattended mathematical research with sessions of six roles. In the redesign recorded in [the design record](lean-canon-design.md), the knowledge of a research instance is a Lean library from its first session on: every assertion the research keeps is a Lean declaration with a written record beside it, and sessions find their work, publish their results and read earlier results through that library. The redesign is implemented in the canonical skill, `.claude/skills/do-math/`.

This document records what was run to check that implementation, what each run showed, and what no run has shown. It is written for two decisions: how far the implementation can be relied on as it stands, and what has to be checked or decided before the first real research is started with it. That research has since been started, and the section "The broker and the runner under a research that imports Mathlib" records its first day.

## Runs with the real compiler

Lean 4.32.0 was used, without Mathlib.

A scripted research, the one `test_pipeline` runs, was run once against the real compiler in place of the stand-in. It finished in 579 seconds with the root formally proved. Creating the instance took 41.7 seconds. The run included the step that the research by real sessions did not reach: the root first receives a proof written in words, and its Lean declaration names the lemma that proof rests on and ends in `sorry`. The proof of the same declaration is then replaced in place by a complete Lean proof, and an import is added to the root's module. The five compiles that extract the index had a median of 57 seconds, and each was charged about 2.1 GB against the memory that Windows had promised to processes, its commit charge, on the machine while another research instance was running on it.

The measurements that the design rests on are in section 12 of the design record. One of them corrects an expectation of the design and matters for cost: replacing a proof leaves the compiled public output of its module unchanged only when the new proof makes the elaborator generate the same auxiliary declarations as the old one. Otherwise the modules that import it are compiled again.

## A research run by real sessions

One research was run from start to finish by real AI sessions and the real compiler. The proposition was that the sum of the first n odd natural numbers is n squared. The operator's part followed the guide: the root in words, a definition and the root statement in Lean in two modules, a record for each declaration, and a check of the definition on six known values as a theorem with a complete proof. The instance is kept at `.skill-workspace/do-math-e2e-20261122/instance`, a local folder outside version control.

The runner launched six sessions, all on the same model (`claude/opus`, effort high): an attempt, an exploration, a peer for each, and a review for each candidate. The following happened.

- The attempt found a complete Lean proof of the root by induction and left it as the text of the root's declaration. Its peer made the candidate: the proof in place of `sorry`, the root's record with the written proof and an explanation, an approach entry, and one new element stating that the recursive definition equals the literal sum for every n.
- The review compiled the candidate itself, read the axioms (`propext` and `Quot.sound`), confirmed and accepted the proof. The runner built the candidate on the canon, installed it and committed. The root was formally proved 863 seconds after the attempt started, and the instance counted as complete from then on.
- The exploration was assigned the root question. Its peer published, 2,377 seconds after the attempt started, nineteen further elements with complete Lean proofs and the first understanding text of the root question. Among them are a second proof of the root's statement as a separate declaration, and two proofs that review confirmed as correct and did not yet accept, giving its reason. Such a proof is published and can be used, and its statement stays marked as work that remains. This is the second of review's two judgments at work.
- The runner then ended. Once the root is formally proved it launches no further attempts or explorations, and the sessions that were running had been carried through to publication. The two statements whose proofs are held back stay marked as work that remains.

The canon at the end holds 23 elements: three definitions, eighteen statements whose proofs are accepted and two whose proofs are correct and held back. Twenty statements are formal.

**What this run did not exercise.** The first proof of the root was already a complete Lean proof, so no formalizer was launched. Two publications were too few for an organizer. No candidate failed to build, so no replacement peer was needed. No candidate offered a new proof to be put in the place of a proof the canon already held, so review never had to decline such a replacement, and no concern or contested statement led to a comprehensive review. No statement was refuted. These paths are covered by the tests and, for the formalizer's replacement in place, by the scripted run with the real compiler. No real session has yet followed the guides along them.

**Two later changes to the paper command.** Both were made on the user's instruction after the run that produced the PDF.

## Independent reading and the review rounds

## Three decisions made after the rounds

Each of the three decisions changes something that the design record states, and the design record is kept as it was written. Where the two differ, the guides and the code follow the decisions below.

**A research that ends with the root refuted.** The dependency map drew what the proof of the root rests on. A refuted root has no settled proof, so its map showed the root alone. The user's decision was that such a research has a result, the refutation, and that the map shows it. The root's card now says that the root is refuted. A settled statement that refutes the root is drawn even where its record marks it as a step and not as a unit, a line of a kind of its own joins it to the root, and the map includes what that statement rests on. A statement that would refute the root and is not settled refutes nothing yet, and the map leaves it out. When every unit of the canon is asked for, such a statement is drawn if its record marks it as a unit. This adds one kind of line to those that section 13.1 of the design record describes.

**Snapshots of Lean's state after import.** Section 12 of the design record adopts two things: the broker saves Lean's state after the import of a file's header and starts later compiles with the same header from it, and a compile that used such a snapshot and failed is run again without it. As implemented, the reuse served only compiles whose imports are all external, which leaves out every compile that imports a module of the canon. The rule also needed an exception. Sessions submit many files that Lean rejects, and running each of them a second time would have cost more than the snapshot saved, so a compile that was rejected after loading a snapshot which an earlier compile had shown to be sound was not run again. Under that exception a snapshot damaged later could have made the broker report a correct file as rejected. The user judged that the saving did not justify these rules, and the reuse was removed: the broker's code for it, the setting `lean.header_snapshots`, its tests and the passages of two guides. Every compile now loads its imports in full. For a file that imports the Lean metaprogramming library this is the 9.7 seconds measured without a snapshot, where a compile started from one took 2.8.

**A statement that supersedes a settled statement.** By rule 3 of section 10.3 of the design record, a better statement enters under a new name, and the proof of the old statement becomes one line that derives it from the new one. For an old statement that was already settled nothing carried this out: a session replaces the proof only of a statement it has registered as its work, and a settled statement can no longer be registered. The user's decision starts from an observation. A new statement that is not yet settled is not a better statement than a settled one, because deriving the settled statement from it would make an established result rest on one that is not established. The rule is therefore that the proof of a settled statement is replaced only by a proof under which the statement stays settled. A candidate that adds the new statement leaves the old proof as it is. Once the new statement is settled, the one-line derivation can take the place of the old proof, and no registration is asked for it. Conditions are set on the derivation where the old proof is the only one through which the statement is settled, or the only one through which it is formal; a proof is formal when it is complete in Lean and rests, through everything of the canon it uses, only on the allowed axioms. The check of the peer's candidate then accepts the derivation when it is complete in Lean and every statement it names is settled, and, where the statement would otherwise stop being formal, when the statements it names have formal proofs as well. Review has to confirm and accept the derivation, since under any other verdict the old proof stays. Where another proof keeps the statement settled, and formal if it was formal, none of these conditions is set, and review judges the new proof as it judges any other replacement. No session is launched to take this second step. A peer whose work comes to the old statement can take it, and until one does the old proof remains.

The first and the third decision came with tests: one of the map of a refuted root, and five of the rule, of which four are of the checks on a peer's and a review's output and one carries the three publications out in order. The removal took the 14 tests of the snapshots with it.

After the three decisions the guides were revised once more. One of the two changes something that the design record states, and the design record is again kept as it was written.

**What each role receives.** The guides a session receives at launch are joined into one document, its pack. ## Formal standing checked from outside the index

*Note of 25 November 2026.* The judge that this section describes was removed from do-math on 25 November 2026, together with the command `judge.py`, the setting `lean.judge` and the test module `test_judge`. A statement is now formal when the library compiles, its Lean proof is complete, and the list of axioms that Lean itself reports for it, the list that `#print axioms` prints, lies within the allowed axioms; the runner stores that list in the structural index, and no second program examines it. For a formally settled root, the runner now compares the root's statement and every definition under it between the index of the instance's first commit and the present index, without compiling, and `status` gives the result under `root_check`. The operating guide describes both under "What makes a statement formal". This section, and the later passages of this document that speak of the judge, of `judge.py` or of `root_judge`, record the earlier state.

Three things followed the revision of the guides, and all concern what the word formal rests on. A statement is formal when its Lean proof is complete and the axioms it depends on are among those the instance allows. do-math reads both from its structural index, which is computed inside a Lean process that has loaded the modules it describes.

**An axiom the index did not count.** The index counted an axiom of the canon only when the axiom was an element, that is, a public declaration with a place in the source. An axiom that was private, or that Lean itself had generated, was neither refused nor counted. Lean's `native_decide` generates exactly such an axiom: since Lean 4.29 it adds one for each computation to the module that uses it, under a private name. A theorem proved by `native_decide` was therefore recorded as proved from the allowed axioms, while the guide said that this tactic brings an axiom that is not allowed. The defect was confirmed on the rows that Lean 4.36.0 printed for such a theorem: the earlier index code gave the theorem no axioms, and the corrected code gives it the generated one. A second defect stood in the same place. A helper declaration that is no element lost its `sorry`, and what it uses, when a module extracted later named it; the index now keeps what such a helper stands for. A private axiom that an author wrote is refused as a public one is, which `init` showed on a canon that declared one. The stand-in compiler of the tests prints no generated axioms, and that is how the defect remained while every test passed.

**Lean 4.36.0.** The latest stable release was installed beside 4.32.0, together with the Mathlib release for it, and its archive matched the checksum its release page gives. `init` created an instance from the small canon of the research described above with it, and no change to the runtime was needed for that. Two differences from 4.32.0 were met. A module compile writes a fifth file, `.ir.sig`, which the broker keeps with the others; the stand-in still writes four. And `native_decide` over a definition of the canon needs a `meta import` of the definition's module in a `module` file. The measurements that the guides quote were taken on 4.32.0 and were not repeated. Mathlib 4.36.0 was checked with one file that imports two of its modules and proves an identity with `ring`. The import of all of Mathlib, with which the 4.32.0 installation had been checked, was not made, because a research instance was running and the machine had about 6 GB of memory left to commit.

**The judge.** From Lean 4.35 on, an installation carries an exporter, which writes a theorem out together with everything it rests on, and `lake comparator`, which reads such an export alone, works out the axioms again, compares it with a second export, and replays every declaration through the kernel. The runner now has every formal proof judged in this way before the change that brings it is published, and `init` does the same for the canon it starts from. A theorem is judged when a change adds or replaces its proof and it is formal afterwards, and also when it becomes formal because the last statement it rests on did; the export follows a theorem through everything it rests on, whichever publication brought each part. A rejection sends the change to a replacement peer, as a failed build does. A judge that cannot run says nothing about the change: the build is made once more, and then the runner stops with the reason. A new command, `judge.py`, judges the root of a finished instance against the root as the instance's first commit fixed it, so that the statement proved is shown to be the statement posed, with every definition it depends on unchanged. A rejection there is written to the instance as a request for a comprehensive review.

## The broker and the runner under a research that imports Mathlib

The first real research with this implementation was started on 22 November 2026, and it is also the first research that imports Mathlib. Its root is the Erdős–Rado sunflower conjecture, it uses Lean 4.36.0 with Mathlib 4.36.0, and between six and twelve sessions ran at a time on a machine with four processor cores. A second research ran on the same machine throughout, from the copy `.agents/skills/do-math`, which is a generation older and uses Lean 4.32.0. Within hours the compiles of both were slow. The larger causes lay in the machine: the compiles that were admitted needed more physical memory than it had, and the sessions gave four cores more work than they carry. Part of the cause lay in the runtime. Its own processes took processor time that the compiles needed, and about a third of the time that compiles ran, counted from the start of each compile to its end, was spent on compiles that only measure memory. Four changes were made to the runtime during that first day, each after a measurement on the running instance and each measured again afterwards. This section reports the four changes, then what was run to check them, and then what no run has shown.

The shares of a core given below were read while the processor was at or near full load, so they are rough.

**Waiting for a compile.** A command that waits for a job of the broker read the broker's whole state file four times a second. `wait` does this, and so do `run`, `build`, the prepare and verify commands and the judge while they wait. On this instance the state file had grown to 2.5 MB, one reading of it cost about 30 ms of processor time, and each waiting command used about 5% of one core. A waiting command now looks only whether the job's result file exists, which costs about 0.1 ms, and it pauses between looks for 0.25 s at first and for up to 1 s later. It reads the job's full record at the start, when the result file appears, every ten seconds, and once more when its timeout is reached. What it prints, its exit codes and its timeout are unchanged. After the change a waiting command on the instance used about 0.2% of one core.

**Saving the broker's state.** A sampling profiler was attached to both brokers, and in each of them 52% of the samples lay in the writing of the state file. The broker writes the whole file again at least once a second while a compile runs, and again for every build that is submitted. The file was 2.65 MB on the new instance and 4.18 MB on the older one, and it was encoded with indentation and written out in small pieces. The broker now encodes its state without indentation in one call and writes it in one piece; the file is still replaced as a whole and flushed to disk, and a broker reads a state file that an earlier broker left indented. On the state of the running instance one save took 337 ms before the change and 35 ms after it, and the broker went from about 30% of one core to between 1% and 2%. The same change was made by hand in the older copy, where the broker went from between 19% and 30% of one core to about 7%.

**Reading the files of sessions, and listing processes.** On every pass the runner read each session's declaration, end check and process receipt. It now reads such a file again only when the file's modification time, size or identity on disk has changed. The runner of the instance went from about 5% of one core to 2.7%. The broker, in order to add up the memory of a running compile, listed the processes of the machine four times a second. It now lists them once a second. The memory of the processes it has already found is still read four times a second. A process that a compile starts is now found up to a second after it starts, where before it was found within a quarter of a second. No compile was running when the processor share of the changed broker was read, so the effect of this change is not known.

**Baselines without a compile.** Before the broker starts a compile it has to know how much memory the compile's imports take, which it calls the baseline of that set of imports. It used to measure every new set by compiling a file that holds only the `import` lines, and the compiles that needed the set waited for that. On this instance 114 sets had been measured, in 378 minutes of compiling.

A baseline cannot be had by adding up figures for the single imports. Imports share nearly everything they load, so the sum counts the shared part once for each import: tried against the 109 sets that had been measured at that time, it was too high every time, and by 98% in the median. What a baseline does follow is the size on disk of what the set loads, namely the compiled files of every module the imports reach, each module counted once. The broker reads from the library which modules a module imports, in the `.ilean` file that stands beside each compiled module, and adds the sizes of each module's public and private compiled file. A straight line fitted through the measured sets then gives a baseline from that size.

The broker estimates only where measurements surround the new set. It uses the sets that were measured in the same Lean environment and with Lean launched in the same way. Two measurements belong to the same environment when the Lean version is the same and the instance's Lean settings `command`, `cwd`, `env` and `cache_epoch` have the same values; the broker keeps a hash of those four as its environment key. Lean is launched either directly or through `lake env`. At least five such sets must have been measured, and the size of the new set must lie between two of their sizes. A set smaller or larger than every measured one is measured as before, and so is a set of which some module cannot be found in the library. The line is fitted through measured sets only, so an estimated baseline never becomes one of the points of the fit. An estimate is also kept within the range of the measured baselines.

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| The instance's 114 measured sets, given to the new code in the order in which they were first measured, with the real Mathlib 4.36.0 library | 10 sets would have been measured and 104 estimated. The estimates differed from the measurements by 0.6% in the median and by 1.9% at most. Of the 104 estimates, 68 were below the measurement, by 34 MB at most, and 36 were above it, by 53 MB at most |
| The older instance's 47 measured sets, in the same way, with its Lean 4.32.0 library | 8 would have been measured and 39 estimated. The estimates differed by 0.9% in the median and by 2.4% at most |

- **The wider extraction on real builds.** After the change, 14 builds extracted the importing modules as well, two of them publications by the restarted runner (revisions 6 and 7). None reported a changed statement, and the root's hash in the index is the one fixed at creation. What to expect from now on is that a candidate which enlarges the imports of a module that others import is refused at `prepare` where it went unnoticed before; in this canon `Sunflower.Defs` has 63 importing modules, and no module imports the root's.
- **The judging of the root.** The broker keeps a job whose session the runner has not recorded, so the builds that `judge.py` submits as `operator` are not cancelled as orphaned. The toolchain folder holds the exporter and the further kernels (`lean4lean`, `nanoda`, `leanchecker`). The seven judge jobs of publications in this instance all ended with code 0, with a median of 47 seconds and 3.0 GB at the peak and at most 213 seconds and 3.8 GB.

**What was not checked.** `verify` now has the importing modules compiled as well, since the extraction imports them, and a test that fixes the modules of such a build was not looked for. The automatic judging of the root has not been run: how long the two exports and the replay with every kernel take on a library that imports Mathlib is not known. Whether the time of an extraction grew with the wider scope could not be told, because the builds after the change ran while the tests were loading the machine.

## What has not been measured

One research that imports Mathlib has been running with this implementation since 22 November 2026, and the section before this one reports what was changed and measured on its first day. The quantities that decide the cost of a research at that scale have still not been measured: how long the import takes in each compile, how often a replaced proof changes the public output of its module and sets off compiles downstream, how long a publication waits for its build while other compiles hold the memory, and how many publications an hour the single publication queue carries. Section 16 of the design record lists what to observe in the first real run. On Windows the page file bounds how many compiles run at once, since a Lean process is charged for the whole size of the library files it maps; the operator's guide gives the measurement and the recommendation, and the change is the user's to make.

## Installation and scope

The redesign changed only the canonical skill. The generated copy `.agents/skills/do-math` was not regenerated, by the user's decision, because a running research instance uses it. Three of the changes that the section "The broker and the runner under a research that imports Mathlib" describes were made in that copy by hand, in its `runtime_support.py`, its `lean_broker.py` and its Lean guide, because the research that uses it ran on the same machine and its broker had the same costs. The copy was not regenerated for them either. The two now differ by a whole generation. The new runtime refuses an instance that was not created by it, so regenerating the copy while that research is still being run would leave its runner unable to start again. The copy is to be regenerated when that research has been ended.
