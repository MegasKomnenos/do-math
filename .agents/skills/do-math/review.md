# Working context of the review session

The review's job is, as a session independent of the author and the peer, to evaluate the published claims and evidence and certify the scope in which they can be used mathematically. A peer is a mathematical response that worked the output directly, and the review judges the claims of both the work and the peer. The peer's criticism and new arguments are also subject to verification and carry no authority. Apply the purpose, authority, reading of knowledge, Lean criteria and submission procedure of [common.md](common.md). You may read the whole published knowledge, but do not go to the target author's session folder, intermediate files or CLI logs to fill in evidence. For the parts that cannot be judged from the published material alone, leave that lack and its effect in the verdict.

## Fixing the target and reading the published records

A research review starts from the original record of `task.target`. Until you have first written your own judgment of the target, do not read the summaries and verdicts of the prior reviews shown in the index, whether the target's entry sits in `knowledge/index.md` or in a module file under `knowledge/index/`. Even when using the index to find other evidence records, keep that distinct from the target's prior reviews.

Read the target's local glossary before judging any result, since the names of its exposition are defined there and the terminology verdict is made against it and against the global snapshot the target cites. Follow the target's references into their local glossaries to confirm that the definitions the target relies on are the ones its evidence actually uses, and open the index modules that hold those evidence records. A goal review is the one review that reads every module, because it must judge the whole published knowledge against the goal, and a record filed in a module counts for or against achievement exactly as it would in the general module. A review publishes no local glossary of its own.

Read `task.instructions`, `task.references` and `task.target` of `input.json`. If `target.kind` is `research`, review the whole output of the one research session that `record` points to. If it is `bundle`, review together the original research record or exploration of `work` and the peer output of `peer` that targeted that work. If it is `goal`, review whether the instance's goal is achieved. The instructions of an additional review tell the issue to examine in particular. In both a standalone research review and a work–peer bundle review, you must not confirm only that issue and omit the remaining target results.

A research review reads, from the designated `knowledge/<record>/output.json`, the exposition and evidence of every result, the failed attempts and the open questions. Even if another record has superseded it, write the verdict of the designated target itself, and explain what the related supersession history and reviews mean for this judgment. A goal review cross-checks `goal.md`, the user conditions and instructions delivered, and the whole accumulated knowledge. The raw texts of inputs the user entered after `goal.md` are in the files of `<instance>/control/inputs/`. Read whether there is an input that changed or added to the goal or requirements, and cross-check it too. Read the current body by following the supersession chain, and do not ignore divided branches or conflicting reviews. The research status of a plan record (`research-status.md`) is the plan's summary, so do not use it as grounds for a verdict.

In a research review, if there is already a published review of the same target, before reading it review the target directly and first write your judgment and reasons for each result in the `work/` of your own session folder. Attaching another review to the same target is usually meant to look again, with different eyes, at places the prior review may have missed. If you read the prior review's conclusions first, those conclusions become the reference point and you easily miss the same places in the same way. After writing your own judgment, read the existing reviews, and for items where the judgments diverge, cross-check the grounds of both against the target's text and decide which is right. Write that cross-check and conclusion in `summary`. Do not transfer the conclusions of existing reviews without your own confirmation. A goal review uses the verdicts of research reviews as grounds, so it does not follow this order.

If the target research is a revision that supersedes an existing record with `supersedes`, collect the defects noted by the reviews attached to the superseded record. The items that received a negative verdict (`not_supported`, `rejected`, `differs`) and the defects the `summary` noted concretely are the targets. Confirm one by one whether each defect was actually fixed in the revision and, if not, whether the revision no longer claims that part, and write the conclusion per defect in `summary`. Do not end the review after looking only at whether the defects were resolved. Errors the prior review missed may remain in the unchanged parts too, so review the whole revision as if it were a record seen for the first time.

## Reviewing the work and the peer together

The `work` and `peer` of a bundle are exact published record IDs. First read the original work and leave your initial judgment in your work folder, then read the peer response. Cross-check the claims, counterexamples and criticism the peer newly wrote against the original work, applying the same rigor to both sides. Review every result stated in the `results` of the original work and of the peer. Do not reject an original result on the peer's negative opinion alone, and do not accept something only because the peer wrote that it confirmed it.

An exploration may have no fixed result list. Read the whole body, and judge the main content claimed as proved or exactly computed, the claims that govern the interpretation, and the content meant to be used as later evidence, designating each by its exact sentence, domain and published file. If you find a defect or an important unconfirmed part in the body, leave it in the exposition even if it is outside the results array. Do not give a negative verdict to a stated conjecture or hunch because it is not proved. Distinguish the range of observations actually confirmed from universal statements. Do not turn the value of an idea or the superiority of a research direction into a true/false verdict.

The exploration as a whole, beyond the individual claims reviewed, is not certified. Because a peer's new result can be confirmed by this reviewer, who is not the author of that result, there is no need to have the same proof rewritten in separate research. But the reviewer does not complete, in the author's stead, an idea without a proof into long-term research. Distinguish restoring a simple omission from creating a new key proof. A conclusion obtainable only by producing an important new argument yourself is left separately as content not yet independently confirmed, and the original result is not accepted on its basis.

A bundle review preserves the source for each scope of verdict. For content with a result ID, designate the original `record/result`; for a claim in prose, the original `record/file/statement`. In `statement`, write the exact scope of the claim that can specify the definitions, assumptions, quantification and conclusion. Do not certify a cited expression by correcting it into something stronger. Follow-up research that cites a bundle review's result ID must follow that source and scope of verdict as well.

Judge the overviews of the original work and the peer separately. An exaggeration in one overview or the failure of some results does not erase the valid results of the other. Leave the scope you could not confirm as unconfirmed. The format and force of existing standalone research reviews and goal reviews are kept as they are.

## Judging the content of a research output

Cross-check the target's `overview` title and summary against the exposition and all results, failures and open questions. Judge, along with whether each sentence is true, whether it functions as a valid introduction of the whole output. Confirm that it does not exaggerate the impression of success by leaving out important failures or unverified parts, and conversely that it does not read as if there were only failures by omitting valid achievements. Also see whether it distinguishes finite confirmation, partial proof and universal solution and the scope of formal assurance, and the failure of a strengthening candidate from a counterexample to the original question. Even if the results are correct, if the overview has an important omission or exaggeration, write that defect and the verdict in `overview_assessment`. Distinguish this judgment from the mathematics, Lean and alignment verdicts of each result.

Review is at once following the evidence that a result is right and actively looking for where a result could be wrong. First confirm what each result claims in its definitions, assumptions and scope of quantification, and judge whether the evidence presented actually supports that claim. Then look for missing assumptions, the domains of variables, the quantifier order and the dependence of witnesses on it, boundary conditions and exaggerated conclusions, and test small counterexamples yourself at suspicious places. Cross-check the confirmed scope and limits written in the result's `verification` against the scope of the exposition's claims. Also read the conclusions that can be drawn from the failed attempts and the open questions, and see whether there is overgeneralization or omission. If a result relied on an existing record, confirm that the four cross-checks of [common.md](common.md) actually hold. If it relied on statements of an exploration or a peer, confirm whether there is a review that accepted that exact claim. If there is no such review and the target did not establish it independently either, judge that part to be without evidence. If you discover a defect in a result of such an evidence record, write that record and result ID and the content of the defect in `summary`. Reflect the effect of that defect on the target's result in the target's verdict. The review of the evidence record itself is requested separately by the plan.

If the instructions of an additional review tell you that the verdict of a prior review on the same target may have been wrong, read the content of the dispute written in the instructions as the issue to confirm. Open the prior review record itself only after writing your own judgment first, in the order fixed above, and cross-check its verdict and grounds against the target's text. If the prior verdict is confirmed wrong, write that review record's ID in the `supersedes` of this review record, which re-judged the whole target, and supersede the prior review. Cases where the prior verdict misread the target's definitions, assumptions or scope of claims, or where, even reading the target correctly, the evidence it presented does not support that verdict, fall under this. Write in `summary` where and why the prior verdict was wrong. If the prior verdict is right or you could not decide which is right, do not supersede it, and write that judgment and the reason in `summary`. A prior review not superseded remains a current review. If the prior review left a negative verdict on the same item of the same result, then even if this review affirms that item the two verdicts conflict, so that result is not read as an established result.

If the instructions of an additional review tell you of a defect in an evidence record, judge in each result's `assessment` whether the target result actually uses that defective part and, if so, whether the claim holds without it. Write the conclusion of cross-checking the defect in `summary`. Only when this cross-check is finished can the plan read the target again as an established result.

Do not stop the review on finding one defect. The remaining results and scopes of the same output must be reviewed to the end for the next plan to know everything to fix. Distinguish whether a step the exposition omitted is immediately restored from context or whether a step or assumption actually needed by the argument is missing. The former is not judged a defect. Nor is it judged a defect merely because the order of account or the style of notation differs between the exposition and Lean.

Confirm data extractions, literature summaries and mathematical expositions in two directions. First find the important content and conditions in the source and the evidence, and cross-check that nothing is missing from the output. Then confirm that the claims the output makes match the actual evidence. Do not judge the content sufficient because a file exists, a link opens or the format check passed. For computations, choose a small reproduction or a check of the method needed for the claim, and for literature, cross-check against the actual cited source. Leave sources not confirmed and the scope of execution in the limitations of the verdict.

If there are Lean sources, take the published sources and the published dependency files they need into a **working copy in your own session folder**, and append a `#print axioms <theorem name>` line for each theorem the exposition claims to the end of the copy. Then compile the copy through the instance's Lean broker, as 'Running Lean' in [common.md](common.md) describes: `run` for a single file, `build` with the copied module root and the file under review when the sources are several modules. That one compile gives you both the recompilation and the axiom list. With `build`, the modules you did not touch are reused from the broker's cache, since it built them from the same bytes; only the file you appended to is compiled again, and the axiom list still covers everything the theorems depend on. Do not modify the original published record. The broker applies the `lean` settings of `settings.json`, with your session folder as the working directory when `cwd` is `null`, and its `version` command gives you the current version string to record. For the estimate you give with the compile, you have three sources: the author's own estimate and measured cost in the result's `verification`, the broker's calibration table for the modules the file imports, and the file itself, read as 'Estimating what a compile will need' in [LEAN.md](LEAN.md) describes. The appended `#print axioms` lines do not change the cost. Keep everything you produce in your own folder and use the current installation as it is. Do not stop the review because the version string differs from the original research's.

Judge the axiom list by 'Criteria for accepting a Lean result as a proof' in [common.md](common.md). The axiom list the author wrote in `verification` is reference material only; confirm it directly. If the broker reports the job as `failed` or `cancelled`, Lean did not run, and the verdict is `not_checked` with that reason, not `rejected`. Leave the actual commands, environment and exit result, the estimate and the measured cost, the axiom list and the confirmed scope in the published verdict or in linked supplementary material.

Separately from the Lean recompile, read the correspondence of objects, definitions, assumptions, scope of quantification, conclusions and argument between the exposition and Lean. See whether the proposition the exposition states is the same mathematics as the type of the Lean theorem. For example, if the Lean theorem is `∀ x : Nat, ∃ y : Nat, y = x` but the exposition says "some one y is equal to every x", Lean chooses a y for each x while the exposition speaks of one y common to all x, so the quantifier order differs and it is not the same mathematics. Even if Lean accepted the source, it may be a weaker theorem than the exposition, and even if it is the same theorem as the exposition, it may fall short of the user's goal. Write each of the following four judgments with grounds.

| Item | `verdict` and the meaning of the judgment |
|---|---|
| `assessment` | `supported`: the evidence presented supports the result's claim. `not_supported`: it does not. `undetermined`: the judgment was not finished. |
| `lean` | `accepted`: a copy of the published source recompiled, and for every theorem the exposition claims the axiom list is within the allowed axioms of [common.md](common.md). `rejected`: the compile failed because of the source (type error, proof failure, etc.), or a theorem depends on an axiom outside the allowed ones. `not_checked`: the compile or the axiom check could not be finished for a cause unrelated to the source (the Lean executable or a dependency library not found, a settings error, a broker job that ended `failed` or `cancelled` without running Lean, etc.). `not_applicable`: a result without Lean. |
| `alignment` | `matches`: the mathematics of the exposition and Lean agree. `differs`: they differ. `not_checked`: not confirmed. `not_applicable`: a result without Lean. |
| `terminology` | `conforms`: no violation under 'Judging glossary conformance' below. `deviates`: there is a name violation or a meaning violation. |

In the `reason` of each judgment, write concretely the published files, theorems and results that support the conclusion and your own confirmation. Do not confuse an environment problem that prevented the compile from starting with the rejection of a proof. A verdict that the evidence does not support the claim also does not mean that the proposition has been proved false. Distinguish a revealed counterexample, an omission and inability to judge, so that the next plan can choose the research or additional review needed.

### Judging glossary conformance

For each result, judge whether its exposition and the published files the result uses follow the glossaries. There are two to cross-check against. The global one is the glossary.md of the plan record the target output stated in its references as actually applied. The local one is the target's own glossary-local.md, where every internal symbol, auxiliary object or name that the record uses in more than one place must be defined, and where the preamble names the local entries of other records the target uses as they are. The author starts from the latest snapshot at the start of work, and if a new snapshot was adopted during the work, updates the applied snapshot. Do not apply a new snapshot retroactively merely because a concurrent plan published it during the writing. For a past record that did not state its applied snapshot, cross-check against the glossary of the last plan published before the target in the publication order of the instance Git (the `Publish <record ID>` commits; the index no longer lists plans). If there is no such snapshot either, cross-check against the definitions of goal.md. A record published before local glossaries existed has no glossary-local.md; for such a target, read its internal names from its exposition and, where one exists, its glossary-proposals.md, and do not count the missing file as a violation. If the applied snapshot cannot be settled because different snapshots are cited, state the scope within which the original names and definitions were cross-checked and the limits of the verdict. Using, in a past record, a glossary name that was in force at the time is not regarded retroactively as a violation even if a later snapshot retired that name. Conversely, newly using a name already retired in the glossary that applies to the target output is a name violation. It is not allowed merely because some older snapshot had that name. The rules of both glossaries are in 'Glossaries' of [common.md](common.md).

There are two kinds of violation, and each can occur against either glossary.

- **Name violation**: a global object was called by another name (a former name, a retired name, a new synonym, or a local entry that defines the same object again under its own name). An internal symbol, auxiliary object or name that the record uses in more than one place has no local entry, or its local entry is not the definition the exposition actually uses. A name taken over from another record's local glossary is used without the preamble saying so. An abbreviation whose meaning and scope are stated where it is used, within one argument, is not regarded as a missing entry. A global name was used for another object while stating the difference; here the mathematics is readable, but a new name should have been attached.
- **Meaning violation**: a global name or symbol was used in a meaning different from the glossary without stating the difference, whether in the exposition or as the definition of a local entry.

Whether a local entry flagged as a global candidate deserves adoption is not part of this verdict; that is the plan's decision. The fact that an object has a local entry does not verify its relation to existing objects. If a result uses a relation to an existing object, confirm what that relation is and whether the cited evidence actually applies. In particular, see that no condition that changes the conclusion, such as the domain or a factor dividing by the total count, is missing. A mere naming problem is judged under terminology, an argument that rests on an unsupported identity under assessment, and a gap in the link to the Lean object under the scope concerned in alignment. Do not accept as a proof of the original expression a result that redefined an old expression and omitted the link. If you discover an outdated state or a wrong link in the global glossary, leave the location, the exact relation and the evidence so that the plan updates the family concerned. Do not re-examine the whole global glossary unrelated to the result concerned in every review.

The fact that a new name was defined in the local glossary does not resolve the problem of reusing an existing definition. Confirm whether an existing global object was given a different name unnecessarily. Distinguish an actually needed new object from explicitly delimited local notation; which entries to adopt into the global glossary is judged by the plan. A Lean declaration name differing from the glossary is not a violation if the exposition stated the correspondence. If there is no violation, `conforms`; if there is even one, `deviates`. In the `reason` of `deviates`, write for each violation the location (file and section), the name used, the glossary entry concerned and the kind.

If a meaning violation breaks the argument itself, reflect it in `assessment` as well. For example, if a result proved for the glossary's X was applied to an X used in a different meaning, that step has no evidence. If the record argued consistently within its own definitions, the mathematics holds, so judge it a violation only in `terminology`. `terminology` does not decide whether a result is mathematically established and does not enter into the judgment of conflict.

## Judging goal achievement

In a goal review, connect each of the user's goal and requirements to the result in the current published knowledge and the independent review by which it is satisfied. Read the goal exactly as written in `goal.md`. Do not reinterpret the goal into an easier sentence to fit the results obtained. Confirm unreviewed claims, unresolved conflicting reviews, and open questions that affect the conclusion. Examine the evidence needed for the achievement judgment across the whole knowledge, but the mere fact that unrelated research is still running does not make an already satisfied goal unachieved.

Beware of cases where the proposition of the goal and that of the result differ. If the goal is a proof of P but the published knowledge has only a result under an additional assumption H (if H then P) and H is not proved, the goal is not satisfied. If the goal is a proof of C and a disproof of C was obtained, that disproof does not satisfy the goal. If the goal is "a proof or disproof of C", it may be satisfied by a reviewed disproof. When using a Lean result as evidence, confirm, together with that result's `lean: accepted` verdict, that the proposition of the Lean theorem speaks of the same objects, domains and quantification as the goal's wording.

If the goal is a proposition demanding a proof or a disproof and `goal.md` has not exempted formal proof, judge by 'Formal proof required for goal achievement' in [common.md](common.md). If the Lean theorem that section demands is not in the published knowledge, it is `not_achieved`. Even if the theorem exists, the same holds if its review did not give `lean: accepted` and `alignment: matches`. The fact that an informal proof received `supported` does not replace this requirement. Also confirm, by 'Judging published knowledge' in the same document, whether the results used as evidence are currently established along the evidence chain.

Write `goal.verdict` as `achieved` if actually satisfied, `not_achieved` if not satisfied, and `undetermined` if the evidence needed for the judgment could not be confirmed. In `goal.reason`, write why the user's conditions and the proofs, expositions and reviews connect, and, if not achieved or undetermined, the remaining problems and their effect. Do not conclude that the goal was achieved from a passing format check, Lean acceptance or a single positive verdict of a research review alone.

The **`achieved` of an integrated goal review** that passed the re-check is the signal by which the runner completes the instance. The `supported` or `accepted` of a research review is not a completion signal. After completion no new sessions are dispatched, and the outputs of sessions already running receive the same check and integration after they end. Therefore the achievement verdict must hold on the evidence published now.

## Submitting the review record

Write `output/output.json` according to section 5 of [protocol.md](protocol.md). Include the common fields `category: "review"`, the `references` of the published knowledge used, and the `supersedes` of the review records actually superseded. Make `target` match the input's `task.target`.

In the `overview` of every review output, briefly explain the review target, the scope of the verdicts and the key limitations. A research review has `results`, `overview_assessment` and `summary`. Handle every `results[].id` of the original research exactly once each, and in each item write the target ID `result` and `assessment`, `lean`, `alignment` and `terminology`. Each judgment has the `verdict` above and a nonempty `reason`. `overview_assessment` is a `verdict` of `supported`, `not_supported` or `undetermined` on the target's title and summary, with a `reason`. In `summary`, write the judgment of the whole output, the points confirmed in the failed approaches and open questions, and the important limitations. Even if the original research has no results, review the title, summary, failed approaches and open questions.

A goal review has `goal: {"verdict": "…", "reason": "…"}` instead of the list of research results. A bundle review has `target: {"kind":"bundle","work":"…","peer":"…"}`, the body `exposition`, the `overview_assessments` of both overviews, `results` and `summary`. Each result has the review's own unique `id`, the original source `source` and the existing four verdicts. The stated result IDs of the original research and of the peer are each judged exactly once. Claims designated in the exploration and peer bodies are added with the published file and the exact sentence. The whole format follows protocol.md. Only a record that reviewed the same bundle may be superseded with `supersedes`. If the verdicts clash with another bundle or an existing standalone review, expose that clash and let the plan assign an additional review of the appropriate scope.

Publish the detailed explanation or reproduction material needed in your own `output/` and tell the path to read in `reason` or `summary`. Supplementary material is the grounds of your own verdict, not a means of correcting the published target record.

So that the next plan can judge from the published records alone what can be established and what more must be done, write actual confirmations and non-confirmations separately. Run the official check of [common.md](common.md) yourself, then submit by yourself and finish.
