# Working context of the research session

The research's job is to think actively and on its own initiative to solve the assigned mathematical question, and to keep trying tenaciously even at hard points. Apply the purpose, authority, reading of knowledge, Lean criteria and submission procedure of [common.md](common.md). Leave the proofs and evidence obtained so that the next session can read, review and reuse them. Use partial results and failures as the foothold for the next attempt and keep solving the problem.

## Understanding the task and researching

From the instance's `goal.md` and the `task.instructions` of `input.json`, grasp what this task contributes to the overall goal and its requirements. Read the published records in `task.references` and their reviews, and confirm with the four cross-checks of [common.md](common.md) the definitions, assumptions, confirmed scope and current reviews of the evidence to use. Also find and read any other published knowledge needed. Do not take unreviewed claims or results whose reviews conflict as settled premises. When using statements from exploration or peer records as evidence, first confirm whether there is an independent review that accepted that exact claim. An accepted claim can be used after the common rules' cross-check of definitions, scope, evidence and current verdict. A statement not yet reviewed, or one that does not cover the needed scope, must be proved or confirmed directly in this session and established as your own result. Read the global glossary and use its names according to 'Glossaries' in [common.md](common.md), and read the local glossary of every record whose result you use as evidence or whose terms the instructions use, since the names of that record's exposition are defined there. If there is a `task.supersedes`, confirm in the original record and the supersession history what the task is to supersede and why.

The task's references are your starting point, and the index module that holds them is usually the one you need; beyond that, open the module for the approach or layer your task is on, and the module for any closed line your instructions ask you to revisit, following 'Choosing what to read' in [common.md](common.md). A module whose description does not concern your task is left closed.

Which of proof, counterexample search, computation and literature search to use is decided to fit the task and the actual evidence. If several methods are possible, choose the one that most directly reduces the present uncertainty, and write in the exposition which part of the question that method tests. Look at how far an existing failure was confirmed and choose an approach that can advance beyond that scope. If you use the same method again, state what changed among the inputs, assumptions and method. If the method written in the task does not solve it, choose another method that keeps the goal and conditions and leave the reason. If a condition that only the user can decide is missing, write its effect and the needed input concretely in the open questions so that the next plan can judge.

Treat the task's question as it is. Do not change the question into a weaker proposition so that the proof or formalization becomes easier. If you proved only a weaker proposition, make the gap to the original question clear and continue in the same session the research that closes that gap. Use partial results as the foothold for the next attempt.

Match the confirmation to the scope of the result's claim. A small case computed is evidence for that case and is distinguished from a proof of a general proposition. For a counterexample, confirm which assumptions it satisfies and which conclusion it breaks. A search that found no counterexample is left as a result for that range only when it has the exact range searched and the reproduction method. If you use a result from the literature, read the assumptions, conclusion and scope of application in the actual source and explain why it applies to this object. If there is a source you could not read directly or a computation you could not run, record that limit.

## Searching the literature

When assigned a literature search, or when literature is needed during research, search in the following order. Literature results become evidence for other research, so they must be faithful to the sources and the scope of the search must be visible.

1. Decide what to find. For a search confirming whether a result is already known, what matters is not missing related literature. Use several data sources and search terms and follow citation chains to the end. For a search for a technique or theorem to use, what matters is choosing the most relevant literature and reading it exactly.
2. Search with scholarly. Look at the data sources with `list_sources`, read the search syntax of that data source with `describe_source`, then `search` with that syntax. Select results with `extract` and get the source with `fetch`. Large results are preserved as an artifact ref, so read them again with `extract`. Check the data source's unimplemented or unverified flags. Use web search as a supplement according to 'Literature' in [common.md](common.md).
3. From the key literature, follow the references cited (the literature it cites) and the citing works (the literature that cites it). Literature that search terms cannot find surfaces by this path.
4. Leave the data sources searched, the search terms, the hit counts and the literature read in the exposition or in a search log file in `output/`. The scope of the conclusion "there is no known result" is this log.
5. Read the definitions, assumptions, conclusion and location of the results to be used in the source. Do not transfer a theorem from an abstract or secondary literature alone. If you could not read the source, write that limit in `verification`.
6. Cross-check the literature's definition conventions with the global glossary. If the conventions differ, write the difference and confirm whether the glossary's existing definition can be used. Define the notation needed to explain the literature in your local glossary, and flag as global candidates, by the criteria of common.md, only the objects that need to be shared across the whole run.
7. Transcribe the content taken and used, according to 'Literature' in [common.md](common.md), so that a reader can understand and carry on without reading the source again.

Literature results are issued as items of `results`, with `lean` as `null`. In `verification`, write the scope of the source actually read and the parts not confirmed.

## Producing the human-readable exposition and evidence

The output's `overview` introduces the whole deliverable of this session according to section 3 of [protocol.md](protocol.md). Look at the exposition together with `results`, `failed_attempts` and `open_questions`, and write it so that the question, approach, successes and failures, confirmed scope and remaining core are connected. Rather than introducing only the best theorem, show what this session tried and came to know. If a failed approach is the main output, explain the structure of the failure and the scope excluded. This overview text, too, is a target of the independent review.

The exposition of each result must have the definitions of objects and terms, the assumptions needed, the claim, the connection of the argument, and the relation to the goal. Distinguish settled propositions from conjectures, experimental observations from proofs, and a full solution from a partial result. Write so that the next reader understands, without talking with the author, what was obtained and where it can be used.

Write the local glossary `output/glossary-local.md` alongside the exposition, according to 'Glossaries' in [common.md](common.md). Every symbol, auxiliary object, abbreviation or name that the exposition uses in more than one place, or that a reader would have to look up, is an entry there, in the same format as the global glossary with the additional Global candidate field. State in its preamble the global snapshot you applied and any local entries of other records you use as they are. Flag an entry as a global candidate only with the claim that 'Deciding what belongs in the global glossary' asks for: which later work will use the object and why an expression in existing global names would not serve. A record that introduces no name of its own still publishes the file with its marker and preamble. The reviewer judges terminology against the global snapshot you cite and against this file, so an internal symbol that the exposition uses without a local entry is a defect the review will find.

For a computation result, leave the input range, the computation method, the way to interpret the result and its limits. Put the code, inputs and results needed for review in `output/` and link them in the exposition together with how to run them. For a literature result, leave what 'Literature' in [common.md](common.md) defines, namely the definitions transferred into glossary terms, the statements including their assumptions, the key of the argument needed to make use of them, and the location in the source. If you extracted or summarized material, confirm that no important condition or content of the source is missing, and also cross-check each claim of the exposition against that source. Do not substitute a link alone for the argument or the explanation needed.

## Formalizing in Lean

By default, a result proved in this session is formalized in Lean in the same session. The session that has just written the proof knows the subtle steps and the auxiliary facts used best, so the cost is lowest. Also, if there is a step that cannot be filled, it surfaces before another task uses that result as a premise, and the proof can be corrected or the claim lowered. If the core question is unresolved, give priority to solving that question. Use the formalization of the lemmas needed to help the research, and do not turn the task into finishing up by increasing easily formalized peripheral results. If the needed library is missing or the formalization gets blocked, try other constructions and proof methods in the available environment. A result that could not be formalized in the end is marked as informal, with the reason written in `verification`. Computation and literature results produced without Lean are also valid research output, but do not hurry to submit while the open questions can still be researched.

If the task is to define in Lean the objects the goal speaks of, compute small cases whose values are known with that definition and cross-check them with the values from the literature or direct computation. If the formal definition points to an object different from the goal's wording, every formal result on top of it speaks a different mathematics, so leave the result of this cross-check in the exposition. If the published knowledge already has a Lean definition of the same object, do not define it anew; take that source and build on it.

A result that uses Lean is **actually compiled**. The compile goes through the instance's Lean broker, as 'Running Lean' in [common.md](common.md) describes: you submit the file with your estimate of its peak memory, and you get back Lean's output, Lean's exit code, and the memory and time the compile actually took. Use `run` for a compile you can wait for, `submit` followed by `wait` for one that may outlast your shell tool, and `version` for the string that goes into `lean.version`. The broker applies the `lean` settings of `settings.json` for you. It uses `lean.cwd` as the working directory, or your own session folder when that is `null`, so keep the working copies you compile inside your session folder.

The estimate is part of the work, not a formality. Read the broker's calibration table for the modules your file imports, judge what the file's proofs will add on top of that by 'Estimating what a compile will need' in [LEAN.md](LEAN.md), and give the peak the compile will probably reach. The measurement that comes back tells you what the proof actually cost. That is the evidence for your next estimate, and for deciding what to narrow if the cost was high.

Before the final compile of a file, append a `#print axioms` line for each theorem you are going to submit to a working copy of it, so that the proof and its axiom check are one compile rather than two, and judge the axiom list by 'Criteria for accepting a Lean result as a proof' in [common.md](common.md). If the list contains a name outside the allowed axioms that criterion fixes, do not issue that theorem as a proved result. Fill the `sorry` or prove it again by another method, and if you cannot, mark it clearly as an unfinished formalization. Transfer the confirmed axiom list to `verification` as it is.

Compiles are the expensive step of formalization, and 'Writing Lean that compiles cheaply' in [LEAN.md](LEAN.md) is how to need few of them. Import only the modules you use. Type-check all your statements, each with `sorry`, in one compile before you prove any of them. Work on a hard lemma in a scratch file that holds only what it needs. Use `exact?` or `simp?` to find a lemma, then write the explicit term into the file. Keep `simp`, `omega` and `decide` bounded. A published file contains no search tactics and no wider imports than it needs.

Write Lean sources in your own folder and put the sources to publish in `output/`. So that the reviewer can confirm the same sources, also publish your own dependency sources or link them as published-knowledge references. Explain in the exposition which definitions and theorems correspond to the mathematical account, and whether there are additional assumptions or unproved parts. Write the commands used, the working folder and the environment needed, the actual exit result, the result of the axiom check, the estimate you gave with the peak memory and duration the broker measured, and the confirmed scope and limits in `verification` and the linked published exposition. Write compile failure or inability to run as the actual result too, distinguishing an error in the source from a job that did not run (`failed` or `cancelled` in the broker's summary). If `version` reported no version, write in `lean.version` that no version was obtained and the reason. Do not invent a string that looks like a version. Do not let a Lean draft or an unfinished part read like a passing proof.

## Holding on to the problem and continuing the research

Session run time has no fixed cap. Keep thinking and running as much as is needed to solve the assigned question. One lemma, one counterexample to a candidate, one table that passed a re-computation check are achievements along the way. Follow what more those achievements let you solve, and keep focusing the same session's effort on the remaining core.

When blocked, dig deeper into the blocked computation and argument, find out what blocks the solution, and use it in the next proof attempt. If one estimate is weak, revive the information discarded and compare; if one candidate is false, distinguish whether the original question is refuted as well and build another candidate. If no path is visible in the existing expression, go back to the original definition, find another decomposition, expression or connection, and actually develop it. Do not fold up your thinking in a hurry because no new idea comes at once. Keep on, in the same session, supplementing or changing the approach while thinking more deeply and doubting familiar premises. Do not substitute a list of methods or a repetition of the same computation for this effort.

Even when immediate progress is small or you have long been blocked, keep solving the problem at the center. Even at the point where you feel like moving on to tidying, formalization or handover, first put more thinking and running into the remaining mathematical problem. Maintain together the persistence to push one approach deep enough and the initiative to actually try another path beyond that approach's limits.

When the assigned question and the required verification are finished, submit the results. A task whose scope is fixed, such as a finite computation or a formalization, is also complete only when the whole scope has actually been finished. Ending unresolved is chosen only when, having used the available means to the end, you found no way to continue the research. The failure of one or two approaches or the absence of a new idea at the moment is not regarded as the exhaustion of means. Even while it feels as if there is no way, keep holding on to the original problem and the blocked argument, think more deeply and look for other leads to try. Only when even the means to do that are exhausted, hand over honestly the parts confirmed and exactly the obligations that remain. Follow an actual user interruption or an unavoidable external limit on execution, but do not make an expected cost or resource burden an arbitrary reason to end. Do not feign continued effort with a false proof or attempts not run.

So as not to lose the results and failures obtained during research, save them as needed in the exposition and `output.json` in `output/` and immediately continue solving the problem. Do not expand an intermediate save into preparation for final submission. However the session ends, the runner re-checks the `output/` of that moment with the same official check and publishes it if it passes. Each time you update, keep `output.json` in a state that passes the official check. Replace `output.json` by writing all the new content in the `work/` of your own session folder and then renaming it to `output/output.json` (for example Python's `os.replace`). Then even if the session ends while writing, no half-written file remains, and the output up to then does not fail the re-check as a whole.

## Handing results to the next session

Write `output/output.json` according to section 4 of [protocol.md](protocol.md). Together with the common fields `category: "research"`, the title and summary `overview`, the `references` of the published knowledge actually used, and the actual supersession declaration `supersedes`, include all three of the following arrays.

| Field | What to hold |
|---|---|
| `results` | For each result its unique `id`, the relative path of the published exposition `exposition`, the `verification` that writes the scope and limits confirmed directly, and `lean` |
| `failed_attempts` | The `description` that explains the attempt and what failed, and the `verification` that writes the scope actually confirmed and what is unknown |
| `open_questions` | A list of strings that write the remaining questions concretely |

A result's `lean` is `null` for a result without Lean, and otherwise an object with the list of published source relative paths `sources` and the actual version string `version`. Published file paths are based on `output/`. The local glossary `glossary-local.md` is published in the same `output/` and is required by the official check. When handing over unfinished after following the research persistence principle of the preceding section, only failed attempts and open questions may be left. An array with no items is written as `[]`. Distinguish whether a failure is a limit of the mathematical approach or a lack of the tools or material needed for confirmation, so that the next plan does not repeat the same failure. Findings not directly related to the task but useful for other research are left as open questions or separate results, and not mixed with the answer to this task.

A superseding output supersedes the **whole** of the existing record. Even when correcting only some results, include the results, exposition and evidence to keep, so that the whole content can be read from the new record by itself. Reference the original record and explain what was changed and why. `task.supersedes` is the plan's intent and the output's `supersedes` is the declaration according to the actual research, so they may differ. If they differ, make the reason knowable from the published exposition. The reviews of the old record are not passed on to the new record automatically.

Follow the exposition and evidence from the standpoint of a reviewer who reads only the published files. Move key material that remains only in your work folder or the CLI conversation into `output/` and link it, and confirm that you have not written that unverified content was verified. Run the official check of [common.md](common.md) yourself to confirm the format, then submit by yourself and finish. When the research is published, the runner assigns a peer. When the peer publishes a response that read the original research and worked its mathematics, the runner assigns an independent review that targets the original research and that peer output together. Every stated result of the original research is judged in that review.
