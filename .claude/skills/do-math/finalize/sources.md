# Finding and checking the sources the records cite

Some results of the library were taken from the literature, and their records name the source. The records of the package, and the paper that is written from them, pass those citations on to their readers. This session finds every cited work in a registry of publications, corrects its bibliography entry, opens it as far as it can be opened, and checks what the records say of it. It also looks for sources of a few results that the library proves and that are known in the literature under a name. It is the only session of the run that looks outside the research, and for that it has the research's literature server.

## Why the sources are checked here

A citation is copied onward by everything written from the record that carries it, so a wrong attribution found only when the paper is almost finished stands by then in every such text, and each has to be corrected. The citations are therefore checked where they first stand, before the library and its records are frozen.

The second task is there because the paper cites only what a record names: where the library proves a classical result whose record names no source, the paper proves it in full, and readers object to this more often than to anything else. A result that the library proves and that a source states in the same form is cited in the paper and not proved again there. For that, the source has to be in the record before the freeze.

## What you are given

- `bibliography.bib`: the bibliography of the research.
- `cited.json`: the keys of the bibliography that a record cites and that are still to be checked, with the records that cite them. A work that an earlier session with this instruction verified is listed again only when a record that cites it has been written since, and otherwise needs no entry from you. A record cites a work in two ways. Its header can name the key under `source`, which says that the result of the record is taken from that work. And its text can carry a citation, which is the key between square brackets, alone or followed by a comma and the place in the work, as in `[abel1826, p. 159]`. For each record the entry gives the path, the text, whether the header names the key (`header`), and the citations of the key as the text writes them (`citations`).

```json
[{"key": "abel1826", "records": [{"declaration": "Lib.abel", "path": "records/Lib/abel.md", "text": "...", "header": true, "citations": ["[abel1826, p. 159]"]}]}]
```

- `requests.json`: the results for which a source is wanted, each with the declaration that states it, the name under which the result is known, and the reason. An entry can also carry `source`, the key of a bibliography entry; the section "The requests" says what that means. It can be an empty array.

```json
[{"declaration": "Lib.abel", "result": "Abel's binomial identity", "reason": "..."}]
```

- `records/`: all records. `lean/`: the library, for the exact statement of a declaration. You write no Lean and compile nothing. The library's root is `{{root}}` and its main theorem `{{main}}`.

## Searching

The literature server attached to this session is the instrument. Begin with `list_sources` to see which sources it serves, read a source's query syntax with `describe_source`, query with `search`, read what a call retained with `extract`, and obtain a specific item by its identifier with `fetch`. It serves sources that are reached through public interfaces, among them the indexes zbMATH, OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar, the registry Crossref and the preprint server arXiv. The full text of many journal articles cannot be retrieved through it; look then for a preprint or an author's own copy, on the web if the server does not have it. Many entries of zbMATH carry a reviewer's summary, which counts as an abstract.

Read the outcome of each search exactly. An empty result says that this source returned nothing for this expression. A result marked restricted, a database error, or the code `source_not_enabled` says nothing about whether the work exists. `rate_limited` says that the source refused for the moment; try again after the interval it names.

## The cited sources

For each key of `cited.json`:

1. **Find the work.** Search by title, authors and year, in more than one source where the first returns nothing. A work is found when a registry has an entry that is plainly this work. Take from that entry the identifier that fixes which document it is: a DOI, an arXiv number with its version, a Zbl or MR number.
2. **Correct the entry.** Bring authors, title, place of publication, year and pages in `bibliography.bib` to what the registry gives, and add the identifier. The key stays. An entry holds what a reference list prints, and nothing else: authors, title, where the work appeared, year, pages, identifier. The paper prints every field of an entry it cites, so nothing in an entry may be addressed to the sessions of the research, such as a `note` that says which version "the records cite" or how the numbering of the work's theorems changed between versions. Take such a remark out of the entry. Where a record needs it to say where a statement stands, it belongs in that record's `## Literature`, and you put it there. A note that describes the work itself stays, such as the translator of an edition.
3. **Open it as far as it opens**, and note how far: the full text, parts of it, the abstract only, or nothing.
4. **Check what the records say of it**, sentence by sentence, as the next section describes. Does the work state what the record says it states, at the place the record gives, under the hypotheses the record gives, and is it attributed to the right author? Where the record says how the source's notation corresponds to the library's, does that hold?

One rule governs every correction: nothing is said of a source beyond what was opened. If you opened the full text and the theorem stands there as described, the passage stays. If the record is wrong about something you can see, such as the number of a theorem, a hypothesis or an attribution, correct it from what you see. If you could open only the abstract, the record can say what the abstract shows, and a statement about a particular theorem or proof inside the work is cut back to that. Where the record gives an account of how much of the source was read, bring it to what was opened in this check.

A work of which you could open nothing is a case of its own, because nothing that a record says of it can be held against its words. Look first for another work that you can open and that states what the sentence cites, such as a thesis of the same author, a survey or a later paper in which the result is stated. Cite that work in the sentence in the place of the one you could not open, under a key of its own, with its entry in the bibliography, and check the sentence against it like every other. A header key `source` that named the unopened work names the new one only when the new work states the result in the form the library has, as the section "The requests" requires of a source; otherwise the header key is taken out. Where you find no such work, the citation goes, in the way that the next paragraph describes for a work that cannot be found, and the rewritten sentence neither cites the work nor says what is in it. What differs from a work that cannot be found is the entry. In both cases, with another work cited in its place or with none, the entry of the unopened work stays in the bibliography, and `sources.json` lists it with `opened` as `none`. The program accepts that only when no record cites the work any more.

A work that cannot be found is removed. Take its entry out of `bibliography.bib`. Rewrite every sentence that cites it so that the sentence stands without it, and take out the header key `source` and the part `## Literature` where they rest on it. The mathematics stays, because the library proves it; what goes is the claim about where it was published. Where the proof in a record consisted of the reference alone, write the proof in words from the declaration's Lean proof in `lean/`. The removal is recorded for the user, so say in `note` where you searched and with which expressions.

If a key can be neither found nor shown to be absent, because the server did not answer or kept refusing, try again later in your session and by other means. If it is still undecided at the end, leave it as it is: no entry in `sources.json`, its bibliography entry and its records unchanged. The program then stops the run and names the key, and that is the right outcome. A source must not be removed, nor called verified, on the strength of a search that did not run.

Entries of the bibliography that no record cites are not yours to check; return them as they are.

## What is checked, sentence by sentence

A check of the sources can pass while the sentences about them are wrong. To ask whether the work exists and contains the result, even twice, lets through attributions that fail when the work is opened at the place the text names. A theorem is cited for a quantity that it does not treat. A remark is cited as a theorem. A family of objects is called by a name that the cited authors do not use. A number is said to be printed at a place where it is not. An article cannot be opened at all. Each of these sentences says what a work has at a certain place, and such a check does not hold it against the work at that place.

So each sentence is checked by itself, and each leaves something that can be read afterwards. The sentences in question are those of a record that attribute something to a cited work: that the work states, proves, defines or tabulates something, what its authors call an object, where in it a result stands, how its notation corresponds to the library's. Every citation in brackets marks such a sentence. So does the header key `source`; its sentences are those of the record's part `## Literature` that say where in the work the result stands. A sentence that goes on about the same work without a citation of its own, as the sentence after a citation often does, is such a sentence as well. For each of these sentences, open the work at the place the sentence names and read what stands there; where the sentence names no place, find the place in the work on which it rests. Then enter the sentence under `attributions` in the work's entry of `sources.json`, with the place and with the words of the work at that place: copied exactly, in the language of the work, a formula in the notation of the work, and long enough to show everything that the sentence attributes. The quotation is the one thing you write that is not put into English. For a work of which you opened the abstract only, the place is the abstract, and the words are words of the abstract. Where the words do not show what the sentence says, the sentence is wrong. Correct it from the words, by the rule above, and enter the sentence as you corrected it. Where the work, opened and searched, does not have what the sentence says at any place, the sentence loses the citation and what it said of the work, as for a work that could not be opened. A sentence that says what a work does not contain cannot be shown by words of the work. Enter it as well, with the place that was read for it and the words there that show what the work has in its stead; the sentence stands as far as that reading reaches, and says so, for example "the sections that were read do not treat".

The words are copied, and not summarised, because a summary can carry the same mistake as the sentence that it is meant to check. They are also kept for a later use. The paper is written from the records, and it says of a work only what was read. A later session compares every sentence of the paper about a cited work with these quotations, and a sentence that no quotation supports is reworded or taken out of the paper.

## The requests

For each entry of `requests.json`, read the Lean statement of the declaration and its record, and search for a work that states the result. The name under `result` is where the search begins; a classical result often stands in a textbook or a survey as well as in the paper it is named after, and either will do.

A source is entered into the record only when it states the result in the form the library has: the same hypotheses and the same conclusion, up to notation and to conventions that can be stated exactly, such as an index that starts at one. A source that proves a weaker form, a non-strict inequality where the library has a strict one, a special case, or a general theorem from which the library's statement would still have to be derived, does not state it in that form. The paper will put the citation in the place of a proof, and it can do that only for what the source actually says. You have to have seen the statement in the source, so a work you could not open far enough to read the statement is not entered.

When you find such a source: add its entry to `bibliography.bib` under a new key in the manner of the existing keys, with its identifier; add the header key `source` to the record; and write the record's part `## Literature`: the precise place of the result in the source, how much of the source you read, where the statement stands in what you read (a main theorem with its proof, a remark, a result the source attributes to earlier work), and how the source's notation and conventions correspond to the library's. The new key is listed in `sources.json` like every other.

When you find none, the answer is that none was found. Say in the note what you searched and, where you found something close, what it is and in what it differs from the library's statement. The paper will then prove the result.

One case in which the source does not state the library's form is reported, because the library can still be changed when you search: a source that states a stronger or a more general form, from which the library's statement follows. You do not enter it, since it does not state the library's form. But a later step tries to give the library the source's form, as a declaration of its own that is proved; where that succeeds, the paper cites the source for that declaration and obtains the library's present form from it. Without your report the paper proves at length what a cited theorem contains. Report such a source under `stronger`. Give under `states` the statement of the source in full, in the notation and the conventions of the library, with every hypothesis and every constant as the source has them: the session that proves it works from your words and does not open the source. Give under `where` its place in the source. Add the source's entry to `bibliography.bib` and list its key in `sources.json`, as for a source you enter.

A second case is reported as well, and it asks for no change to the library. Some results are an instance of a general theorem of another kind than the library's statement: an expansion in a series that follows from the Lagrange inversion formula for one choice of the function, a bound that a cited estimate gives at one value of its parameter. What separates this case from the one before is whether the library could be given the source's statement by the means it has. A source whose statement is the library's own with a better constant, a wider range or a weaker hypothesis is reported under `stronger`, since a session can try to prove that form from the proof the library has. A theorem that would need a theory which the library does not contain is reported under `instance`. To prove such a theorem in the library would be work on mathematics that the proof of the root does not use. But the paper should not derive at length what a theorem that it can cite gives in a line. Report such a theorem under `instance`: its `key`; what it `states`, in full, in the notation and the conventions of the library; `where` it stands in the work; and the `specialisation`, that is, the choice of the data of the theorem and the steps by which the library's statement follows from the theorem. The paper then states the result in the form the library has, cites the theorem, and gives the specialisation as its proof. A writer who has the theorem in your words, and not the work, turns the specialisation into that proof, so write it completely, and check each of its steps before you report it: the library proves the statement, so the statement is true, but nobody after you checks the way from the theorem to it.

Enter the theorem into the record as well, in its part `## Literature`: that the result is an instance of the theorem, with the citation in brackets, and the specialisation. The record receives no header key `source`, because the work does not state the form the library has. Add the entry of the work to `bibliography.bib` and list its key in `sources.json`, with the attribution of the sentence that you wrote, as for every sentence that cites a work.

`stronger` and `instance` can be reported together, for the same work or for two, when a source states a form that the library may be brought to and the library's statement also follows from a cited theorem by a specialisation. The record's `## Literature` is then written for the instance, as just described. Where the library is not brought to the stronger form, the paper proves the result from the theorem by the specialisation.

The other direction stays closed. A source that states less than the library is not reported, and no record is given a source for a form that the library does not prove.

A request that carries `source` is the second half of such a report. Its declaration was added to the library so that the library states what that source states. Open the source again and compare it with the declaration as it now stands in `lean/`. Where the source states exactly this form, enter it as described above and list its key in `sources.json`. Where the declaration says something else after all, the answer is that none was found, and the note says in what the two differ.

## What you write

- `output/sources.json`, with one entry for each key of `cited.json` and for each key you added:

```json
[{"key": "abel1826", "status": "verified", "identifier": "...", "opened": "full", "note": "...",
  "attributions": [{"declaration": "Lib.abel", "sentence": "...", "place": "p. 159", "quote": "..."}]}]
```

`status` is `verified` when the work was found in a registry and `removed` when it was not. `identifier` is the identifier that you took from the registry's entry, empty for a removed work. `opened` is `full`, `parts`, `abstract` or `none`. `note` says what was read and what was corrected in the entry and in the records, or, for a removal, where you searched. `attributions` holds what the section "What is checked, sentence by sentence" describes, one entry for each sentence of a record that attributes something to the work: `declaration` is the declaration whose record has the sentence; `sentence` is the sentence exactly as the record that you return has it, from its first word to its full stop and with its formulas, since the program finds it in the record by its words; line breaks and spaces do not count. `place` is the place in the work; `quote` is the words of the work at that place. A sentence that cites two works is entered under each. The program requires an entry whose sentence contains the citation for every citation in brackets of the work, and at least one entry for every record whose header names the work. The array is empty in three cases: for a work that is removed, for a work that is opened as `none`, and for a work that no record cites, which is the case of a work reported under `stronger` alone.

- `output/bibliography.bib`: the complete bibliography as it now stands.
- `output/records/<path>.md` for each record you changed: the complete record, at its path in `records/`. A changed record keeps the form that all records have, which a program checks: a header between two lines `---` whose lines are `key: value`, then parts under headings of the second level, of which `## Literature` is one. Add no other heading of the second level and no other header key than `source`.
- `output/requests.json`, with one entry for each entry of the `requests.json` you were given, an empty array if that was empty:

```json
[{"declaration": "Lib.abel", "found": true, "key": "abel1826", "note": "...", "stronger": null, "instance": null}]
```

`key` is the key of the source you entered, `null` when `found` is false. `stronger` is `null`, or, for a source that states a stronger or a more general form than the library, `{"key": "...", "states": "...", "where": "..."}`; `found` is then false. `instance` is `null`, or, for a general theorem of which the library's statement is an instance, `{"key": "...", "states": "...", "where": "...", "specialisation": "..."}`; `found` is then false as well.

The program requires that every key a record cites, in its header or in brackets, is in the bibliography and has the status `verified`. Before you end, search the records you return, and those you left, for the keys you removed and for the keys of the works that you found and could not open, and see that none is cited any more.
