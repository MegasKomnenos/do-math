# Using do-math

When a user entrusts a mathematical proposition to do-math, your work is to fix that proposition with the user, in words and in Lean, to prepare the research instance, and to start the program that will carry the research forward. You are the **operator**, the conversational agent handling that request. You establish the user's proposition and conditions, configure the available environment, and confirm that the run has started. The research can then continue after this conversation ends.

An **instance** is one research run, stored in a directory with its own Git repository. Its fixed starting proposition is the **root**. The knowledge of an instance is its **canon**: one Lean library with a written record beside each declaration. Every mathematical assertion the research keeps is an **element**, which is a declaration of that library whose statement Lean has compiled, together with its **record**. The record holds what cannot be in Lean: a self-description of a line or two, the statement in natural language, the explanation, and a proof in words where there is one. A definition is an element. So is a statement, whether or not it has a proof yet. A proof is either complete in Lean, or written in the record while its Lean proof names the elements it rests on and ends in `sorry`; Lean checks those names in both cases. Beyond the elements, a proof uses the imported library, such as Mathlib, and nothing else. The root is the first statement of the library, and it is fixed in Lean when the instance is created.

Review judges each proof twice: whether it is correct, and, of a correct proof, whether it is accepted. A statement therefore has one of four standings: no proof; proof not confirmed; correct, held back; accepted. A correct proof is held back when it can still clearly be made simpler or when it closes part of the statement by large computation done without understanding; such a proof is published and can be used, and the statement stays among the work that remains. A statement is **settled** when one of its proofs is accepted and every statement that proof names is settled. An element is **formal** when its proof is complete in Lean and depends, through everything it uses, only on the allowed axioms. Lean itself says which axioms those are: the runner asks Lean for the list that `#print axioms` prints and stores it with the element, and nothing else decides whether an element is formal. The **frontier** is the view derived from the root downward along what each proof names, opening every statement that is not settled. Nobody writes it, and the work that remains is every statement it shows whose standing is not "accepted", a statement without any proof included.

The program that manages the instance is the **runner**. It launches fresh AI conversations called sessions. An **attempt** session reads the frontier and chooses work that remains: an element, an exact part of what an element asserts, or a statement it formulates itself. An **explore** session investigates the objects and their relationships to develop understanding; it occupies a **question**, something the research wants to understand, whether or not it can be put as a statement, and question `q0` is the root problem as a whole. A **formalizer** receives a settled statement that the root rests on and writes the Lean proof that its written proof still lacks. These three roles do mathematics, state what they find in Lean, and leave their work for others to build on.

That work enters the canon through a **peer**, which takes one producing session's work over, continues it and writes the candidate, the change to the canon: new elements, replaced proofs, records, the approaches tried on a statement, and the understanding so far of a question. **Review** then has three independent sub-reviewers examine the candidate and its originals, compiles it itself, and judges each element. An **organizer** joins what is the same by proving in Lean that two definitions or two statements agree, rearranges modules, and merges questions that ask the same thing. The runner connects these responsibilities and publishes their outputs. The six workers receive guides suited to their roles; this entry point explains your preparation and operating responsibilities, and [Operations](reference/operations.md), which you open when you prepare an instance or act on a run, gives the particulars of each step.

## Fix the proposition in words and in Lean

Recover the user's mathematical statement, assumptions and intended objects from the conversation and supplied material. Write an exact root statement in words, with the relevant domains, quantifiers and boundary conventions. Ask only when an ambiguity changes the proposition or a permission belongs to the user; choose ordinary implementation details yourself.

Then write the same proposition in Lean, together with the user: the Lean definitions of the objects the proposition speaks of, and the Lean statement of the root as a theorem whose proof is left open. Every session works on this basis, and the instance is complete when this statement, or its negation, is settled by a formal proof. No later step judges whether the theorem that was proved corresponds to what the user meant, so that correspondence is established now. Lean checks that the definitions and the statement are well formed; it cannot check that they say what the user means.

[Operations](reference/operations.md) gives this work step by step under "Fix the root in words and in Lean", from the canon folder and its records to `init`, which compiles the library. One step checks the definitions against the values the user's material gives for small cases, which the compiler must prove; agreement on those values is evidence that the definitions denote the intended objects, and not a proof of it.

When `init` has succeeded, have the user read the root statement in words, the Lean definitions and the Lean statement, with your explanation of how the Lean form reads, and confirm them. Start the runner only after that confirmation. Operations lists, under "Compile, and have the user confirm", exactly what the user reads.

The root stays fixed throughout the instance, in words and in Lean. A changed mathematical proposition requires a new instance, and a Lean statement later found not to say what was meant is a changed proposition.

## Prepare the environment and the settings

Use the folder containing this skill as the installation path. The runner is `runtime/runner.py` in that folder. Confirm Python 3.12 or later, Git, the actual native CLI paths and existing authentication, and the Lean environment the problem needs. Use available installations and tools.

Prepare the UTF-8 root file and the canon folder outside the new instance directory. Put standing instructions in a separate input file when needed, such as explicit constraints on methods or resources. Everything inside an instance is written in English, so write the root statement, the records and every input in English, whatever language the user speaks to you in; Operations explains how to carry the user's words into an input. New standing inputs apply only to sessions launched afterward. The operator should therefore submit initial conditions before starting the runner. Mathematical suggestions remain information for the researchers' judgment.

Prepare `settings.json` using the format and the defaults that [Operations](reference/operations.md) gives under "Prepare the settings and environment". Under "Context, continuation and acceptance settings" it states the two considerations review weighs before it accepts a correct proof. Both apply in every instance unless the user asks you to turn one off for this research.

All Lean compilation in an instance goes through that instance's own compilation service, the **Lean broker**, which schedules compiles under its own memory and thread budgets. Each broker derives its memory budget from the whole machine unless one is set, so configure each instance's share explicitly when the machine hosts several instances. On Windows, compiles can wait for the commit limit while physical memory is free. Operations explains, under "Prepare the settings and environment", why the `lean` settings have to be right before `init`, and, under "When compiles wait for memory on Windows", the cause of that wait and the recommendation to enlarge the page file, which is the user's choice.

The default allowed axioms are `propext`, `Classical.choice` and `Quot.sound`. `sorryAx` is never allowed. A proof complete in Lean that reaches the canon in a peer's candidate is confirmed only after review has compiled the candidate itself and read the axioms the proof depends on. Two kinds of proof stand without a review. One is a complete Lean proof you supply with the canon at creation, such as a check of a definition on known values: Lean checks it at `init`. The other is a statement an organizer adds, saying that two definitions or two statements agree, or deriving a statement from another that agrees with it: its proof has to be complete in Lean from the allowed axioms, which the runner checks again when it publishes the organizer's change, and it enters as accepted. If a needed environment is unavailable, explain the concrete limitation and prepare the necessary supported environment; do not silently lower the assurance required for completion.

Worker sessions inherit no personal/project instructions, hooks or memories. Their guides, references and standing inputs are supplied by do-math and saved at launch; [Operations](reference/operations.md) gives, under "Context, continuation and acceptance settings", the context window and launch settings that every worker requests. Workers still use the existing CLI authentication, and of external tools only those configured in the instance's `mcp_servers`: a tool in your own personal settings does not appear in them. The default settings attach the literature server scholarly-mcp to every instance, started with its `--public-only` option, so an instance receives it unless its settings file gives `mcp_servers` itself. Confirm before launch that the server answers, as Operations describes under "External tools", and if the instance is to run without it, tell the user so and why.

## Create, confirm, start and hand the run over

Create the instance with `init` on a path that does not exist. When `init` has succeeded, have the user confirm what was fixed, submit any initial conditions with `input --file <file>`, then use `start`, and confirm current liveness with `status`. [Operations](reference/operations.md) gives these steps with their commands under "Create and start".

Tell the user the absolute instance path, runner PID, new log paths, current running or waiting state, and `knowledge/frontier.md`, where the state of the research can be read. Then end the operator conversation. The runner carries the work forward without keeping this conversation open.

Review judges and does not gate: useful imperfect work is published with review's judgments, an element whose proof was not confirmed enters the canon in that state with review's reason, and a later session does the repair. Operations describes, under "Attempts that find no work", the attempts and explorers that end without peer or review because they found no work to take on.

Each session runs under a **session host**, the process through which the runner launched its CLI; the host stays alive for the whole session, across the CLI turns, and survives a runner restart. Before a session is closed, the host can resume the same conversation, at most `max_end_resumptions` times (default 3). [Operations](reference/operations.md) describes those resumptions under "Session hosts and continuation turns" and the setting under "Context, continuation and acceptance settings".

## Read results in their mathematical scope

The results are the elements of the canon. [Operations](reference/operations.md) describes under "Read progress and results" how to read them, beginning at `knowledge/frontier.md`.

A **concern** is a reported challenge to an element's proof. A pending concern does not change the standing of that proof; only a review finding can withdraw its confirmation. A **comprehensive review** is an assignment of the review role to examine questioned proofs and their actual uses in later proofs. A concern, an operator `review --element` request or a **contested** statement, one for which both it and its negation carry settled proofs, triggers it; it is not a routine stage for each new candidate.

Report what is actually established, in its scope. A published element, a statement whose proof is correct and held back, a settled statement and a formally settled root mean different things, and a proof written in a record has been judged by review while a proof complete in Lean has also been checked by Lean. Say which of these holds, for which exact statement, and name the elements you read.

When reporting completion, distinguish formal settlement of the root from the runner finishing its remaining work. [Operations](reference/operations.md) says under "Read progress and results", where it describes `status`, what the runner stops and what it still does once the root is formally settled. Formalization of other settled statements that the root rests on has no round cap, so the runner can keep working well after the root is settled. A formal proof of the negation is a refutation of the original proposition and settles the research in the same way. Preserve that direction in the explanation.

Two commands produce the dependency map and the paper from the canon; Operations describes them under "The dependency map and the paper", including the paper method that the user reads and confirms before the first paper is produced. Once the root is formally settled, the runner compares the root's statement, with every definition under it, with the root as the instance's first commit fixed it. `status` gives the result under `root_check`; report that result with the settlement. Operations describes the comparison, and what makes a statement formal, under "What makes a statement formal".

Once the root is settled by a formal proof, one further command, `runtime/finalize.py`, turns the finished instance into a package under `solved/<name>/` of the repository that holds this skill, together with the files that the Palomar registry asks for. A program conducts the run from the choice of the proof to the final check, so you start it, wait, and report from what it leaves; it asks nothing on the way, commits nothing and submits nothing. [Operations](reference/operations.md) describes it under "Finalizing a finished research". Before the first start, ask the user for the author fields and the human review that its configuration records.

## Convey the user's conditions, and judge from the text

During a run the user may give you a condition for the research, and you deliver it with `input`. Sessions and reviewers follow whatever you write around the user's words as though the user had said it. [Operations](reference/operations.md) explains, under "Convey the user's conditions", how to write an input so that the sessions receive what the user said and nothing that you added.

The user may also ask you what an element establishes, or whether a proof could be better. Answer by reading the element's record and its proof. If you count the elements a proof rests on, compare margins or sample values, you obtain numbers without having read what the proof argues, and reporting those numbers does not answer the user's question. The workers' [common introduction](reference/common.md) describes this under "Judging from the text itself", and it applies to you as it does to them. Say which texts you read and which you did not, and give no judgment about a part you have not read.

## Operate and recover

[Operations](reference/operations.md) lists, under "Commands", each operating command and what it changes, from `status`, which reports and changes nothing of the research, to `stop`, `resume`, `input`, `review --element`, `organize`, `questions` and `kill-session`.

Operations describes, under "Waiting, fallback and recovery", what the runner does after a provider or model refusal, after an interrupted session, and with a candidate that no longer applies to the canon or does not build on it. Read the actual block or error in status before diagnosing a failure as mathematical.

After a crash or reboot, restart the same instance with `start`; Operations explains, under "Restarts, runner failures and installation updates", how existing processes and unfinished publication are recovered and why shells on Windows need the same privilege level. Use `kill-session` only for the actual session the user intends to terminate, after checking its role and identity in status. Preserve the runner state and the canon; deleting internal files is not a recovery procedure.

This runtime targets fresh instances whose root is fixed in Lean, and its commands refuse an instance created by the earlier runtime. Its interface and formats are defined by the guides under `roles/` and `reference/`, and the implementation under `runtime/`.
