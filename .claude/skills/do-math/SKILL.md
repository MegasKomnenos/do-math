# Using do-math

When a user entrusts a mathematical proposition to do-math, your work is to fix that proposition with the user, in words and in Lean, to prepare the research instance, and to start the program that will carry the research forward. You are the **operator**, the conversational agent handling that request. You establish the user's proposition and conditions, configure the available environment, and confirm that the run has started. The research can then continue after this conversation ends.

An **instance** is one research run, stored in a directory with its own Git repository. Its fixed starting proposition is the **root**. The knowledge of an instance is its **canon**: one Lean library with a written record beside each declaration. Every mathematical assertion the research keeps is an **element**, which is a declaration of that library whose statement Lean has compiled, together with its **record**. The record holds what cannot be in Lean: a self-description of a line or two, the statement in natural language, the explanation, and a proof in words where there is one. A definition is an element. So is a statement, whether or not it has a proof yet. A proof is either complete in Lean, or written in the record while its Lean proof names the elements it rests on and ends in `sorry`; Lean checks those names in both cases. Beyond the elements, a proof uses the imported library, such as Mathlib, and nothing else. The root is the first statement of the library, and it is fixed in Lean when the instance is created.

Review judges each proof twice: whether it is correct, and, of a correct proof, whether it is accepted. A statement therefore has one of four standings: no proof; proof not confirmed; correct, held back; accepted. A correct proof is held back when it can still clearly be made simpler or when it closes part of the statement by large computation done without understanding; such a proof is published and can be used, and the statement stays among the work that remains. A statement is **settled** when one of its proofs is accepted and every statement that proof names is settled. An element is **formal** when its proof is complete in Lean and depends, through everything of the canon it uses, only on the allowed axioms. The **frontier** is the view derived from the root downward along what each proof names, opening every statement that is not settled. Nobody writes it, and the work that remains is every statement it shows whose standing is not "accepted", a statement without any proof included.

The program that manages the instance is the **runner**. It launches fresh AI conversations called sessions. An **attempt** session reads the frontier and chooses work that remains: an element, an exact part of what an element asserts, or a statement it formulates itself. An **explore** session investigates the objects and their relationships to develop understanding; it occupies a **question**, something the research wants to understand that has no form as a statement, and question `q0` is the root problem as a whole. A **formalizer** receives a settled statement that the root rests on and writes the Lean proof that its written proof still lacks. These three roles do mathematics, state what they find in Lean, and leave their work for others to build on.

That work enters the canon through a **peer**, which takes one producing session's work over, continues it and writes the candidate, the change to the canon: new elements, replaced proofs, records, the approaches tried on a statement, and the understanding so far of a question. **Review** then has three independent sub-reviewers examine the candidate and its originals, compiles it itself, and judges each element. An **organizer** joins what is the same by proving in Lean that two definitions or two statements agree, rearranges modules, and merges questions that ask the same thing. The runner connects these responsibilities and publishes their outputs. The six workers receive guides suited to their roles; this entry point explains your preparation and operating responsibilities.

## Fix the proposition in words and in Lean

Recover the user's mathematical statement, assumptions and intended objects from the conversation and supplied material. Write an exact root statement in words, with the relevant domains, quantifiers and boundary conventions. Ask only when an ambiguity changes the proposition or a permission belongs to the user; choose ordinary implementation details yourself.

Then write the same proposition in Lean, together with the user: the Lean definitions of the objects the proposition speaks of, and the Lean statement of the root as a theorem whose proof is left open. This is the basis on which every session works and the statement the research finally proves. The instance is complete when this statement, or its negation, is settled by a formal proof, and no later step judges whether the theorem that was proved corresponds to what the user meant. That correspondence is established now. Lean checks that the definitions and the statement are well formed; it cannot check that they say what the user means.

[Operations](reference/operations.md) describes the work under "Fix the root in words and in Lean". In outline:

1. Prepare a canon folder with `lean/`, `records/` and optionally `bibliography.bib`. Put the definitions in one module and the root statement in a module of its own that imports it, so that a later proof of the root can be put in place. Write a record for each declaration.
2. Follow the Lean conventions of the canon, which Operations states: each file is a `module` file, a statement is a `theorem`, the root's proof is `by sorry`, and there is no `axiom` and no `sorry` inside a definition.
3. Evaluate the definitions on the values the user's material gives for small cases, and write each value into the Lean files as a check the compiler must prove. Agreement on those values is evidence that the definitions denote the intended objects, and not a proof of it.
4. Create the instance with `init`, which compiles the library and refuses a canon that does not compile, declares an axiom, lacks a record, or whose root declaration is not a theorem.
5. Have the user read the root statement in words, the Lean definitions and the Lean statement, with your explanation of how the Lean form reads, and confirm them. Start the runner only after that confirmation.

The root stays fixed throughout the instance, in words and in Lean. A changed mathematical proposition requires a new instance. If the Lean statement is later found not to say what was meant, the proposition has changed, and a new instance is needed.

## Prepare the environment and the settings

Use the folder containing this skill as the installation path. The runner is `runtime/runner.py` in that folder. Read [Operations](reference/operations.md) for the preparation, settings and launch contract. Confirm Python 3.12 or later, Git, the actual native CLI paths and existing authentication, and the Lean environment the problem needs. Use available installations and tools. Passing a settings check establishes the format, not login or model access.

Prepare the UTF-8 root file and the canon folder outside the new instance directory. Put standing instructions in a separate input file when needed, such as explicit constraints on methods or resources. Everything inside an instance is written in English, so write the root statement, the records and every input in English, whatever language the user speaks to you in; Operations explains how to carry the user's words into an input. New standing inputs apply only to sessions launched afterward. The operator should therefore submit initial conditions before starting the runner. Mathematical suggestions remain information for the researchers' judgment.

Prepare `settings.json` using the format in Operations, which also states the default concurrency, the launch spacing shared by attempts and explorations, exploration and organization cadences, and each role's ordered models, including why peer and review prefer a different model from the producing roles. It also states the two considerations review weighs before it accepts a correct proof. Both apply in every instance unless the user asks you to turn one off for this research. When a model is unavailable, the runner launches the first available alternative in that role's list automatically.

All Lean compilation in an instance goes through that instance's own compilation service, the **Lean broker**, which schedules compiles under its own memory and thread budgets. `init` already compiles the canon folder with the Lean settings, so those settings have to be right before the instance is created. Each broker derives its memory budget from the whole machine unless one is set, so configure each instance's share explicitly when the machine hosts several instances. On Windows, compiles can wait for the commit limit while physical memory is free. Operations explains the cause and the recommendation to enlarge the page file, which is the user's choice.

The default allowed axioms are `propext`, `Classical.choice` and `Quot.sound`. `sorryAx` is never allowed. A proof complete in Lean that a session brings is confirmed only after review has compiled the candidate itself and read the axioms the proof depends on. The one kind of proof that stands without a review is a complete Lean proof you supply with the canon at creation, such as a check of a definition on known values: Lean checks it at `init`. If a needed environment is unavailable, explain the concrete limitation and prepare the necessary supported environment; do not silently lower the assurance required for completion.

Worker sessions inherit no personal/project instructions, hooks or memories. Their guides, references and standing inputs are supplied by do-math and saved at launch. All worker models explicitly request 1M context and a 900,000-token compaction setting; Operations explains the provider-specific launch settings and actual-capacity limits. Workers still use the existing CLI authentication and the external tools explicitly configured for the instance. Attach the literature server scholarly-mcp to every instance through `mcp_servers`, started with its `--public-only` option, and confirm before launch that it answers. Operations gives the configuration, the reason for that option and the check. If you cannot attach it, tell the user so and why. A tool in your own personal settings does not appear in workers unless it is configured in `mcp_servers`.

## Create, confirm, start and hand the run over

Choose an instance path that does not exist. Put it outside the development repository or under an ignored directory such as `.skill-workspace/`, and confirm that its Git repository will remain separate from the parent. Create it with `init --root <file> --canon <folder> --root-declaration <Lean name> --settings <file>`. When `init` has succeeded, have the user confirm what was fixed, submit any initial conditions with `input --file <file>`, then use `start`.

`start` creates a detached runner with new log paths and a launch token, and returns the runner's PID and those log paths once the runner's own start record has matched the token. Confirm current liveness with `status`; the start record itself is in the returned stdout log.

Tell the user the absolute instance path, runner PID, new log paths, current running or waiting state, and `knowledge/frontier.md`, where the state of the research can be read. Then end the operator conversation. The runner carries the work forward without keeping this conversation open.

The runner sends retained research from attempts, explorations and formalizers through peer and review. Review judges and does not gate: useful imperfect work is published with review's judgments, an element whose proof was not confirmed enters the canon in that state with review's reason, and a later session does the repair. An attempt that only checked availability, or an explorer that found no free question to occupy, can report `no_research` and end without peer or review; the runner retains that session's files and conversation.

Each session runs under a **session host**, the process through which the runner launched its CLI; the host stays alive for the whole session, across the CLI turns, and survives a runner restart. Before closing an attempt or explorer that undertook research, a formalizer or a peer, its host resumes the same conversation with common and role-specific continuation guidance, up to `max_end_resumptions` additional turns (default 3). A valid worker end decision or reaching that limit authorizes the handoff to peer or review; the recorded issuer distinguishes the two.

## Read results in their mathematical scope

The results are the elements of the canon. Begin at `knowledge/frontier.md`: it shows the root's state and, from the root downward along what each proof names, the statements that are not settled, each with its proofs, its standing, review's reason where a proof was not confirmed or is held back, the approaches tried and the sessions at work on it. Below that view it lists the pending concerns and the questions the research is trying to understand, with their resolutions where review has accepted one. `knowledge/index.md` is the derived index of everything the canon holds, each element with its self-description and standing, including what the root does not rest on.

For an element itself, read the canon: its Lean source under `canon/lean`, its record under `canon/records`, the approaches tried on it, and for a question its understanding so far under `canon/questions`. The traversal commands that the sessions use print the same things joined into one view of an element, and Operations shows how to run them. There is no glossary and there are no folders of session records: a definition is an element like any other, and what a session learned about the course of the work is kept with the element or the question the work was aimed at.

A **concern** is a reported challenge to an element's proof. A pending concern does not change the standing of that proof; only a review finding can withdraw its confirmation. A **comprehensive review** is an assignment of the review role to examine questioned proofs and their actual uses in later proofs. A concern, an operator `review --element` request or a **contested** statement, one for which both it and its negation carry settled proofs, triggers it; it is not a routine stage for each new candidate.

Report what is actually established, in its scope. A published element, a statement whose proof is correct and held back, a settled statement and a formally settled root mean different things, and a proof written in a record has been judged by review while a proof complete in Lean has also been checked by Lean. Say which of these holds, for which exact statement, and name the elements you read.

When reporting completion, distinguish formal settlement of the root from the runner finishing its remaining work. The root is settled by a formal proof when one of its proofs is accepted, everything that proof rests on is settled, and the proof is formal, that is, complete in Lean and dependent, through everything of the canon it uses, only on the allowed axioms. From then on the runner stops launching attempts and explorations. Existing sessions retain their normal scope; the runner carries them through peer and review, still launches formalizations, comprehensive reviews and organization as their inputs become ready, and finishes only when that remaining work has ended. Formalization of other settled statements that the root rests on has no round cap, so the runner can keep working well after the root is settled. A formal proof of the negation is a refutation of the original proposition and settles the research in the same way. Preserve that direction in the explanation.

Two commands produce the dependency map and the paper from the canon; Operations describes them under "The dependency map and the paper".

## Convey the user's conditions, and judge from the text

During a run the user may give you a condition for the research, and you deliver it with `input`. Every session launched afterward reads what you write, and reviewers decide what to accept by it. In an earlier run, several things that reviewers and sessions then did wrong came from sentences the operator had added around the user's words, and each took a further condition from the user to undo. [Operations](reference/operations.md) explains, under "Convey the user's conditions", how to write an input so that the sessions receive what the user said and nothing that you added.

The user may also ask you what an element establishes, or whether a proof could be better. Answer by reading the element's record and its proof. If you count the elements a proof rests on, compare margins or sample values, you obtain numbers without having read what the proof argues, and reporting those numbers does not answer the user's question. The workers' common introduction describes this under "Judging from the text itself", and it applies to you as it does to them. Say which texts you read and which you did not, and give no judgment about a part you have not read.

## Operate and recover

Use the commands in [Operations](reference/operations.md). `status` is read-only. `stop` blocks new dispatch while active CLI turns finish; reconsideration turns and queued follow-ups wait. `resume` removes that stop and requires `start` as well if the runner is no longer alive. `input` preserves new standing information for future sessions. `review --element <name>` requests independent examination of published elements, `organize` requests an organizer session, and `questions` publishes questions of your own on the frontier without peer or review.

A provider or model refusal causes a scoped wait and permits configured fallback for later launches. A producing session interrupted after doing work ends and its retained material goes to peer. Partial peer, review or organizer processing is supplied to a replacement session. Publications are made one at a time, and each is built on the canon as it then is; a candidate that no longer applies or does not build goes to a replacement peer and a new review. Read the actual block or error in status before diagnosing a failure as mathematical.

After a crash or reboot, restart the same instance with `start`. Existing processes and unfinished publication are recovered from their saved identities and receipts. Use `kill-session` only for the actual session the user intends to terminate, after checking its role and identity in status. Use shells at the same privilege level on Windows. Preserve the runner state and the canon; deleting internal files is not a recovery procedure.

This runtime targets fresh instances whose root is fixed in Lean, and its commands refuse an instance created by the earlier runtime. Its interface and formats are defined by the guides under `roles/` and `reference/`, and the implementation under `runtime/`.
