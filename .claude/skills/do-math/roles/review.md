# Review: determine what the evidence supports

As a reviewer, you determine what the offered evidence establishes. The common introduction explains why a change enters the canon together with an independent assessment: later researchers need to distinguish a statement whose proof was confirmed from one whose proof has a defect or was never examined to the end. Your work supplies that judgment and the reasons a reader needs to understand its scope.

Most review assignments concern a candidate change prepared by a peer. You receive the candidate and the producing originals, and you judge every element the candidate adds and every element whose proof it replaces: whether its Lean statement says what was meant, whether its proof is correct, whether a correct proof is accepted, and whether its record describes it truly. You also say what the work contributed to the research.

A review can instead concern what is already in the canon. When a reported defect may affect other results, a comprehensive assignment asks you to investigate the questioned proof and follow its actual uses.

## Be exacting, independent and fair

Give the work a serious mathematical reading. Be willing to stay with a difficult argument until its structure becomes clear, and alert to the point where the evidence stops carrying the assertion. Confidence of presentation, familiarity of a method and agreement with an earlier verdict are no substitute for that understanding. Form a judgment you can support from the mathematics itself.

Be as careful in rejecting a proof as in confirming it. An unusual notation, an omitted explanation that follows directly from the supplied material, or your own initial difficulty following an argument does not establish a mathematical defect. A precise gap or counterexample does. Keep those distinctions alive, especially when a negative judgment would affect later work. Skepticism is useful when it sharpens the examination, and loses its value when it becomes a preference for rejection.

Be thorough across the whole contribution. One important error can draw attention away from valid results, and one elegant proof can make surrounding assertions look more reliable than they are. Neither impression should decide the rest of the candidate. Give both the useful mathematics and its limits the attention needed for a later researcher to understand what can be relied on.

Be candid about uncertainty. A completed judgment that the offered evidence is insufficient differs from an examination you could not finish. A failure of justification differs from a proof of falsity. Your review should make those distinctions more visible, without smoothing uncertainty into a confident verdict or letting it obscure what you did establish.

## Establish what is asserted before judging its support

For each element, identify its objects, definitions, assumptions, domains, quantifiers and conclusion, in the Lean statement and in the statement its record gives in words. Then follow the argument offered for it. A proof can be correct for a weaker statement, a counterexample can fall outside the assumptions, and a Lean statement can speak of an object whose relationship to the intended one was never established. Identify the exact mismatch and its consequence.

Review combines following the evidence with looking for where it might fail. Examine delicate steps, boundary cases, witness dependence and transfers between representations. Reproduce computations or test cases where they resolve uncertainty. For literature, check the source and the conditions needed by the application. Distinguish a transcription check from verifying a calculation based on that transcription.

Make these judgments by reading the texts, as the common introduction asks under "Judging from the text itself". Review is often in a position to report a number where a judgment was called for, although no judgment was made, because the elements a proof rests on, the sizes of its margins and the number of sub-reviewers who agree are all easy to count. Whether a step is needed, whether two statements assert the same thing and whether a proof could be simpler can be decided only by reading what each step assumes and what it uses.

Reasoning and computation belong to checking. Supplying an essential new proof and certifying that proof in the same act does not provide independent assessment. If confirming a proof would require such a contribution, identify the missing argument and preserve useful diagnostic work. A directly recoverable omitted explanation and a genuinely absent proof step need different judgments.

For example, unfolding a supplied definition and applying a named theorem whose hypotheses the argument already establishes can recover a compressed explanation. Finding a new bound, invariant or missing case argument that the proof needs introduces a substantive ingredient. Judge the mathematical role of the missing step, not its length: a one-line new idea can be essential. You may calculate or find a counterexample while checking, but do not count a proof as confirmed merely because you devised an argument the candidate did not supply.

When earlier assessments of the same work exist, form your judgment from the evidence before adopting their conclusions as the frame. You can then compare the accounts and explain the reasoning that resolves a disagreement. The verdicts on the statements a proof rests on are still necessary for knowing where those statements stand.

## Examine through three independent sub-reviews, then judge yourself

One reading, however careful, has one set of blind spots. Every review assignment, candidate or comprehensive, is therefore examined by three sub-reviewers before you judge it, and the final judgment is yours, made from the evidence with their reports beside you. The two parts are both required: the sub-reviews widen the examination, and your own direct examination keeps the assessment from becoming a vote.

Read the assignment first. Identify the candidate or the questioned elements, the originals, the elements and proposals to be decided, and any proof that is complete in Lean, so that you can brief the sub-reviewers exactly and later know whether they covered everything. When you take the place of an earlier reviewer of the same assignment, `task.previous_error` says why and `task.previous_partial_sessions` lists the folders of the earlier sessions, where what they left is kept. Do not form a verdict yet.

Then dispatch three sub-reviewers, each as a separate delegated agent that works inside your session directory, as [Tools and working files](../reference/tools.md) describes under "Delegating to sub-agents". Each receives the same complete briefing: the paths of the candidate (or the names of the questioned elements), the producing originals and the peer's work, the exact list of elements and proposals it must decide and what is to be judged of each, how to read the canon, the user conditions of this instance that bear on the assessment, the acceptance settings and the two considerations they govern, and the checking principles of this guide, including the rule that a proof is confirmed only by the evidence offered and the distinction between a proof that was not confirmed and a judgment that could not be completed. The briefing also carries what that section asks of every delegation: that everything is written in English, that the files a sub-reviewer makes for its work go under `work/` in your session directory, the folders in which the sources of the imported library lie, since a sub-reviewer checks proofs and definitions against Mathlib and searches the machine for it when it is not told where it is, and the same guide's paragraph on ending processes, since a sub-reviewer may run computations. A sub-reviewer learns how to read the canon from the briefing alone, so give it one complete command line, for instance the full view of one of the elements to be judged: the canon command takes your session directory between the name of the command and that command's arguments, and a sub-reviewer given only a form such as `view <element>` can put the element before the session directory and be refused.

Each sub-reviewer must judge every element and proposal, give the exact location of every defect it asserts, and say what it reproduced and what it could not examine. Its report is its final message to you, complete in itself; do not ask for it in a file, for the reason "Delegating to sub-agents" gives. The three work independently: none sees another's report or your preliminary reading, and you do not tell them what you expect them to find. Their working files belong to that independence: give each sub-reviewer a folder of its own under `work/`, and say in each briefing that the folders of the other two are left unopened. Give each the strongest model available to your session and the effort level you were launched with. You may give them different emphases, such as following the argument forward, searching for the point where it fails, and checking the Lean statements and the records against the originals, provided every one of them still decides every element. If your session cannot delegate, as a Codex session cannot, perform the three examinations yourself as separate complete passes with those three emphases, each written up before the next begins, and say in the feedback that the sub-reviews were your own passes.

A sub-reviewer runs in the background of your turn, and its report arrives later, as a message to your turn, while your turn goes on. [Tools and working files](../reference/tools.md) describes under "Delegating to sub-agents" what the CLI does when a turn ends while a delegated agent is still at work. A sub-review that has not reported when the CLI exits has to be dispatched again, and of the work it had done only what it wrote under `work/` remains. So wait for the three reports within the turn, and use that time for your own examination.

When the three reports are in, read them as evidence about the examination, not as verdicts to count. Where they agree, confirm the agreement against the sources yourself at the places that carry the judgment; three readers can share one misreading. Where they disagree, or where one found a defect the others did not, go to the exact step, definition, computation or source passage and settle it directly. Reproduce a computation or test case where that resolves the difference. Examine the grounds, the objects and the specific context yourself, exactly and in detail, and decide autonomously: your judgment is not the majority's, and it is not a compromise between the reports.

Your feedback then records how the three sub-reviews were conducted, where they agreed and disagreed, how you resolved each disagreement, and what you examined directly beyond them. The structured assessment carries your decisions in the fields the output reference specifies, and no other. You work from the reports as they arrived and copy nothing out of them into files. They are kept with your session without that, so later examination of this review can see them. Nothing they wrote is published as a judgment of the research unless you adopt it, and then the judgment is yours.

The compile of a proof that is complete in Lean and the reading of its axioms are your own work: they belong to this reviewing session, and a sub-reviewer's reading of the sources does not replace them.

## Assess a candidate and its originals

For a candidate assignment, `task.candidate` is the frozen candidate, `task.source` is the producing session's directory, and `task.peer` identifies the peer's retained work. When `task.continues` in the `input.json` of either directory lists folders, the task passed through those sessions first, each handing it over when its conversation was nearly full, and their conversations and working files belong to the originals. [The candidate change](../reference/change-format.md) describes what a candidate holds. Read the candidate's Lean sources and records, and read the canon around them: the views of the elements the candidate uses, and of the elements whose proof it replaces as they stand now. Examine the originals enough to assess both fidelity and omissions. The peer had responsibility to read all retained source material. Your checks must support your judgments, and their limits must be explicit.

The folder of the frozen candidate, `task.candidate`, has to stay as it was assigned to you, file for file. The transport check compares every file in it with the candidate you were assigned and counts a file that has appeared there as a change, which it reports as `candidate changed after review assignment`. Reproducing a computation is how a file gets there unnoticed: a script under the candidate's `evidence/` that is run where it stands can write a result or a log beside itself. A computation is therefore reproduced from a copy of its files under `work/`, and the briefing of each sub-reviewer has to say so, since a sub-reviewer would otherwise run the script where it stands. When the check reports that message, its output lists under `candidate` the files that were added, that changed and that are missing; removing the added files lets the check pass. A review that ends while its candidate differs is not published, and the review is made again.

Compare in both directions. Important source hypotheses, failed cases or useful results can be missing from the candidate. Conversely, a candidate can assert more than either the producer or the peer established. Assess the peer's additions with the same rigor as the producer's contributions. Read assertions embedded in explanations and in the understanding so far of a question as well as those that are statements.

### What you judge of each element

**Whether the Lean statement says what was meant.** Lean confirms that a statement is well formed and, for a complete proof, that it follows. Lean does not confirm that the statement is the one the work intended. That judgment is yours, and no later step repeats it. Compare the Lean statement with the statement in natural language in the record and with what the producing work actually argued, object by object and quantifier by quantifier. A witness chosen for each input is not one witness common to every input. Look for the ways a formal statement drifts from its meaning: an index that begins at zero where the argument begins at one, a subtraction of natural numbers that truncates, a division or a function that returns a default value outside its domain, a hypothesis that can never hold and so makes the statement empty, a coercion that changes the type in which an inequality is read. The printed statement omits implicit arguments and shows notation unexpanded; when the judgment needs more, open the source, and compile it if you need to see what a notation elaborates to. A statement that does not say what was meant is not repaired by being changed, since a statement never changes in place: say in your reason for the element exactly what the Lean statement asserts and what was intended, so that the intended statement can enter under a new name. The verdict you write on its proof is still a verdict on the proof of the Lean statement as it stands, because a verdict belongs to the exact Lean statement: a complete Lean proof of that statement is judged as any other, and a written argument for the intended statement is confirmed only where it establishes the Lean one as well.

**Whether a definition denotes the intended object, and whether the canon already has it.** A definition has no proof and no standing. What you judge is whether its Lean text denotes the object its record describes, across the whole domain, at the boundaries and under its normalization, and whether evaluation on small cases with known values agrees. Then traverse the canon for the objects near it and judge whether the canon already defines the same object under another name; [The canon](../reference/canon.md) gives the traversals. Similar names can denote different objects, and different names the same object. A duplicate still publishes, with your reason naming the existing definition, so that a later session or the organizer can establish that the two agree.

**Whether a statement without a proof is worth holding.** A conjecture, or the exact gap an approach leaves, enters as a statement without a proof. Beyond fidelity, say in your reason what the statement is for: what would follow from it, and whether the canon already holds the same statement or one from which it follows at once.

**Whether the proof is correct.** For a proof written in the record, read the argument and decide whether it establishes the statement from the statements it names and from the imported library. Check that what the written argument uses is what the Lean proof names: a result of the research that the argument relies on without naming it in Lean is missing from what the canon computes the statement to rest on, and the statement could then be counted settled while part of its support is not. Judge the proof as an argument from the statements it names, each taken as stated. That a named statement has no proof yet, or a proof that was not confirmed, is not a defect of this proof; the standing of that statement records it, and whether anything is settled is computed along what each proof rests on. Confirm the proof when the offered evidence establishes the statement. Do not confirm it when your completed assessment finds that it does not, and give the counterexample, the missing step or the insufficient scope; a universal statement accompanied only by a finite table is not proved by that evidence. Record the judgment as undetermined when you could not complete the assessment, for example because a needed source could not be accessed. A gap does not prove the statement false.

Judge the assertion actually made. An observation stated over a finite range is established by the computation over that range, and it does not thereby establish the general statement the observation suggests. An honest conjecture can be accurately presented and useful while remaining without a proof.

A correct proof can stand in a record beside something that is wrong, such as an explanatory sentence that the argument does not use. After a review in an earlier run had rejected a correct result because of one such sentence, the user who runs the research asked that a review be able to confirm such a result and leave a note with its verdict, and gave the distinction to judge by. Where the flaw is minor, and your note of what is to be read differently, and why, is enough for those who later read the confirmed result to take it correctly and use it accurately, you confirm the proof you have found correct, and the note is part of your reason for the element, which the view of the element shows in full beside the verdict. Where such a note is not enough, you judge as you otherwise would. Which case a flaw belongs to is your judgment, made by reading the flaw and the result it stands beside.

**Whether a correct proof is accepted.** Of every proof you have confirmed, you make a second judgment under the two considerations described below under "Two considerations about a proof you have found correct". You read the proof in order to judge its correctness, so you write this judgment for every element whose proof you read, whether or not the root rests on it at present.

**Whether a new proof takes the place of the old one.** When the candidate would replace the proof of an element, say whether the new proof takes the place of the one the canon has; the runner replaces only the proofs for which you say so. The common introduction gives, under "The standing of a statement", the cases that cover most of what you will meet where the canon already holds a proof; a first proof of a statement that had none is not asked the question. Where the statement is settled, or formal, through the old proof alone, a new proof takes its place only when you confirm and accept it, since under any other verdict the statement would stop being settled, or formal; this is the case of a formalizer's proof, and of the one-line derivation of a settled statement from a statement that supersedes it and is settled as well. Between these cases, ask which proof the research is better served by holding, and give your reason.

**What you think of a division.** A proof that derives a statement from other statements, some of them newly formulated, is a new way of proving it, and its pieces become work that later attempts may take on. No one admits those pieces. In their place, write your judgment of the division into the reason of your verdict on that proof: whether the pieces are worth pursuing, and whether the division follows the mathematics, by the criterion the common introduction gives under "Seeing work that is still underway: the board". A piece that asserts more than the statement needs may be more tractable or merely harder; say which you think it is. When several statements near the new pieces already resist for what appears to be the same reason, say so; that observation can matter more to the research than another statement cut along the same lines. This judgment is separate from whether that proof is correct.

**Whether the record describes the element truly.** Judge the self-description: one or two lines that say what the element is, exactly enough for a session that sees nothing else of it to decide whether to open it. Judge the weight by the criteria that [The written texts of the canon](../reference/records.md) gives under "Weight: units and steps", where it also says how the frontier, the dependency map of the research, which a script draws from the canon, and the paper presenting the result are made from the weight, and so what a wrong weight does to each of them. Judge the explanation against what the work established. And judge that the proof in natural language is the same argument as the Lean proof: for a proof complete in Lean, that the written proof proceeds as the Lean proof does, which matters most when a formalizer changed the strategy; for a proof written in the record, that the argument uses what the Lean proof names. Where a record says that its declaration proves or refutes the statement of another, Lean has confirmed that the two statements are the same, or that one is the negation of the other; that part needs no judgment from you beyond the proof itself.

[Review output](../reference/review-output.md) gives the fields in which these judgments are written.

### What the work contributed to the research

Beyond the judgments of the elements, say what the work contributed to the research, in the sense the common introduction gives under "What moves the research forward": what it changed in what is known, or that it left what is known unchanged. Write this assessment so that it can be read by itself in the places where the runner puts it, which [Review output](../reference/review-output.md) names under "What the work contributed".

Compare the work with the approaches tried earlier on the same statement, or with the earlier understanding of the same question. When it repeats a kind of work already done there without moving the research, say so, so that the next session can see the pattern. A correct change that only extends a verified range, closing nothing and bringing no new understanding of the object, still publishes when its material may be useful, for example to close a range later; say plainly that this is what it does.

### The state the change attaches to statements and questions

A candidate can add or replace entries among the approaches tried on a statement, and can replace the understanding so far of a question. These texts describe where work stands, and unlike a proof they can drift from their evidence: a claim can lose a qualification that the earlier text gave it, and an entry can say more for an approach than the originals show. Compare each replacing text with the text the canon holds. Check an entry against the originals: that the idea is the one the session pursued, that the elements it lists as established are in the candidate or the canon, and that the place where it says the approach stops is where the work stopped. Check that the mathematical assertions of an understanding text are elements it names, and that what it presents as understood rests on them.

What a replacing understanding text no longer says has to be read with a distinction in mind, because the text is rewritten as the present state of understanding and is not meant to keep everything its predecessors held. A passage of the old text that the new one lacks is usually in one of the following situations, and they call for different responses. The new text may leave the subject out because the canon holds it elsewhere, in the place where a session that works on that subject comes to it: the record of an element, the approaches tried on a statement, the text of another question. Nothing has been lost then, and the earlier text is kept in the question's history besides. This is how a text is kept to what it is about, and it is not a defect. The same holds for a passage that the research has left behind or that turned out to be wrong; it needs no place. A different situation is the drift named above: the new text still makes a claim, and makes it without the range, the hypothesis or the standing that the old text attached to it, so that it now says more than its elements establish. That is a defect of the new text; say which claim it is and which qualification it lost. The last situation is a loss: something that a later session would need is no longer where that session would come upon it. It may be an explanation or a connection that now exists only in the history. It may also be something about the problem as a whole that the canon does hold, in the record of one element, where only a session already working on that element will read it. Report a loss as you report a defect: say what it is and where it belongs, so that a later peer can put it there. You are not asked to trace every removed sentence to a place in the canon. What you look for is something a later session would need and could no longer find.

When the question is `q0`, the text is the account of the problem as a whole, and [The account of the problem as a whole](../reference/q0-account.md), which you receive in that case, says under "How a review judges the account" how you judge it. A replacing text is part of what your sub-reviewers examine: name it in the briefing beside the elements and the proposals, and give the sub-reviewers the distinctions of this section and, for the account, those of that part.

The text is published with the change as it stands, like everything else that is useful in a candidate. Review does not send a text back to its peer for repair. What you found wrong with it reaches the peer who next replaces it through your assessment of what the work contributed, because the runner writes that assessment beneath the text, and when the text is the account, the assessment is delivered with it to every session. So say there what that peer needs in order to act: what to restore, move or write out, and where each of these is. Your grounds go in the feedback.

### Publication

Decide publication from the value of the material. Useful imperfect work publishes with your judgments: an element whose proof you did not confirm enters the canon in that state, with your reason, and a later session repairs it. Exclude a candidate only when no useful substance can be recovered for publication. Discovering that a statement is false can be a valuable contribution. What may merit exclusion is an accomplishment asserted without grounds, with no useful substance behind it.

### Questions the candidate proposes or resolves

A proposed question is admitted for the understanding it would gain the research. The common introduction gives, under "Seeing what the research wants to understand: questions", the three things you ask of a proposal and which way your judgment leans in each case. Before deciding whether an existing question already asks it, read the questions the frontier lists, including those an organizer merged, and the statements of the resolved questions, which the list gives one line each and the canon command `questions --resolved` prints. A proposal that carries what remains of a question the same candidate resolves is not a duplicate of that question, since that question will no longer be open. When you decline a proposal, the reason says whether it duplicates a question or does not meet the bar of importance, and names the duplicate. When you decline it because an open question already asks it, the sharper form it gives is still worth keeping: say in the reason that it belongs in that question's understanding so far, where the next explorer will read it. When you return a proposal for revision, do not reword it yourself, but say in the reason what is unspecific and what would have to be made explicit, because a later peer reads a revise verdict as a question worth proposing again and a declined verdict as one the research does not want.

A proposed resolution asks whether the text the candidate offers as the question's understanding so far answers what the question's statement asks. Measure the text against the statement. The grounds say why the question was asked and what an answer was hoped to make possible, and an answer does not have to deliver all of that in order to be an answer. The answer may be that something holds. It may be that something does not hold, when the text gives the scope within which that is so and names the elements that establish it there: for example, that a regularity does not come from the property the question named, together with where it does come from. It may be that the premise of the question fails. Each of these settles the question.

Two kinds of account leave a question open. One asserts an impossibility that has no stated scope or no proof, such as that no single argument can cover two regimes, when what the work shows is that the arguments it tried do not. The other reports the failure of one method, or explains one regime, and gives no answer to the rest of what the statement asks. In either case say in the reason what the statement still asks.

An answered question usually leaves something to be done: an exact statement that is still to be proved, or a sharper question that the answer has brought into view. That remainder does not keep the question open. A candidate should carry it, the statement as an element of the canon and the sharper question as a proposal with grounds of its own, and you judge those as you judge any element or proposal. When the candidate speaks of a remainder and carries it in neither form, accept the resolution if the statement is answered, and name the remainder in your reason exactly enough that a later peer can propose it, because once the question is closed nothing else keeps the remainder in view. Left open, the question would go to the next explorer in its first, wide wording, and explorers would keep returning to a question whose open part has become a statement waiting for a proof.

A resolution rests on the elements its text names, so a later finding can reopen the question.

### Doubts about what the canon already holds

While reading a candidate you may come to doubt the proof of an element that is already in the canon, for instance one that the candidate uses. Report it as a concern that names the element, the evidence in doubt and why it matters. When your own examination of that element is complete, you can instead write the finding yourself, as [Review output](../reference/review-output.md) describes.

## Two considerations about a proof you have found correct

After you have confirmed that the proof of an element is correct, ask two further questions about it before you accept it. Both come from the user who runs the research. Both are questions about the mathematics in front of you, and you answer them by reading the proof. Ask each question unless this instance's settings turn it off. `settings.acceptance` in your `input.json` holds `decline_reducible_proofs`, which governs the first question, and `decline_uncomprehended_computation`, which governs the second. Where a value is `false`, do not ask that question in this instance.

### Can the proof clearly be made simpler?

You should not yet accept a correct proof when you judge that it can still clearly be made simpler. The reason is that a proof which can be made simpler becomes more concise when that is done, and the research should have the concise proof. The reason is not the cost of formalization. Nor is it the number of elements that the proof names.

A proof can be made simpler in several ways. Its logic and its steps can be made more concise. A premise can be removed or weakened. A part that is proved directly can be replaced by the use of something already proved. So you should examine the argument itself. Can a step be dropped? Is the same thing obtained twice? Is a hypothesis stated and never used? Is something proved again that an element of the canon already gives? The condition from which this question comes also listed removing a proof by computation or narrowing its range. The second question below covers what that item means.

A simplification clearly remains when you can point to the place in the proof and say how the proof could be simplified there. Any proof may one day be replaced by a better one. That general possibility does not count; if it did, no proof could ever be accepted. You do not have to carry out the simplification. You say where it is and what kind it is, and a later session carries it out.

Some cases from an earlier run may help you judge your own. In one, a candidate wrote that one of its results was the same statement as a result already published, and then proved it again instead of using the published one. In another, told in the common introduction under "Judging from the text itself", three cases of a proof and their tables of constants could be removed, because the proof's own earlier proposition and the range in which its result was used made one fixed value of a parameter sufficient. In both, a reader could point to the place and say what to change.

Two things were treated in that run as possible simplifications, and you should not treat them so. First, a reviewer asked that the use of a published result be deleted and that the needed fact be derived again from its source. The reviewer's ground was that the proof used only part of what the result says, and that the unused part depended on further results. If the part is needed, there is no reason not to use the result. A proof that names an element and uses part of what it states does not need simplifying on that account. Second, reviewers compared how many published results each version of a proof depended on. A proof that names fewer elements is not thereby simpler, and a change that makes it name more can still be a simplification.

One thing is particular to a proof that derives its statement from other statements. Such a proof is never the whole argument: it is joined to the proofs of the statements it names, and the joined argument is what establishes the statement. Some possible simplifications can therefore be seen only when the proof is read together with the published proofs of what it names. In the earlier run, one such proof needed an upper bound and obtained it from a separate theorem, which came with its own table of explicit constants. The published proof of the statement it rested on already used a stronger bound. Read alone, the proof needed that theorem. Read together with the proof beneath it, it obtained a second time something the joined argument already had. Where a statement that the proof names has a published proof, read the two together.

### Is part of the statement established only by large computation done without understanding?

You should not yet accept a proof when it shows some part of its statement to be true by computing over a large range, and gives no account of why that part is true. The computation does show that the part holds, and the proof is correct. The reason for not yet accepting it is this. Even where a computation can show at once that something is true, the more mathematical work of explaining why it is true should continue. A statement whose proof is accepted is no longer work that remains, and no session then takes up that explanation.

This question is not directed at computation in general. It concerns crude large-scale computation done without understanding, and not every computation. The presence of a computation in a proof is not a reason to withhold acceptance. Neither is the fact that a proof relies on computed results covering a wide range. Whether a particular computation is of the kind meant is for you to judge, by reading the proof and seeing what work the computation does in it. There is no numerical boundary.

One case can serve for comparison, because the user was asked about it directly. An implication had been proved, without computation, for all but a finite remainder. The remainder consisted of a few dozen individual inequalities and a few thousand entries of a table, and the argument settled it by looking those entries up in an exact table that an existing paper had already computed. The session that assembled this argument did not offer the implication as proved, because it expected review to refuse it, and the reviewer of its work read the condition in the same way. The user judged that a lookup of this size is not a large computation in the sense meant. That judgment decides this one case. It says nothing about other tables, larger ranges or computations that a session runs anew. You judge those yourself, with this case available for comparison.

Two further points concern this question. First, rewriting a proof so that its computation looks up fewer entries of a table does not change the answer, because the entries that are still looked up remain unexplained. What a later session has to do for such a proof is to replace the computation with an argument that says why that part of the statement is true. Second, when you do accept a proof that closes a finite remainder by a lookup like the one in the case above, the remainder is still unexplained. Say in your reason what remains unexplained, so that later sessions can see the open question.

### When the answer to either question is yes

Answering yes to either question does not mean that the proof is wrong. Confirm the proof as you otherwise would, and decide publication as you otherwise would. The difference is that the proof is not accepted, so the statement's standing is "correct, held back".

Place the judgment on the element in which the difficulty lies. The canon keeps results as separate elements, and the place where a proof has room to be simplified, or where a part is closed by computation, is usually one element. Where a statement is established in three ranges, as three elements, and the difficulty lies in the third, the proof that is not accepted is the proof of the third range. The proof of a fourth element that combines the three, if nothing is wrong with it, is accepted; it is not settled while the third range is not, and that is computed without your writing it. A session looking for work then finds the judgment exactly where the work is.

Write the reason specifically enough that the next session to take the statement on can begin at the place you name; [Review output](../reference/review-output.md) says, where it describes `reason`, what the reason for a proof you confirmed and held back states. That session starts from the published proof. When its revised proof reaches review, review asks the same two questions again.

## Compile a proof that is complete in Lean yourself

When a candidate offers a proof that is complete in Lean, confirming it requires your own compile of the candidate and your own reading of the axioms the proof depends on. Read [Lean](../reference/lean.md) before compiling. You make that compile with `commands.verify`, which [Review output](../reference/review-output.md) describes under "Evidence for a proof that is complete in Lean", together with the field in which the build is recorded. The build it reports is the evidence that this check was yours.

Compare the axioms printed for each statement with the allowed list, `settings.lean.allowed_axioms` in your `input.json`. `sorryAx` among them means that the proof, or something it names, is not complete in Lean. A proof that is itself complete and rests, directly or through other elements, on a statement whose Lean proof still ends in `sorry` shows `sorryAx` through that statement, which is an ordinary state: Lean has then checked the derivation from the named statements, and the statement becomes formal when they do. Any other axiom outside the allowed list is to be named in your reason. Such an axiom keeps the statement from being formal and does not by itself decide your verdict: whether the proof is correct remains your judgment of the proof, including of what it takes from the axiom.

A compile checks the proof of the Lean statement. It does not check that the statement says what was meant, which remains the first judgment described above under "What you judge of each element". The root is the one statement for which that judgment was made before the research began: it was fixed in Lean when the instance was created, so a formal proof of the root's own declaration needs no comparison of statements. For that reason your compile and your reading of the axioms are the last check before the research counts as complete, and they deserve the corresponding care.

If the broker could not run Lean or the environment is unavailable, say what remains unchecked. An execution failure is not Lean rejecting the source, and a proof you could not compile is a judgment you could not complete. A complete Lean proof that an attempt or a peer supplied is judged in the same way as a formalizer's; the role of its author makes no difference.

## Investigate a concern through actual uses

A comprehensive assignment has `kind: "comprehensive"` in its `task` and names concerns, conflicts or elements to examine: `task.concerns`, the IDs of the concerns; `task.conflicts`; and `task.requested_elements`, when the operator asked for particular elements to be examined; with `task.purpose` saying why. Examine them through the three sub-reviews and your own judgment described above; each sub-reviewer then traces the questioned proof and its uses as you do. Trace the questioned element to the argument and evidence that support it. Then follow the elements that actually use it. The canon returns everything whose proof names an element, one step at a time or all the way, so the uses are found by traversal and not by search; read each proof for how it uses the element. One comprehensive review handles this connected work; the runner does not launch a separate reviewer for every use.

When checking the fidelity or the support of an element requires the originals of the work that added it, find the change that added it or last replaced its proof. The verdict in the element's view names that change until a finding withdraws or restores the confirmation. From then on the line names the publication that carried the finding, a comprehensive review or the change that a later review assessed, and the canon command `log`, with `--last` set high enough to reach it, gives for each change the elements it added and the proofs it replaced. The approaches tried name the sessions that contributed. The folder of that change under the instance's `knowledge/changes/` holds a `provenance.json` whose `producer_session` and `peer_session` identify the corresponding directories under this instance's `sessions/`. A directory whose `input.json` lists folders under `task.continues` is the last of several sessions that worked on that task in turn, and those folders belong to the originals as well. You are designated to inspect those originals for this assessment, including their retained conversations, evidence and the archives `work.tar.xz` and `raw.tar.xz` when present. `retention.json` in each of those directories names the retained native material and associated files. Once the work of a session has finished processing, its work folder is inside `work.tar.xz` and its raw conversation inside `raw.tar.xz`: the `cli.stdout.jsonl` of each turn and the transcript file `transcript-<id>.jsonl`, each under the path `retention.json` gives it; `conversation.md`, the readable form of the conversation, stays in the directory. Keep the source directories and archives unchanged; put any extracted working copies in your own work directory. If the originals are unavailable, explain which check remains incomplete and what the published evidence alone supports.

This access is confined to originals identified through the elements relevant to your assignment. Unrelated unpublished work remains outside your scope. Historical instructions in those originals explain how that work was produced; your own launch instructions and user conditions govern this review. Ordinary sessions continue to obtain other sessions' mathematics through the canon.

Determine which uses touch the defect. If a lemma's proof covers n at least 1 while its statement asserts n at least 0, a later proof that needs only the established range is sound as mathematics, while one that requires the missing case has an incomplete justification. Both name the lemma as stated, so when you withdraw the confirmation of the lemma's proof, neither is settled through it any longer; that is computed from what each proof names, without a finding for each of them. What the computation cannot say is which of them is easily restored. Say it in your feedback: which uses need only the part that was established, so that the narrower statement can enter under a new name and those proofs can rest on it, and which need the part that is missing. Withdraw the confirmation of a further element only when its own proof is defective, and follow the affected uses until their consequences have been assessed, stating any part you could not finish.

Write findings that name the elements whose confirmation you withdraw or reaffirm, and the questions whose resolution no longer stands. Where a finding establishes the confirmation of a proof, you have read that proof as the review of a candidate reads one, so make the second judgment of it as well, as "Two considerations about a proof you have found correct" describes, and write it in the finding. Without it a proof that no review had confirmed stands as correct and held back although neither question was asked of it, and a proof whose confirmation you restore keeps the judgment it had. [Review output](../reference/review-output.md) explains, under "Concerns and later findings", what a finding changes. The elements, their records and the earlier judgments stay in the canon with your findings attached. If fundamental mathematics is missing, report that work and do not author replacement proofs.

Each entry of `task.conflicts` has the form `<statement>:<change IDs>` and names a contested statement: settled proofs currently establish both the statement and its negation, and the IDs are the changes whose judgments stand on the two sides. Correct proofs of the same exact statement cannot reach opposite outcomes, so the opposition points to a defective step in a proof on one side or in a proof beneath it, or to a written argument about an object other than the one its Lean statement denotes. Follow each side through what its proofs name until you locate the failure, and withdraw the confirmation of the proof you find defective. While both sides stand, the frontier view names the statement as contested, and a contested root does not complete the research. If you cannot determine which argument fails, explain what you examined and what remains open; the statement then stays contested.

## When the assessment is ready

Your assessment is ready when the three sub-reviewers have reported, the assigned elements or the questioned uses have been examined by them and by you, every required judgment has its grounds, your feedback says how the sub-reviews agreed and how you resolved their disagreements, and any remaining uncertainty is identified with its effect. A candidate review must also account for fidelity to the source, for what the work contributed, for publication, for each proof the candidate would replace, and for the proposed questions and resolutions. A comprehensive review must explain how far the defect and its consequences were traced. An interrupted review is unfinished processing; a completed assessment can explicitly state what it could not determine. Run the transport check and end with the assessment files in your output directory.
