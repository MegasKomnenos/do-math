# The account of the problem as a whole

How the account of the problem as a whole, the understanding so far of question `q0`, is written and judged: the standard it is written to, the procedure by which a peer writes it, and how a review judges it.

## The standard the account is written to

The account is read differently from every other text of the canon. Any other text is opened by a session that has a reason to open it. This one is placed in the starting context of every session, in every role, and each session reads all of it before it begins the work it was launched for: an attempt before it chooses a statement, an explorer before it chooses where to look, a peer and a reviewer before they judge what a piece of work changed in what is known. What these sessions need from it is the state of the problem at the level of the whole. For a problem in the middle of its research that usually includes what is asked and what is known; how the existing proofs and reductions go; where the root is held up, and why there; what the places at which different lines of work stopped have in common; and what is not understood. A session that has read nothing else should come away able to say where the difficulty lies, and to recognise work that would bear on it.

Because every session reads all of it, the account has a fixed room. Its text, as the peer writes it in the candidate and without the `## Review` part that the runner adds at publication, is at most 40,000 characters. No program checks this. The peer counts the characters of its file, and review says so when a text is over. The reason for the room is what happened in one research while the account had none. The account was written nine times there, by the peers of nine successive explorations. The guides of that time asked each peer to keep what the text held and to add what its exploration had found, and each peer did so with care. Within three days the text grew from 20,000 characters to 180,000, and it came to name more than five hundred elements in sixty paragraphs, some of them ten thousand characters long. The text was not careless: its statements were exact, and they named the elements they rested on. But the passage that said where the root was held up was by then one among many of the same standing, and a session reading the text could no longer tell it from the rest. And the text by itself took up about a fifteenth of everything a session can keep in view while it works, in every session, before that session had read a line of what it was launched to do. The room is twice the length of the first of those nine texts, which told a session what it needed at that stage of the research. It leaves a later account, of a problem about which more is known, twice that space.

The room states what every session gives up in order to read the account. It is not a measure of the account. A text can be within its room and of no use to anyone, and whether an account serves its readers is judged by reading it, as the common introduction asks of every judgment under "Judging from the text itself".

The room suffices because the account is not where the research keeps its results. The canon keeps each thing in one place, and a session reaches it there. The statement of a result, its proof and the explanation of what it shows are in its element. How an approach to a statement went and where it stopped is among the approaches tried on that statement. What is understood about a narrower question is in that question's own text. Which results exist about an object is what a traversal from that object returns. What the account holds is what none of these places can hold, because it concerns how they stand to one another: how the results combine, which of them the progress so far rests on, why lines of work that look different stop at what is one difficulty, and where all this leaves the root. So the account names an element where its argument turns on that element, and says what the element gives at the length the argument needs: a clause for a result that is used, the construction itself for an example on which the argument rests. It does not retell an element for the element's sake, and it does not list the elements that exist. A passage that is about one statement, one object or one narrower question, and not about the problem as a whole, belongs with that statement, object or question, and the account needs at most a sentence that says it is there. The dependence runs one way. Because the account is rewritten, no other text of the canon can rely on a passage of it: a record that needs a point states the point, and does not send its reader to the account for it.

A text is brought within its room by deciding what it is about, and not by saying the same things in fewer words. The difference shows on a case. Suppose the account contains this paragraph:

> The induction on $n$ needs a bound for the sum over the short intervals. The estimate of `Sum.short_le` loses a factor $\log n$ there, because it bounds every term by the largest one. Near the endpoints the terms are smaller than the largest by a factor $n^{1/2}$, and no argument in the canon uses this yet.

A writer who is short of room is tempted in two directions. One is to keep every fact and press the words together: "Induction blocked at short-interval log loss (SIL); endpoint slack $n^{1/2}$ unused." A session that has not read the earlier text cannot read this. It has to guess what is blocked, what "SIL" abbreviates and which slack is meant, and the reason for the loss, which was the part it could have used, is gone. The other direction is to keep the sentence and let its content go: "The inductive approach meets difficulties with certain sums." This is true and reads smoothly, and a session can do nothing with it. Each of the two took a paragraph that did its work and left one that does not, to save about two hundred characters. The paragraph should have stayed as it was. Space for it is made by taking out something whole that the canon holds in another place: the list of the ranges in which the bound was verified, for instance, which the record of the verifying element gives, or the story of a second approach that its approach entry already tells.

Those two are the most frequent forms of one failure, and the failure takes other forms as well: a name coined for the occasion and then used as if the reader knew it; a noun phrase that packs a process into three words; a row of element names with nothing said of what each contributes; a "see `X`" standing where the explanation was the point; a claim that stays while the range or the hypothesis that made it true is dropped; a "so" or a "because" removed, so that two facts stand side by side without their relation. In each of them the cost of the shortening has been moved from the writer to the reader, and the reader of this text is every session. [How this user likes to be written for](user-writing-preferences.md) describes the same thing from the side of the user who reads the research: plain writing shows all of what it has to say, and compressing or hinting is not a way of being plain. Whatever stays in the account therefore stays at the length its content needs. Its objects are introduced before they are used, its claims have the exact form and scope that their elements give them, and the step from each claim to the next is written out. This holds most of all for the passages that carry the account's own subject. Where the root is held up, and why there, is told with the particulars that let a session act on it: the step that fails, the example that shows the failure, the quantity that is lost and the reason it is lost. Those particulars are what the account exists to give, and no space is saved on them; that this part takes the largest share of the room is to be expected.

## How a peer writes the account

When the question your assignment names is `q0`, the understanding text you write is the account of the problem as a whole. Every session launched after your change is published reads all of it before it begins its own work, so of everything in your change it is the part that the most sessions will meet, and it deserves the corresponding share of your thought. Review reads your account by the standard of the part above, the fixed room of its text included. Each peer that replaces the account answers for the whole text within that room; it does not append to the text it found.

Do not take the current text as a draft to which the findings of this exploration are added. Start from what a session brings to the account, which is the question of where the root is held up and why, and decide what the account has to say now in order to answer it: which few things, in which order, and how much each of them needs. The exploration changed something in that picture. Decide what it changed and where in the account that belongs. It may be a new passage. It may be that an existing passage becomes shorter because the exploration settled what the passage left open, or that the weight of the whole shifts toward something that was a side remark before. The producing session's report says how it sees this, and its view is material for your decision, as everything in the report is. Review's assessment beneath the current text is addressed to you, the peer who next replaces it: where review found something wrong with that text, it says there what to restore, move or write out, and where each of these is.

Then read the current text against your plan, passage by passage. A passage that belongs in the account as you now plan it, and that says what it has to say exactly and in full, stays as it stands; nothing is gained by rewording it. For one that does not belong, find out where its content is held, because that decides what you do with it. Most often the canon already holds it: the passage retells the record of an element, an entry among the approaches tried, or the text of a narrower question. Nothing is lost by leaving such a passage out, and the account keeps at most a sentence that says where its content is, when a session needs to know that it exists. How far to check where a passage is held is a judgment of what depends on it. For a subject that a later session will need, open the place and see that it holds what the passage said. For a passage that named results in passing, it is enough that those elements stand in the canon with their records. Sometimes the passage holds something worth keeping that is about one object or one statement and that no record and no entry has, for example why a definition behaves as it does, or the place where an approach to one statement stops. Put it where it belongs, in this same change. An explanation goes into the record of its element; a candidate can replace a record without replacing the proof and without holding a section, as [The candidate change](change-format.md) says under "Written texts". The place where an approach stops goes among the approaches tried on its statement. And sometimes a passage is no longer needed anywhere. It then stays in the history of the question, where `question q0 --history` prints what each replacement left out. History is read only by a session that goes looking for it, so see to it that whatever a later session will need ends up in a place where that session comes upon it.

Writing the account in this way depends on your understanding of the problem as a whole, and the writing is where you find out how far that understanding reaches. You will come to passages of the current text that you could repeat and could not explain: why a method stops where it does, what a reduction gains, how two results that the text sets side by side are related. A passage you cannot explain is one you cannot rewrite, since you cannot tell which of its words carry it, and you cannot decide whether it belongs in the account either. So go and get the understanding. Open the element the passage names and read its statement, its record and its proof. Read the approaches tried on the statement. Read what the producing session did there. Work the step yourself until you could answer a colleague who asked why. Often the passage then becomes shorter by itself, because you can now say the one thing it was circling around. Sometimes you find that nobody understood it: the text asserted a connection that no element establishes and no argument supports. In that case, if the account needs the point, it says exactly this, in plain words: what is established and by which element, and that the reason, or the connection, is not understood. If the account does not need the point, the passage goes, and the description of the change says that it went because nothing in the canon supports it. What the account should never hold is writing that sounds like understanding and is not: a sentence of the old text with its terms in a new order, a general phrase where the old text had a particular fact, a "hence" between two statements whose connection you did not check. A sentence of that kind is worse than a gap that is marked, because the session that reads it has no way to see that something is still to be looked into.

The text you replace may be far above its room, several times over. Do not try to cut such a text down to size. Cutting a text to a quarter of its length, passage by passage, keeps a quarter of every passage, and the result is the compressed text that the part above describes. Write the account anew from your plan, and use the current text as a source in which you look up what was established and by which element. A passage of the current text that fits your plan as it stands can be taken over whole. What is ruled out is shortening the passages in order to keep them all. Most of what the current text holds will not be in the new account and should not be. The elements it names keep their records, and a session that works on one of those statements meets them there. Plan the account once you have read the producer's work and formed your own view of the problem, before the rest of the candidate takes your attention, and give the writing the time that a text of this weight needs.

Tell review what you did to the account, in two places. The description of the change says it in a few sentences: that the account was rewritten, what it is now built around, and anything that has left it of which a later session should know. The detail goes into a list that you write as a file in your `work/` folder, which review reads with the rest of your retained work, and the description names that file. The list says which subjects the new text no longer treats, and for each of them whether an element, an approach entry or another question's text holds it, whether you moved it there in this change, or whether it now stands only in the history. It says for which of them you opened the place and read it, and for which you rely on the old text's naming of the element. It also says which claims of the old text you corrected, and which you left out because you found nothing that supports them. Review reads the new text against the old one, and this list is what lets it tell a subject you moved from one that was lost.

## How a review judges the account

When the candidate you review replaces the understanding so far of `q0`, the replacing text is the account of the problem as a whole, which every session reads in full at launch. Judge the new account by the standard that the first part of this document gives, the fixed room of its text included. You have read the old account in your own starting context, so read the new one by itself first, before you compare the two, and ask what a session would learn from it that had this text and nothing else of the research. Does such a session learn where the root is held up, and why there? Is what the text says exact, each claim with the scope its element gives it? Is it written out, or does it use a name it never introduced, set element names in a row without saying what each gives, or mention in a phrase what the old text explained? Check the sentences that connect one result with another against the elements they name. An account can read as though a connection were understood when no element establishes it, and a later session will take such a sentence at its word. That the account is shorter than the one it replaces is to be expected and is no ground for concern by itself. That it is within its room does not make it good. No program checks the room, so count the characters of the candidate's text yourself; when the text is over its room, say so, and say which of its subjects the canon holds elsewhere.

The description of the change says what the peer did to the text and names a file in the peer's retained work, under `task.peer`, that lists the subjects the peer took out and where each of them is held. Check what the list says by opening those places, and read the old text yourself for what the list does not mention.
