# How the paper is written

## What this document is for

When a do-math research instance has settled its root proposition by a formal proof, one command writes a paper about the result: a mathematical paper in English, as a LaTeX manuscript and a PDF. The command runs to the end without asking anyone anything. It does the work by launching AI sessions. One session plans the paper, several write its sections side by side, and others read the manuscript and repair it. A script gives each session its files, assembles what the sessions leave, and checks it.

Sessions that cannot talk to each other write one paper only if they share the decisions that shape it: who the paper is for, what each of its parts does, which results receive a number, in what order they come, and how the text is written. These decisions are also not the sessions' to make. They belong to the user who runs the research. The user made them once, before any paper was written, so that every paper comes out in the same way and no run has to stop and ask. This document records those decisions and the reason for each.

If you are one of the sessions, the decisions below are made, and your work applies them to the research in front of you. What remains yours is everything that a decision made in advance cannot contain: how this particular mathematics is explained so that the reader described below understands it and can check it. The reasons are written out because a research will always present cases that this document did not foresee. In such a case the reason tells you what the decision was meant to achieve, and you act for that.

The prompt of your session says which files you receive and which files you leave. This document says what the paper is.

Three things that pass between the sessions are named throughout. The *materials* are what the script takes from the research; the next section describes them. The *plan* is what the planning session writes, and the script checks it before any section is written. It fixes the sections of the paper and its numbered statements, in their order, and records every other choice that this document gives to the planning session; the sessions that write and repair follow it as it stands. A *part* is the LaTeX of one section. A session that writes a part receives a *brief* for its section, which the script makes from the plan and the materials and which the section "What each session works from" describes.

## Where the paper comes from

The research is kept as a library written in Lean, a proof assistant. The library is called the canon. Each declaration of the library is an element: a definition, or a statement, which Lean calls a theorem. Beside each element is a written record. The record holds what Lean cannot hold: a short description of the element, its statement in natural language, an explanation of what it says and why it holds, a proof in natural language, and, for a result taken from the literature, its source and how much of the source was read. The record also gives the element a weight, which is either *unit* or *step*. A unit is a result that someone describing the structure of the proof would name. A step is a move inside one proof. A definition has a weight in the same sense: an object that such a description would name is a unit, and an auxiliary notion that serves one argument is a step.

The root is the proposition the research was started to settle. It was fixed at the beginning, in words and in Lean, and it does not change. The paper is written when the root has a formal proof, that is, a proof that Lean has checked completely and that review has accepted. Everything that this proof rests on has then been proved in Lean as well, the results taken from the literature included, from no axioms other than those the instance allows. The materials list them; unless the user changed them, they are the three standard axioms of Lean. When the research refuted the root, the formal proof is a proof of its negation, and the main result of the paper is that negation.

The main result of the paper is the statement that settles the root, unless another theorem of the research was chosen for it before the paper is written. That is done when the research proved a theorem that says more than the root and from which the root follows. The paper is then built around that theorem, since it is the one that carries the insight, and the root stands in the paper as a numbered statement of its own. The materials say which element is the main result and which element settles the root. Where this document speaks of the main result or the main theorem, it means the element that the materials name as the main result.

The script takes from the canon the materials of the paper: every element that the formal proof of the root rests on, and when the main result is another theorem also everything that theorem rests on, each with its record, in an order in which each element comes after everything it depends on; the root in words; the account of the problem as a whole that the research kept while it ran; and the bibliography. The body of the paper is what the proof rests on, and nothing else. There is one addition, which is made when the paper is written from a draft, as the section "The draft that comes before the paper" describes. The materials then hold as well the declarations that were added to the library to close a gap of the draft, with everything those rest on, also where the proof of the root does not use them: a consequence of a result, a bound at the values the paper uses, a formula that a proof refers to. They were added so that the paper can state them, and the materials mark them as added for the draft. Results that the research found and the proof does not use, approaches that were abandoned, and questions that stayed open are not in the materials and are not in the paper. The user decided this because the paper is to state the mathematics of the result cleanly, accurately and concisely. It is not a report on the research.

The canon was not made to be read from beginning to end. It grows by addition, it is cut into the small declarations that a proof assistant needs, and its proofs were written by many sessions, each for one element by itself. A coherent exposition does not exist inside it. The paper is therefore not the canon carried over declaration by declaration. It is a new exposition of the same mathematics for a reader who will never see the canon.

When drawing on the account of the problem as a whole, read the review that the materials give beneath it. The review covers the change that supplied the account, and it opens by naming that change and the revision at which the reviewer made that judgment. Use the qualifications that bear on the passage you draw from. The status beside each theorem comes from the revision used to prepare the paper materials.

## What holds the sections together

A section refers to another section only through what the plan fixes: the section itself, by its label, and its numbered statements, by theirs. An equation, a subsection or a remark of another section is not referred to, also when the writer sees it in a finished part, because nothing in the plan promises that it will still be there after a repair. The labels that a writer creates, for its equations and its subsections, are its own, and they begin with the id of its section so that two writers cannot create the same one.

The plan fixes the notation of every object that more than one section speaks of, and not only that of the main objects. A paper written by one hand needs a table of notation for its main objects and can name an auxiliary quantity where it first appears, since every later passage is written with that passage in view. Here no writer has another writer's passage in view. An auxiliary function, set, constant or condition that one section defines and another uses, or that the proofs of two sections both use, is named alike in both only if the plan names it. The planning session therefore gives such an object an entry in the notation: its symbol, or its name in words when it has no symbol, and what it denotes, with its conventions. The entry also says which definitions of the materials the object is, so that the script can show the entry in a brief at every element it belongs to; a writer who finds no entry there has to invent a name, and two writers invent two. The entry names several definitions where the canon spreads one mathematical object over several declarations. The script checks what it can see: a definition of the materials that the writers of two sections meet has an entry. Whether an object that is no definition of the materials needs an entry, such as a quantity that several records introduce in passing, is a judgment about the mathematics, and it stays with the planning session. An object that one section alone uses has no entry, and its writer names it.

An object is still defined exactly once. When the plan writes a definition into places that lie in different sections, the first of them in the order of the paper defines it, and the later ones use it under its notation and may point to the section where it is defined.

## The reader

The paper is a research article in mathematics. Its reader is a mathematician who works in the area of the main result or in a neighbouring one, at the level of a doctoral student or above. This reader knows what the graduate textbooks of the area contain and is used to reading proofs. The reader has not necessarily met the particular objects of this paper or the vocabulary that grew around them, and expects every notion that is not textbook material to be defined, or cited, at the place where it is first used.

The reader has the paper and the public literature, and nothing else. The reader has no access to the work from which the paper was drawn, cannot ask its authors, and does not know how the paper was produced.

The reader comes to the paper to learn exactly what is proved, with every hypothesis, and to decide from the introduction whether to read on. A reader who goes on checks proofs line by line and will want to cite statements by their numbers. Some readers read only the abstract, the introduction and the statements. Some read one proof closely. Many arrive in the middle of the paper through a cross-reference and have to understand a statement at the place where it stands.

The paper is read in several sittings by someone who has time, who can search the text and turn back, and who will stop at the first step that does not follow.

## The parts of the paper

The paper has the parts that the reader expects of a research article, and each part has one job.

The **title** names the object and the result as specifically as a title can. It does not describe how the result was obtained.

The **abstract** is one paragraph that states the main result in words, with as little notation as the statement allows. It circulates apart from the paper, so it has to stand by itself: it contains no citation and no reference to a numbered statement. What it says of the result is true of everything it speaks about. It may leave out a hypothesis that a reader of an abstract does not need, but it keeps every restriction of the range: an abstract that says "for every $n$" where the theorem holds for $n \ge 1$ asserts a case that was not proved.

The **introduction** does four things, in this order. First it states the problem: the objects are introduced, briefly and exactly, and the question about them is asked, so that a reader from a neighbouring area understands what is at stake before any result appears. Second, it states the main result as a numbered theorem with every hypothesis. A reader who reads no further must leave with the exact statement. When the main result is not the statement that settles the root, the introduction also says, after the main theorem, what follows from it for the root and where the paper states that. Third, it places the result relative to earlier work, as far as the materials support this and no further. Fourth, it outlines the proof: the strategy, the step at which the difficulty lies, and the idea that overcomes it. The outline is what lets a reader follow a long argument without losing the thread, and it is where the reader learns which of the many steps carries the proof. The introduction draws on the account of the problem as a whole and on the explanations in the records of the main result and of the elements that its proof names directly. Those are the places where the research wrote down how the proof goes and where it was hard. A short paragraph on how the paper is organised may close the introduction when the paper has enough sections to need one.

The **preliminaries** fix the notation and recall the definitions and the cited results that the proofs use. They contain what the reader needs in order to read the rest and nothing that the rest does not use. A short paper may have no separate section of preliminaries; the plan decides.

The **results with their proofs** are the body: one section or several, in which every result that the main theorem rests on is stated and proved, and the main theorem is proved at the end. Each of these sections begins by saying what it establishes and what that will be used for.

The **references** list the works cited, and only those.

The paper has no section of open problems, no account of other approaches, and no acknowledgements. The first two are excluded by what the paper is, as the section "Where the paper comes from" said. The author line is not a session's to write either. Who signs the paper is the user's decision, so the script writes that line from the names that the user's configuration gives, each with its address and its email where the configuration has them. When the configuration names nobody, the manuscript has no author line.

## Which elements receive a number

A numbered statement is a promise to the reader that the statement is worth finding again: it will be referred to, or it is a result that the reader may want to cite. A paper that numbers everything breaks that promise and hides its structure. A paper that numbers too little forces long proofs to carry results that deserved a name. The canon has already recorded, for each element, on which side of this line its author and its reviewer saw it. That record is the weight, and the weight is the material for the choice.

**An element whose weight is unit becomes a numbered statement. An element whose weight is step is absorbed into the proof that uses it.** A numbered statement is a theorem, a proposition, a lemma, a corollary or a definition, and the choice among them follows the ordinary meaning of the words. The main result is a theorem. The statement that settles the root is numbered as well when it is not the main result: as a corollary where it follows from the main theorem in a few lines, and otherwise as a theorem. A unit that a reader might cite for its own sake is a proposition, or a theorem if it stands beside the main result in importance. A unit whose value lies in the proof that it serves is a lemma. A unit that follows in a few lines from a statement already numbered is a corollary. A definition whose weight is unit is given either as a numbered definition or in the running text, and the plan says which. It is numbered when later text has to refer back to it by its number. The objects of the problem are defined in the introduction, where the problem is stated. A definition whose weight is step is introduced where it is needed: inside the proof that uses it, or, when a numbered statement mentions it, in that statement or in the text before it. In every case an object is defined exactly once, before its first use.

To absorb a step is not to drop it. Its content is written, in the notation of the paper, at the place in a proof where it is used, as part of that proof's argument and without a statement of its own. If the step is routine for the reader, a clause is enough. If it is not, its argument is given in full at that place.

The proofs of the paper come from the proofs in the records. Where a record gives no proof in words, the statement has nevertheless been proved in Lean. The writer then gives the argument, which in such cases is usually short, and writes only an argument that it has checked step by step. A fact that the reader knows from the textbooks is used with an ordinary phrase and needs neither a citation nor a proof.

The weight was assigned inside the canon, and the paper sometimes needs a different choice. A step that several numbered proofs use is better stated once as a lemma than repeated. A step whose argument is so long that it would break the proof around it is better taken out of that proof and numbered. In the other direction, some units are not numbered: a unit that falls under one of the cases of the section "What of the canon the paper leaves out", a result from the literature that is only cited in the running text, a definition that is given in the running text. These are the usual cases and not a closed list; what decides is the promise described at the start of this section. The planning session makes these choices, and for every element that departs from its weight the plan records the reason, so that the departure can be seen and judged afterwards.

A numbered statement states exactly one element: the paper does not combine two elements into one statement, and no session adds a numbered statement of its own. The reason is the correspondence that the section "Every numbered statement says what its element says" describes: for each numbered statement it must be possible to point to the formally proved statement that it expresses.

## The order

Two demands fix the order, and they pull in different directions. The reader needs to know the destination first, so the main result is stated in the introduction, before any other theorem, proposition, lemma or corollary. And a proof may use only what has already been stated, so every object is defined before the first statement that mentions it, and every numbered statement is stated before the first proof that uses it.

The two are reconciled in the standard way. The main theorem is *stated* first and *proved* last, when everything it rests on is available. The introduction therefore has to define the objects of which the main statement speaks. The sections after it build up, in order of dependence, what the proof needs. Every other proof follows its statement directly; where the order of the paper requires it, the plan can defer one in the same way, and it then names the section that carries the proof.

Among the orders that respect dependence, the paper takes the one in which a reader reads. The materials arrive in one valid order of dependence, but that order comes from the arrangement of the library into modules and from the sequence in which the research happened to add declarations, and the user decided that neither is followed. Results that belong to one idea stand together. A lemma stands close to the proof that uses it, when dependence allows. The section boundaries follow the mathematics.

## What of the canon the paper leaves out

Some things are in the canon only because of the way a canon is built and kept. The user listed them and decided for each what the paper shows instead. The planning session recognises these cases from the materials, which show for every element what its proof names and what its statement mentions. For each element that the paper does not state, the plan records the element that stands in its place.

*The consequence left behind by a superseded statement.* The canon never deletes a statement. When the research replaces a statement by a better one, the old statement stays under its name, and its proof becomes a one-line derivation from the new statement, so that everything that used the old one still compiles. The paper does not state the old statement. Where a proof used it, the paper calls the new statement, and adds a clause on how the case that is needed follows if that is not immediate.

*Several proofs of one statement.* The canon can hold more than one proof of a statement. The paper gives one: the proof that the formal proof of the root rests on. The materials already contain only that one. If the same statement nevertheless reaches the materials under two declarations, the paper states it once, for the declaration whose proof it gives, and the plan records the other as not stated.

*Two definitions and an element stating that they agree.* A session that found the original definition inconvenient for a proof may have introduced a second definition of the same object, together with an element stating that the two agree. The paper has one definition of each object, namely the one that fits the root as it is stated in words, and every statement about the other definition is restated with it. What becomes of the element that states the agreement depends on what it says mathematically. If the two definitions differ only in how the object is represented in Lean, the paper has nothing to say and the element is not stated. If they describe the object in two mathematically different ways, for example by a recursion and by a closed formula, the second description is a result about the one object, and it is stated or absorbed like any other element.

*Conventions of the Lean form.* A statement in Lean often carries conventions that come from the proof assistant: indices that start at zero, quantities that are normalised, functions that are defined everywhere and take an arbitrary value where the mathematical function is undefined, subtraction of natural numbers that stops at zero. The paper uses the conventions of mathematics, and where there is a choice it uses those of the root as stated in words, because that statement is the user's own formulation of the problem. Statements are carried over into these conventions, as the section "Every numbered statement says what its element says" describes.

*The course of the research.* The canon records which proofs are held back and why, which approaches were tried, and what earlier versions of a record said. None of this is mathematics about the objects, and none of it is stated.

*The arrangement of the library.* The division into modules and the order of declarations are not followed, as the section "The order" said.

*Results taken from the literature.* They are cited and not proved again, as the section "Results from the literature" describes.

## Every numbered statement says what its element says

Each numbered statement of the paper says exactly what the natural-language statement of its element says: the same objects, the same hypotheses, the same quantifiers and ranges, the same conclusion. It is neither stronger nor weaker. This is the property on which the worth of the paper rests. What stands behind each statement is a proof checked by Lean and a review that judged whether the statement in words expresses the statement in Lean. If a statement of the paper drifts from the statement of its element, that chain is cut at the last link, and nothing in the paper would show it.

The property is kept by the way a statement is written. The writer starts from the statement in the record, and not from the theorem as it is usually remembered or as it would be most convenient. The writer changes only what the paper requires:

- the notation, which becomes the notation that the plan fixed for the whole paper;
- the conventions, which become those described under "Conventions of the Lean form" above; when a convention changes, the statement is carried over, and the boundary cases are checked one by one, because an index that moves by one or a quantity that is rescaled changes the range of every variable it touches;
- the names of other elements, which become references to numbered statements or the ordinary mathematical names of the objects;
- a definition that the paper does not use, which is replaced by the one it uses.

Exactness comes before convention. If a statement cannot be carried into the conventions of the paper without changing what it says, it keeps its own convention and says so in words.

Each numbered statement also carries its own hypotheses. A hypothesis is not moved into a sentence at the head of a section, because a reader who arrives at the statement through a cross-reference would not see it there. A standing assumption, such as a sentence saying that a group is finite throughout, is a hypothesis in this sense. Notation fixed in the preliminaries is the exception.

Where a record gives no statement in words, the Lean statement is the source. Where the statement in words and the Lean statement of an element seem to disagree about a hypothesis or a boundary case, the Lean statement is the one that was proved, and the paper says what it says.

One numbered statement is held to one thing more, namely the statement of the element that settles the root. It says what the root says in words, in the conventions of that statement; when the root was refuted, it says the negation of that. In most papers this statement is the main theorem. When another theorem was chosen as the main result, the main theorem says what its own element says, like every numbered statement, and the statement that is held to the root is the corollary or the theorem in which the paper states the settling element.

The property is checked in two ways. The script writes a correspondence file beside the paper, not inside it. For every numbered statement the file names the element, and it gives the statement in the record, the Lean statement, and the statement as the manuscript has it, so that anyone can read the three side by side. And after the manuscript has been assembled, sessions whose only task is this comparison, one for the numbered statements of each section, judge for each numbered statement whether it says what the statement in words of its element says, with the Lean statement beside it to settle a doubt. Such a session has the statements of its section, the records of their elements, and the manuscript, in whose definitions and notation the statements are read. If it finds that the words and the Lean statement of an element disagree with each other, it reports this as well; the writer has no other way to make such a finding known. A difference goes to a session that repairs the section of the statement, and the statements are compared again. A difference that remains is reported in the log and in the correspondence file.

## What the running text asserts

The numbered statements are not all that a paper asserts. Around them stands the running text: the text of a section outside its numbered statements and outside its proofs, and the abstract. The running text introduces a lemma, says what a section establishes, draws a consequence after a proof, or remarks what a result gives in a special case. A reader takes such a sentence for a result of the paper as much as a numbered statement, so it has to be one.

**In its running text the paper asserts about its objects only what an element states.** An element covers an assertion when the statement of the element says the same thing or says more. The assertion is then that statement in other words, a special case of it, or a weaker form of it. A sentence that follows from two elements together, and that no single element states, is not covered, however short the argument that would join them. Neither is a strengthening that looks harmless: a strict inequality where the element proves a weak one, a range that is extended by one value, a remark that a bound cannot be improved.

The reason is the one that holds for the numbered statements. Behind every element stands a proof that was checked. Behind a sentence that no element states stands only the session that wrote it, and nothing can be put behind it afterwards: the paper is written from the materials as they stand, and the library is not extended for the sake of a sentence.

Most of the running text asserts nothing in this sense, and the rule leaves it as it is. A sentence that says what the paper, a section or a proof does or will do, a definition, the introduction of a notation, a sentence that says where a hypothesis is used or why a definition is made as it is, a fact that the reader knows from the textbooks of the area and that is used with an ordinary phrase: with these a paper explains itself, and they are wanted. What the paper says about the literature is governed by the section "Results from the literature". A proof is not running text either, since its steps are the argument for the statement that it proves. One thing inside a proof is held to the rule all the same, namely a displayed formula to which text outside that proof refers. The paper uses such a formula as a result, so an element has to state it.

The rule also fixes where a derivation stands. A computation or an argument whose result one proof uses belongs inside that proof, at the place where the proof uses it, and not before the statement or between two statements. Inside the proof it is a step of the argument for the statement that the proof proves. Outside it, the same lines are running text, and they assert their result as a result of the paper, which no element states. Most of what a comparison of the running text with the elements reports is of this kind: derivations that are correct, that a proof uses, and that stand outside that proof. A step that two proofs need cannot stand inside both, and the second proof cannot refer to a formula inside the first without using it as a result. Such a step is stated once, by an element: the plan gives it a number where an element states it, and the draft asks the library for it where none does, as the section "The draft that comes before the paper" describes.

For a session that writes, the rule means that a wish to say more is met by saying what the elements say. Where a consequence would be worth stating and no element states it, the sentence is left out. It is not written with an argument of its own, because an argument that is written at that moment has been checked by nobody. The time to ask for a result that the paper lacks is before the paper is written, and the section "The draft that comes before the paper" describes how that is done.

The section "What is checked after writing" describes how the running text is compared with the elements once the manuscript is written, and what becomes of a sentence that no element covers.

## Results from the literature

A result that the canon took from the literature is cited and is not proved again. When it is numbered, it is stated in the form in which the canon states it, with its attribution and with the exact place in the source that the record gives, for example the number of the theorem, and no proof follows it. It is written in the notation and the conventions of the paper like every other statement. When the plan only cites it, the running text says what it is and where it is found. The reader can then tell at every statement whether the paper proves it or relies on someone else's proof.

This holds also for a known result that the research proved for itself. A result which the library proves, and which a source that was found and checked states in the same form, is cited and not proved. The record of such an element names the source, the materials then show the element as a result from the literature, and the plan cites it like every other. A paper that proves a classical identity or a standard bound in full hides what it adds to them, and readers who set such a paper beside published papers object to this more often than to anything else. The paper does not say that the library proves such a result. That it does is true, and it is said beside the paper, in the correspondence file. Inside the paper it would be a remark on the formal proof, which the section "The implementation does not show" excludes.

Between a result that is cited and a result that is proved there is a third case. A result of the library can be an instance of a general theorem that a source states: an expansion in a series that the Lagrange inversion formula gives for one choice of the function, a bound that a cited estimate contains. No source states such a result in the form the library has, so it is not cited in the place of a proof. But a paper does not derive at length what a theorem that it can cite gives in a line. Where the draft of the paper found such a theorem, the brief of the section gives it, with its place in the source and with the specialisation by which the result follows from it. The paper then states the result in the form of its element, cites the theorem, and gives the specialisation as its proof. The library proves such a result by an argument of its own. The paper does not say so, and the correspondence file does, where it names the theorem.

The paper says no more about the literature than was read. The records say, for each source, how much of it was read: the full text, named parts, or the abstract only, and whether the proof was read or only the statement. The paper describes a source to that depth and not beyond. It cites only works that are in the bibliography of the materials. A session may well remember other relevant work. It does not add it, because nobody in the research read it for this purpose, and nobody could vouch for what the paper would then say about it.

What was read is, sentence by sentence, on record. When a paper is written from a draft, the draft can carry, for every sentence of a record that attributes something to a work, the place in the work and the words of the work at that place, copied when the work was opened. A sentence of the paper that says what a work states, proves or defines, what its authors call an object, or where in it a result stands, says what such a quotation shows, at the place the quotation has. The writer takes these sentences from the records and does not sharpen them: a remark does not become a theorem, and a theorem about one quantity is not cited for another. Attributions that were written before the works had been opened in this way did not all stand when they were. After the manuscript is written, the sentences about cited works are compared with the quotations, as the section "What is checked after writing" describes.

For the same reason the paper does not say that a result is new. Whether something is new is a claim about the whole literature, and the research can only report where it looked. Where the records say what the sources that were read contain and do not contain, the introduction may say exactly that, with the scope that the records give. Where the materials say nothing about earlier work, the introduction says nothing about it either; it does not fill the place from memory.

## The draft that comes before the paper

The paper states only what the library contains, in its numbered statements and in its running text. A paper that is written first and compared with the library afterwards learns too late where it wanted more: it states a classical bound in the strict form in which the literature has it where the library proved a weaker form, its later proofs cite by its equation number a bound that is no element, it states an inequality at the values of the parameters that it uses where the library has it at other values, or it states a lemma in a general form where the library has a special case. When the library is extended for such a place after the paper exists, everything that was made from the library is made again. The user therefore decided that the paper is drafted before the library receives its final form, that the library is then brought to contain what the draft asks for as far as that can be done, and that the paper is afterwards written from the draft and states nothing beyond it. The command is then run once for the draft and once more for the paper. A paper can still be written in one run without a draft. What its sections want beyond the library is then found only by the checks that the section "What is checked after writing" describes, and is cut.

The draft is the plan of the paper together with a sketch of every section. A sketch contains no prose. It gives the numbered statements of the section as the paper will state them, and the displayed formulas that the section will use as results, that is, the formulas to which text outside the argument in which they stand will refer. And it gives the *gaps*. A gap is a place where the paper wants to state something that no element states: a numbered statement in another form than its element has, a formula that is used as a result and that no element states, a claim that the running text will make or that a proof will rely on and that no element covers. The session that sketches a section works as the writer of the section would, up to the point of writing, because a gap shows only to someone who has decided how the section will go.

Two kinds of gap are easily missed, and the draft looks for them. The first is a step that two proofs share and that no element states. Without an element, the second proof repeats the derivation or refers into the first proof. The sketch lists such a step, so that the library can receive it as a declaration which both proofs use. The second is a numbered statement of which the paper uses only some parts, where the element states several parts together. A numbered statement says what its element says, so without a declaration of the used parts alone the paper has to state them all; one paper stated a lemma in four parts and used two, and its readers asked what the other two were for. A sketch may give the used parts alone and declare the gap. Where it gives them without declaring one, the comparison of the sketch reports it, and the statement is treated as a gap all the same.

A gap is a request to the library, and it is made where a paper would in fact state the result in that form. A sketch with gaps is not a worse sketch. What harms the paper is a gap that is not declared, since a statement that silently says more than its element is at best found by the comparison and replaced. The sketched statements and formulas are compared with their elements as the statements of a manuscript are. One that differs from its element without a declared gap is a slip of the sketch, and the text that the element supports takes its place. The gaps go to sessions that try to extend the library by what was wanted. Where a declaration is added that states it, the gap is closed, and the paper may state what it wanted. Where none is added, the paper states what the library has. After that the library is fixed, and every statement and every formula of the draft says what an element states.

The planning session of a draft has one more task. It names the results that the paper would cite and not prove if their records named a source, as the section "Results from the literature" describes: results that a mathematician of the field would call known, which the research proved for itself and recorded without a source. A later session looks for a source of each, and enters it into the record only when it has found a work that states the result in the form the element has.

The paper is then written from the draft. Its plan is the plan of the draft, carried over to the library as it has become: an element that is gone leaves the plan, a declaration that closed a gap enters where the gap stood, and a statement whose element now has a source is cited. The brief of each section gives the statements and the formulas of the section as they were drafted, and they stand in the section in that form, because that form is the one that was compared with the library.

## The implementation does not show

The canon is the means by which the result was obtained and verified. The user decided that it does not appear in the paper: the paper states the mathematics, and a reader who has never heard of the canon misses nothing.

This excludes the following from the text, from comments in the LaTeX source, and from labels alike. No Lean name of an element or of a module is written; an object is called by its mathematical name, for which the short name in its record is the starting point. The paper has no statement of the form "A is proved by B", that is, no theorem and no list whose content is which declaration proves or uses which. A sentence saying that a lemma will be used in a later proof, or that a proof uses Lemma 3.2, is ordinary mathematics and is wanted; what is excluded is an account of the declarations of the library. The paper does not speak of sessions, records, reviews, weights, units, steps, or of the way the paper itself was produced. These words are excluded as names for the bookkeeping of the research, and their ordinary mathematical uses, such as the step of an induction, are untouched. The script checks the first of these exclusions by machine. It searches the manuscript for the full Lean names of the canon, for those last components of names that no ordinary word resembles, such as a name with an underscore or with a capital letter inside it, and for the words Lean and Mathlib themselves. It makes the same search in the titles and the key words of the plan, since both stand in the paper as the plan has them.

That the result has been formally verified is true, and an account of the verification has readers of its own. They want to know how the objects were defined in Lean, how the formal statement corresponds to the mathematical one, on which axioms the formal proof rests, and where the source is kept. The user decided that such an account is a document apart from the paper, and that the paper stands as mathematics in natural language. Its theorems are stated in words and formulas. Its proofs are arguments that the reader described in the section "The reader" can follow and check with the paper alone. Nothing in it rests on the fact that a machine has checked something. The paper therefore carries no remark on formal verification and shows no Lean statement, and it does not mention Lean, a proof assistant or a formal proof anywhere. The materials give the Lean statement of every element, and the writer uses it to state the mathematics exactly.

## The manner of writing

The paper follows the conventions of mathematical writing that its reader is used to. The authors are "we", and the tense is the present. A formula is part of a sentence and is punctuated as such. A sentence does not begin with a symbol, and two formulas are separated by words. Every symbol is defined before it is used or at the place where it is first used, and it keeps one meaning throughout; one object has one name and one notation, which the plan fixes for all sections. Quantifiers in running text are written in words. A displayed formula receives a number only if the text refers to it. A statement is referred to by its kind and number. Words such as "clearly" and "it is easy to see" are used only where the step is in fact immediate for the reader described above; where it is not, the step is written out.

Beyond these conventions the paper is written in the way its first reader, the user, prefers. The document `user-writing-preferences.md` describes those preferences together with the occasions on which they came to light, and it is given to every session that writes. It asks not to be reduced to rules, and what follows is not a set of rules. It describes how each of those preferences bears on a mathematical paper.

*The purpose comes before the content.* The reader of a paper should know why something is there before reading it. The introduction asks the question before it gives the answer. A section says at its start what it establishes and where that will be used. A lemma is introduced by a sentence on what it is for. A long argument of several stages is preceded by a few sentences on how it goes. A reader who knows where an argument is going can check each step against that, and a reader who does not can only verify the steps one by one without seeing what they amount to.

*A proof begins with its first step.* The purpose of a proof is its statement, which the reader has just read, so a proof needs no opening of its own. The first sentence of a `proof` environment does something in the argument: it introduces a definition or a notation that the argument needs, makes a reduction ("It suffices to show …"), says what is proved first, or names the method as part of that step ("We prove … by induction on $n$"). No passage inside a proof has the order of the steps to come as its content. "We first bound …, then compare …, and finally …", "The proof rests on two facts …" and "The proof inserts … and then uses …" are passages of this kind. What becomes of such a plan depends on the length of the proof. A proof of less than about two pages, which is about 6000 characters of source, has no plan anywhere. A longer proof with distinct stages may have one. The plan then stands outside the proof, as the last sentences of the paragraph that precedes the statement, or, when the statement stands earlier and the proof is headed "Proof of …", in the paragraph before that proof. It has at most three sentences, speaks of the proof in the present or the future ("The proof first … It then …"), and refers to no equation that stands inside the proof. A sentence later in the proof does not refer back to a plan ("the second step", "the two facts announced above"), since the plan is not part of the proof. This is the practice of published papers, where a plan is given once, outside the proof or in the outline of the introduction, and the user decided that every paper of this command follows it.

*Plain, and fully explained.* The paper does not try to impress. It has no figures of speech, no announcements that something is remarkable or surprising, and no sentence that stages a turn. But plain does not mean short. The user's objection to ornament and the user's objection to compression turned out to have the same cause: both put an impression in the place of an explanation. Every step that the reader needs is written out in ordinary sentences, at the length that its content requires.

*What matters shows in how the paper is built.* The proofs of a paper do not all carry the same weight. One or two steps are where the argument could fail, and they are the reason the result was not obvious. Those steps receive the room. For such a step the paper says what the difficulty is, what idea meets it, and why that idea works, and then gives the argument in full. A routine verification is brief and is called routine. The outline in the plan names the step or the steps at which the difficulty lies, so that the outline in the introduction and the proofs in the body give it the same place. The weight is shown by position and development. A sentence that announces importance does not show it.

*One step at a time.* Each paragraph takes one step, and the reader is not asked to hold something that has not yet been explained. Between steps, and between sections, a sentence says how what was just shown leads to what comes next. This is a matter of order and connection. It is not a call for short paragraphs or clipped sentences.

*Detail is there to carry the idea.* Where the materials contain a small case or an example that makes an idea graspable, the paper can use it at the place where the idea is introduced. An example is included because it helps the reader to see the idea, and for no other reason. It stands in the running text or in a remark. The paper adds no mathematical claim that the materials do not support.

*Reasons, and not bare assertions.* Where a hypothesis is needed, the paper says where it is used. Where a definition could have been made differently, the paper says why it is made as it is. The explanations in the records are the source for this. The reader is then in a position to judge the argument and is not merely asked to accept it.

Two cautions from the same document apply to every session. A session does not adopt a policy that this method does not contain, such as a house style of its own or a section that it thinks a paper ought to have. And a session does not present a division of the material as if it were the only possible one when the mathematics does not impose it.

## The papers this paper is written like

This document describes a mathematical paper in words: its parts, its order, its manner. A description of this kind can say that an introduction states the main result and outlines the proof. It cannot show how the introductions of good papers do that, sentence by sentence. A session that has only the description writes what it imagines such a paper to be, and what it imagines agrees with real papers in outline and differs from them in many small ways that a mathematician notices at once. The user therefore decided that the sessions that plan and write the paper read real papers closely and write as those papers are written.

The skill keeps a collection of research articles for this purpose. They come from refereed journals, from different fields of mathematics, and they are of different lengths; each is the LaTeX source that its authors wrote. They were chosen for their writing and their structure, which are what a careful referee would call standard, and not for their results. The planning session receives the list of the collection as `exemplars.md`, with the field, the length and the abstract of each paper.

The planning session chooses from that list the papers that this paper is written like, and records their keys in the plan. It chooses those from which the most can be learned for the paper at hand. A paper near it in subject has a vocabulary and a way of arguing that fit. A paper near it in length is built as this one will be, since a paper of four pages and a paper of forty are built differently. The planning session reads each of the chosen papers whole, from the title to the references, before it plans, and writes down what it found, as described below. For each section it also names in the plan two of the chosen papers: those from which the most can be learned for a section of that kind.

A session that writes a part receives the planning session's account and the two papers that the plan names for its section, and it reads those two whole before it writes anything. It does not read all the chosen papers, which for six papers in full is about 450 kilobytes of reading for one section: the user decided to reduce this reading, by the goal that the section "What each session works from" reports. The reading of real papers itself is kept, since it is what makes the text read like a paper. A session that repairs a section has the same account and the same two papers, and the session that takes up what concerns no single section has the account and the papers of every section; a repair reads the papers before it rewrites text of the paper, as its prompt says.

The papers are read for how they are written, in everything that makes up a paper. How long the title is and what it names. What the abstract says and what it leaves to the introduction. With what the introduction opens, how soon and in what form it states the main result, how it places the result among earlier work, how it outlines the proof, and whether it ends by describing the sections. How a section begins. How a definition is introduced and a notation fixed. How a theorem is worded, and what is said just before it and just after it. How a proof opens, how it announces its steps, how it refers to what was proved earlier, and how it closes. How a formula stands inside its sentence and how it is punctuated. How much is said between two displays. How a remark or an example is used. How earlier work is cited. These are instances and not a list to work through. Whatever a mathematician would register as the way papers are written belongs to what is read for, including what none of these sentences names.

Reading for this is slower than reading for content. It means stopping at a sentence and asking what the sentence does at that place, and then looking at the same place in the other papers: what all of them do there, what most do, what only one does. What all or most of the papers do is the practice, and the paper follows it. What one paper does is a habit of its authors, and a paper that imitated it would be written like one paper and not like papers.

What is taken from the papers is their manner. No sentence of theirs is copied into the paper or paraphrased in it, and nothing of their mathematics enters it. The mathematics of this paper and the wording of its statements come from the materials, as the earlier sections say.

The planning session writes down what this reading found, as `study.md`: an account of how the chosen papers are written, part by part, in the respects named above and in whatever else it noticed. Where a passage shows a practice better than a description of it would, the account sets the passage down as it stands in the paper, so that whoever reads the account sees the practice itself. It lets sessions that cannot talk to each other write the parts of the paper from one reading of the same papers, and it is all that a writer has of the chosen papers that it does not read itself. The account therefore says, where the papers differ by the kind of section, what each kind does: how an introduction is built, and how a section of preliminaries, a section that carries a long computation and a section that proves the main theorem open, proceed and close. It does not replace the writer's own reading of its two papers, because an account says less than the papers do.

The decisions of this document hold where the papers do otherwise. What receives a number, what of the canon is left out, that the author line is the script's and carries the names the user gave or none: these come from what this paper is, and the sections above give their reasons. The papers show how to do well everything that those decisions leave to the writer, and that is most of what a reader sees.

## The LaTeX of a part

The manuscript is one file in the `amsart` class of the American Mathematical Society, which is what a journal in mathematics expects. The script writes everything that the parts have in common, so that the parts fit together whoever wrote them. It supplies:

- the preamble: the class, the package `amssymb`, and the environments. `amsart` itself brings the mathematics of `amsmath` and the `proof` environment;
- the environments `theorem`, `proposition`, `lemma`, `corollary` and `definition`, which share one counter within each section, so that a reader finds "Lemma 3.4" by its number alone; and the unnumbered environment `remark`;
- equation numbers within sections;
- the title from the plan; after it the author line, with `\author`, `\address` and `\email` for each author; the subject classification, with `\subjclass`, from the codes that whoever runs the command gives, and the key words of the plan, with `\keywords`, each only when it is given; the `abstract` environment around the text of the abstract; `\maketitle`; and the running head, which carries the short title of the plan, or the title when the plan gives none; a head that is too long for its line is left out, and the pages then carry their numbers alone;
- the heading of each section, with the label `sec:` followed by the id of the section in the plan;
- the bibliography commands, and `references.bib`, which is the bibliography of the materials.

A part is the body of one section, from the first line after its heading. It may contain subsections. It repeats nothing that the script supplies, and it defines no macro and no environment and loads no package. A macro that one part defined would be unknown to the others or would collide with theirs, so every formula is written out with the commands of LaTeX and of the AMS packages; a named operator is written with `\operatorname`. An operator is followed by its argument, as in `\operatorname{wt}(p)`. A quantity whose name has several letters and which stands by itself, such as a surplus called Sur, is written with `\mathrm`: set as an operator it takes the wrong spaces beside a plus or a minus sign.

Each numbered statement of the plan appears once, in the environment of its kind, in the section and at the position that the plan gives it, with the plan's label directly after the opening of the environment. A proof stands in the `proof` environment. It follows its statement, or, for a statement that the plan proves in a later section, it opens as `\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{...}]`. A numbered result from the literature carries its citation in the optional argument of its environment, as in `\begin{theorem}[{\cite[Theorem 2.1]{key}}]`, and has no proof. A proof that ends in a displayed formula places its end mark with `\qedhere`. Remarks are unnumbered, and a remark makes no claim that needs a proof.

A reference is written with its kind and a tie, as in `Theorem~\ref{thm:main}`, and a reference to an equation with `\eqref`. A citation gives the place in the source in the optional argument, as in `\cite[Theorem 2.1]{key}`, and the key is a key of the bibliography in the materials. A label names the mathematics and never the element. The prefix `sec:` belongs to the script. An equation takes `eq:` and a subsection `subsec:`, each followed by the id of the section and a hyphen, as in `eq:tilt-bound` in the section whose id is `tilt`. A part refers to no equation of another section, as the section "What holds the sections together" says, with one exception. Where two sections need the same displayed formula, each would display it under a number of its own. The plan therefore lists the formulas that more than one section needs, each with the section that displays it and with its label. That section displays the formula once under that label, and the others refer to it with `\eqref` and do not display it again.

A displayed formula that the text does not refer to is written with `\[ ... \]` or with a starred environment such as `align*`. One that the text refers to is written with `equation` or `align` and a label, with `\notag` on the lines of an `align` that are not referred to. A defined term is set with `\emph` at its definition. Text inside a formula is set with `\text`. A statement with several parts uses `enumerate`. The source is plain ASCII, with accented letters written by TeX's accent commands, so that every TeX engine typesets it. TeX does not break a displayed formula by itself, so a formula that is too long for one line of the page is broken over several lines with `multline` or `align`, starred or not by the rule just given. A formula with a number has less room than the line, because the number stands at the left margin and the formula has to clear it: of the 360 points of the line about 300 are left. A numbered formula that is broken keeps its number and its label, with `split` inside `equation` or with `multline`. The part leaves spacing and fonts to the class.

In running text a fraction is written with a slash, as in `$1/(k+1)$`, and built-up fractions, sums and products with limits and binomial coefficients are kept for displays, because in a paragraph they push the lines apart. A formula that is longer than about a third of a line is displayed. TeX breaks an inline formula only after a relation or an operation sign, and it does so inside a parenthesis as well; where a long formula leaves it no good place, the line is set loose or the formula is split in the middle of a parenthesis.

## Reading the manuscript beside real papers

This section is for a session that receives the manuscript `paper.tex` and several real papers, and it is the only part of this document that such a session receives.

A script has produced the manuscript with AI sessions. The people who will use it want it to be written as research articles in mathematics are written: in its structure, its wording, its manner and its flow, and in every other respect in which such papers resemble one another. You are asked whether it is. The sessions that wrote the text of the manuscript worked from other papers of the kind you have, and yours were not among them. The comparison is therefore with the practice of mathematical papers, and not with models from which the manuscript could have been copied.

Read the papers first, each from its title to its references, and attend to how they are written more than to what they prove: what the title names, what the abstract says, how the introduction opens and where it states the main result, how a section begins, how a theorem is worded and what stands around it, how a proof opens and closes, how a formula sits in its sentence, how earlier work is cited. Read the manuscript in the same way. Then compare the two place by place, its title with their titles, its abstract with their abstracts, the opening of its introduction with the openings of theirs, and so on to the last page; and compare them as wholes: the proportions of the parts, the length of the paragraphs, how much is explained and how much is left to the reader, how the text moves from one thing to the next.

A departure is a place where the manuscript is written otherwise than these papers are written, in a way that a mathematician reading it would notice. The manuscript may do something that none of the papers does. It may lack something that the papers have. It may do what they do in another manner: at greater length, more explicitly, more stiffly, in other words. Report each departure with its evidence. Give the place in the manuscript and quote the passage exactly. Quote exactly, from at least two of the papers, passages that show what the papers do at such a place. The passages need not do the same thing: where the papers go different ways and the manuscript goes none of them, passages that show two of those ways are the evidence. And describe the difference, saying what a paper written like these would have at that place. Two papers are asked for because one paper shows a habit of its authors, while two that agree begin to show a practice. Where the departure is that something is missing, the manuscript has no passage to quote, and you name the place where the missing thing would stand.

Not every difference is a departure. The papers differ among themselves. Where they do, the manuscript is within their practice as long as it does what one of them does, and this is not a departure even when the other papers agree with each other on something else. Its subject is its own, and so is its length: a short paper lacks what only a long paper needs. The following are as they are by decision and are not reported. The author line is written by the script from the names that the user of the script gave, and the manuscript has no author line when the user gave none. The manuscript has no acknowledgements, no section of open problems and no account of other approaches. And it does not say that a result is new.

Say also, in a few sentences, in what the manuscript is like the papers, since a list of departures alone does not show what was compared. If you find no departure, say so. That is a result as much as a departure is.

## What each session works from

The user set the goal by which the arrangement below was made: the quality is kept, and the effort is reduced. On a long proof the effort is large when every session is given everything. With several hundred elements and some fifteen sections, the whole materials, the chosen papers in full and the whole plan come to about two megabytes. A session that writes one section from them produces twenty to thirty kilobytes of LaTeX in about twenty minutes, and the records of the elements that its section states, proves or absorbs are at most about a tenth of those two megabytes. A planning session that holds the whole materials and the papers at once ends close to the limit of what one session can hold. So each session is given what its own work needs, and sessions whose work does not depend on each other run at the same time. The checks that carry the quality stay as they are, and the section "What is checked after writing" describes them.

The planning session receives a digest of the materials. The digest gives every element by what it says: its name, its kind and weight, its short name, its description, its statement in words and in Lean, what it names and what names it. It leaves out the explanation and the proof in words of each element and gives their lengths, since most of the materials consists of these, and since the plan decides how each element appears, for which what the element says and what it rests on is nearly always enough. Two records are in the digest in full, because the account of the whole proof is in them: the root's, and that of the declaration whose formal proof settles the root. When the main result is another theorem, its record is in the digest in full as well. Nothing is withheld from the planning session by this. The full record of every element is in its folder as a file of its own, and it opens the file of an element whenever a decision needs the proof or the explanation, for example to judge whether a step is routine or how long an argument is.

A session that writes a section receives the brief of that section. The brief holds, from the plan, the outline of the proof, the sections of the paper, the notation, the numbered statements of the whole paper with their labels, and what the plan puts into this section. It holds, from the materials, the full records of the section's own elements, that is, of the elements that the plan has the section state, prove, absorb or cite, in an order in which each comes after what it depends on. And for every element of another section that these name, in a statement or in a proof that this section writes, it holds what that element says, the place where the paper has it, and the symbol or name under which the paper knows it. A writer uses a result of another section by its statement and does not need its proof, as an author does who cites a lemma. There are three cases in which the brief gives more than the statement of an element that belongs elsewhere. A step that another section absorbs has no number by which this section could refer to it, so where a proof of this section uses it, its content has to be written at that place, and the brief gives its record in full. An element that the paper does not state is written nowhere, and the brief of every section whose elements name it gives its record together with the element that stands in its place. And the brief of the introduction gives in full the records of the elements that the proof of the main result names directly, because the outline of the proof is drawn from their explanations. For every other case in which a statement turns out not to be enough, the writer has the full record of every element in its folder, one file each, and the root with its record and the account of the problem as a whole as a further file.

The introduction is written first and alone, because it defines the objects of the problem and states the main result, and every other writer receives it. The other sections are then written side by side, as many at once as the command was told, and a writer receives as well the parts that happened to be finished when it started. It may consult them for how the paper speaks. It cannot rely on a section that it does not have, and it cannot know which those will be. So the sections do not depend on each other's text, and what holds them together is the plan alone.

## What is checked after writing

The sessions write without being able to see the whole, so the whole is checked afterwards, and each check returns what it finds to a session that can repair it.

The script checks by machine what a machine can check. Every numbered statement of the manuscript is one that the plan names, of the planned kind, in the planned place. Its proof stands where the plan puts it, and a result from the literature carries its citation and has no proof. Every reference and every citation resolves. No Lean name of the canon appears in the manuscript, and neither do the words Lean and Mathlib. A part repeats nothing that the script supplies, defines no macro and no environment, loads no package and is plain ASCII, as the section "The LaTeX of a part" requires. Then the script typesets the manuscript with TeX. Failures of either kind go to repair, and the manuscript is assembled and checked again. A line that TeX set more than a few points wider than the text block, so that it stands out into the margin, goes to repair in the same way. So does a displayed formula that has less room on its line than it needs. TeX gives no warning for it: it closes up the spaces inside the formula, or moves the number of the formula to a line of its own. The script finds such formulas by typesetting the manuscript a second time, only to measure it. If such a line or formula is still there after the repairs, the paper is typeset with it and the command reports it. A repair changes the text of the parts and keeps the plan. So a line that is too wide in what the script supplies, that is, in the title, a heading, the author line or the bibliography, goes to no repair, and the command reports it as well.

A repair is made section by section, here and after each of the readings below. What a check or a reader found in one section goes to a session that repairs that section and no other. That session has what the section was written from, the brief and the two papers of the section, together with the manuscript and the findings, and the sessions of different sections work at the same time. The machine checks know the part in which a failure lies, the comparison knows the section of a statement, and a session that reads the manuscript names the section of each place it reports. What concerns no single section, such as the title or the proportions of the whole, goes to one session that may change every part and that works after the others, on the manuscript as they left it. That session has the manuscript, the plan and the record of every element. An error of the typesetting also goes to it, because TeX reports the line at which it noticed an error, and the cause can lie in an earlier part.

After that, a session that has the manuscript and the section "The reader" of this document, and nothing else, reads the paper as that reader. It reports what it understood the paper to prove and every place where the reading failed. The findings go to repair as just described. After the sessions of the sections, the session that may change every part compares what the reader understood with the research, that is, with the root and the account of the problem as a whole, the outline of the plan, and the records of the main result and of what its proof names; where the reader understood something else, the manuscript did not convey it, and that session revises it. This comparison is made after every reading whose findings go to a repair of this kind, also when each of the findings lies in one section. A reader who has seen nothing but the paper is the only test of whether the paper stands by itself, because every session that wrote it knew more than the paper says. The reading is repeated on the repaired manuscript, a bounded number of times. What the last of these readings finds is repaired as well, and this repair is of another kind, because no reading of the whole manuscript follows it. A repair can lose a step of a proof while it improves a sentence, as when a repair that shortens a proof leaves it arguing a bound at one value of a parameter where the bound is needed for every value, and the reading that follows a repair is what notices this. The last repair is therefore narrow. A session changes the passage that a finding names, as far as the finding requires, and leaves every other passage as it is; where a finding can be answered only by more than that, the session keeps the text and says what would have to change. The comparison of what the reader understood with the research is not made in this repair, because what it finds is answered by revising passages that no finding names. Then one session reads what was changed. It has the manuscript and every passage that differs from the text which the last reader read, each with its text before and after. It reads each changed passage where it stands and says whether the change is sound, that is, whether every step is still there, every statement still says what it said, and the notation and the references still agree with the rest; and it says whether the change answers the finding it was made for. A change that is not sound is put back as the last reader read it, together with the other changes that were made for the same finding. No further round follows. In this way every passage of the paper is text that such a reader has read, or a change that was read where it stands and found sound. What this last repair did not settle stays open for the time being, each finding with the reason: the session kept the text, or the change was put back, or the change is sound and stays and does not answer the finding. The session that resolves what the readings leave open, described below, takes it up.

Then the manuscript is read beside real papers, as the section "Reading the manuscript beside real papers" describes. A session that has the manuscript and a few papers of the collection that the plan did not choose, so that no session that wrote the text has seen them, reports the places where the manuscript is written otherwise than those papers. Repair sessions take up what it reports, and another session reads the repaired manuscript with other papers. The readings go on until two sessions in succession, each with papers of its own, have found no departure, and there are at most eight of them. One reader with one set of papers can miss what another sees, and two that agree are the test that the user asked for. When the eighth reading still reports departures, they are repaired in the narrow way just described, a session reads what was changed, and what that leaves stays open. When the eighth reading reports none and the seventh reported some, those were repaired, and one reading has confirmed the repair where two are asked for; they stay open with that reason. What stays open here is taken up by the same resolving session.

A repair that follows one of these readings rewrites passages for their manner, and a rewriting can change what a proof says or take out a step that the reader needs. The machine checks do not see such a change. So once the readings beside real papers have ended, and if a repair that followed one of them changed the text, the reading described two paragraphs above, by a session that has only the manuscript and the section "The reader", is made again in the same way. The narrow repair after an eighth reading is not such a rewriting, since every passage it changed has been read where it stands; it does not by itself cause this reading.

Then the comparison described in the section "Every numbered statement says what its element says" is made, for the sections at the same time. A repair of what the comparison finds corrects a statement and adjusts its proof, so when such a repair has changed the text, the same reading is made once more before the statements are compared again. The session that corrects a statement changes its own section. A use of the statement in another section was written from the statement of the element, which is what the corrected statement now says, and the reading that follows is of the whole manuscript. A statement that a narrow repair changed is compared here like every other. No repair follows the last comparison, and a statement that still differs then is reported with the paper as open.

The readings can leave findings open, and one session then resolves them. A narrow repair changes one passage, and some findings cannot be answered in one passage. A reader beside real papers finds that a section gives a full proof where papers give a sentence, or that two sections do what papers do in one. A reader of the manuscript finds a statement that stands before the notion it needs. Every repair up to this point keeps the plan, so none of them can answer such a finding. When a session outside the command resolves such findings, the command can no longer be run on that paper. The resolving session is therefore part of the command. It has what the session that may change every part has, and every open finding of the readings with the reason it was left open. It is not bound to the plan. It may rewrite a part. It may move a numbered statement, change its kind, take away its number and write its content into the running text or into the proof that uses it, and give a number to an element that the plan absorbed. It may join sections or divide one, and change titles and notation. When it changes the structure it returns the plan that says what the manuscript now is. Four things it may not do, because each would separate the paper from what was proved or undo a decision of this document: add a numbered statement that states no element, change what a statement says, cite a work that is not in the bibliography of the materials, and mention Lean. It answers every finding, by a change or by its reason for leaving the text as it is. The script holds a returned plan to the check that the first plan passed, and the manuscript to the machine checks and the typesetting. Because this session may rewrite, the manuscript is then read once more, when the session has changed a part, by a session that has only the manuscript, in the way described above, and that reading ends in a narrow repair like the others. With that the findings of the readings are closed. Those that were answered with a reason, by the resolving session or by that last narrow repair, are listed with the paper, each with its reason, for the user to judge. They are not reported as open.

Then the running text is compared with the elements, as the section "What the running text asserts" requires. The numbered statements whose text has changed since they were compared are compared first, once, and one that differs then is reported as open. Then, for the sections at the same time, a session that has one section, the records in full of that section's elements and the statements of all other elements goes through the running text of the section, with the abstract for the first section, and through the displayed formulas of its proofs to which text outside the proof refers. It reports every sentence or formula that asserts mathematics which no element states, with the element whose statement comes nearest. In the first section it holds what the abstract says of the main result to the element of the main result, with the range of every variable, as a numbered statement is held to its element. And when the paper is written from a draft that carries the quotations from the cited works, it reports every sentence about a cited work that no quotation supports, as the section "Results from the literature" requires. What it reports is repaired in the narrow way. The sentence is cut, or it is reworded so that it says no more than that element, or no more of the work than a quotation shows; no argument is written in its place. One kind of passage has a third answer: a derivation that a proof of the section uses and that stands outside that proof is moved into it, as it is. The script accepts a move when the reported passage stands inside a proof word for word and no longer outside one, and when every formula of it that carries a label stands before the places that refer to it. A session reads the changed passages as after every narrow repair, and the running text of the sections that were changed is compared once more. What is reported then, and what the repair left as it was, is reported with the paper as open.

Last comes a check of the elements that have no numbered statement. The correspondence file says of each of them where the plan put it: into which proof or into which section it was absorbed, or which section cites it. The writers wrote the sections from the plan, and a repair can reword such a sentence, move it into a proof or cut it. So when a repair has changed a part since the sections were written, one session reads the manuscript and says of each of these elements whether the manuscript has its content, and where, and the correspondence file carries that beside the place the plan gave. Nothing is repaired for it. When a later pass changes a part, the check is made again.

Each of these loops is bounded: it runs at most a fixed number of times. What is still open at the end is not hidden: the log that the command leaves beside the paper lists every session that was launched and what every check found, and the command reports findings that remain. What can remain open is a numbered statement that differs from its element or was not compared, a sentence of the running text that no element covers, a line that is too wide for the page, and a failure of the machine checks or of the typesetting that their repairs did not remove. With such a failure the command ends where the failure stayed, without the readings and the comparisons that would have followed, and it does not take that manuscript up again. The paper, the plan, the correspondence file and the log are what the user receives, and the user confirms the result by reading them. The user can also have the command take up a finished paper again and act on the findings that remain, on all of them or on those the user chooses. They are then repaired in the narrow way, a session reads what was changed, the numbered statements whose text changed are compared with their elements again, and the running text of the sections that were changed is compared with the elements again.
