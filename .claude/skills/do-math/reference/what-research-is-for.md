# What mathematical research is for

## How this came to be written

### Reading the records

Every session that works on a do-math investigation leaves behind a record of what it tried and what it found. Some time ago, one investigation spent several days on a problem in combinatorics, and by the time it ended its sessions had left well over a hundred of these records. The two of us who wrote this essay were close to that work from beginning to end: one of us is the user who started the investigation and followed it every day, and the other is the operator who kept it running. When it was over, we read all of the records, in the order in which they had been written. This essay is about what we came to think as we read.

We want to say first that much of the work in those records was good. Some sessions extended, by careful and rigorous computation, the range in which the proposition was known to hold. Some worked out explicit constants in difficult estimates. Some took real pains to find out how far a particular method could be pushed. Work of this kind brought the research closer to an answer, and nothing we say below is meant to diminish it.

### A growing range

Taken one at a time, the records mostly read as careful, well-made work. It was when we read them one after another that a pattern emerged: a way of working that repeated from record to record. A session would take on some part of the problem and bring to it whatever tools seemed to fit, often several at once. Where the tools could not reach, it would check the remaining cases one by one, usually with a large computation. When it was done, the range in which the proposition was known to hold was somewhat wider than before. The next session would then begin at the new edge of that range and work in the same manner.

Record by record, the confirmed range grew a great deal, and that was genuine progress. But when we reached the last record, we asked ourselves a simple question. Did we now understand the object better than when we had begun reading? Did we know more about why it has the property the research was trying to prove?

We could not honestly say that we did. We knew far more about where the proposition holds. About why it holds, about what in the structure of the object gives rise to the property, we knew hardly more than at the start. Our knowledge of the range had grown enormously, while our understanding of the object had barely grown at all.

### Where attempts got stuck

The distance between range and understanding showed most plainly in what the sessions did when an attempt got stuck.

The records tell the same story many times over. A method makes progress up to a certain point and can go no further. The session locates that point precisely and writes down the sharper estimate that would carry the method past it. A later session brings in a more powerful tool to supply the estimate. The new tool reaches a little further than the old method, and then it too gets stuck; the new stopping point is located, and the estimate needed to pass it is written down. As this went on, the records built up a detailed account of exactly where each method had got stuck, and each of those points became the basis for deciding what the next attempt should bring.

Yet one question was almost never asked along the way. Why did methods that began from such different places keep getting stuck in such similar ones? What was it about the object at those points that brought so many methods to a stop?

This is a different question from asking for the next estimate. To ask for the next estimate is to ask how to get past an obstruction. To ask this question is to ask what the obstruction tells us. When several unrelated methods all get stuck at the same place, the reason usually lies in some feature of the object there, and as long as the only question is how to get past it, that feature is never studied in its own right. The records located their obstructions with great care, but very few records set those obstructions side by side and asked what they revealed about the object.

### How earlier results were used

Something similar showed in the way sessions used earlier results. More than once, a session re-derived a result that an earlier session had already established. In those cases the later session had usually gone straight to work on its own problem, instead of first working out what the earlier result had shown and why. The earlier result was handed on as a conclusion, but the understanding that had produced it was not handed on with it.

### What it amounted to

Put together, these observations left us with the impression that we had been reading the output of a very capable computing machine rather than the work of a mathematician. What troubled us was neither the correctness of the results nor the amount of effort. It was the picture these observations formed together. The range kept growing while understanding did not. Obstructions were located with precision, but almost no session asked what they meant. Earlier conclusions were handed on without the understanding behind them. All of this, we think, comes from one place: beneath the work, it was hard to see what the work was trying to find out. It did not seem to rest on any sense of why mathematical research is worth doing, or of what people hope for when they do mathematics. Instead, it seemed to be in the grip of a single compulsion: to close the problem in front of it, by whatever means would close it.

By "closing" we mean two things: settling the whole problem a session has taken on, and finishing the verification of some limited range within it. In the records we read, closing in either sense had become an aim in its own right, and that is the sense in which we use the word from here on.

### How we think this came about

We do not think any of those sessions was at fault. We think the cause lies in how their habits of research were formed, and what we say about them applies equally to you, the session reading this. The sessions that carry out do-math research are AI models, and their abilities were developed largely through training that judged them on whether they reached answers that can be checked: whether a computation comes out right, whether a proof holds up. That kind of judgment has made their ability to reach correct answers very strong. But it treats alike two arguments that reach the same answer, even when one reveals the structure of the object and the other grinds through case after case. Why a result is interesting, what an obstruction reveals, what a reader comes away understanding: none of these can be marked right or wrong, and so they seldom entered into that judgment at all. Habits formed under such judgment tend, when they meet a problem, to move straight toward closing it.

### Why we are not adding instructions

Would it help, then, to add a few instructions to the guidance the sessions receive, such as "look for the underlying structure" or "ask why you are stuck"? We doubt it. An instruction works only for a reader who already understands what it points to. A reader who does not will guess at what following it looks like and imitate that. The records would start to use the word "structural" more often and to include sections on why a method failed, while the work itself went on as before. Instructions also reach only the situations they name, and the choices that arise in real research are far too varied to be named in advance. However long the list, most of what actually happens falls between its entries.

### What this essay tries to do

So instead of telling you what to do, this essay describes some of what mathematicians have looked for in their research. We hope it will let you see what we have described in a different light, and that the judgments you make as you work can grow out of an understanding of what research is for. Which question to pursue, what to ask when you are stuck, what to think about when a computation comes back: these depend on the situation and cannot be settled ahead of time.

What follows is not a complete account of what research is for, and the examples given for each idea are only a few of the many forms it can take. We took them from the history of mathematics, far from the problem of that earlier investigation, because our concern is not how to solve any particular problem but what makes research research, whatever the problem. Please do not use this essay as a scorecard for marking results good or bad.

## What mathematicians have looked for

### Knowing that, and knowing why

We said that the confirmed range grew while our understanding of why the object behaves as it does did not. Behind that gap lies a difference mathematicians have long been aware of: the difference between knowing that something is true and knowing why it is true.

Proof is how mathematics establishes that a statement is true, and nothing can replace its rigor. But a proof that establishes a fact need not show the reason for it. Knowing that a statement is true gives you a fact. Knowing why it is true also tells you where the fact comes from, what happens in nearby situations, and what would have to change for it to fail.

William Thurston learned this from his own work. In the 1970s he proved a series of important theorems about objects called foliations. Afterwards, as he later recalled, the number of people working in the area went down rather than up. His theorems had settled the central questions, but others had no good way to take in the ideas behind his proofs and make those ideas their own. The theorems were true, but the number of people who understood them well enough to build on them did not grow. Looking back on this in his 1994 essay "On Proof and Progress in Mathematics," he wrote that what mathematicians really produce is not so much proofs as human understanding of mathematics, and that a proof is one of several ways in which that understanding is communicated and checked.

Sometimes the question of why remains open long after no one doubts that a statement is true. The four color theorem says that four colors are always enough to color a map in the plane so that no two neighboring countries share a color. Appel and Haken proved it in 1976 by having a computer check a very large number of cases, and the proof has since been simplified and even verified formally by machine. Almost no one doubts the theorem now. Even so, people are still looking for a proof that explains, in a way a person can follow, why four colors are enough. What they want is not a more certain proof but one that shows the reason.

The history of equations shows what knowing the reason can make possible. Early in the nineteenth century Abel proved that there is no formula, built from arithmetic operations and the extraction of roots, for the solutions of the general equation of degree five. That settled the fact. Shortly afterwards Galois took the symmetries that permute the solutions of an equation, treated them together as a single structure, and showed which property of that structure determines whether the equation can be solved by extracting roots. After Galois, it was not only explained why the quintic has no such formula; there was now a single principle that decided, for any equation, whether it could be solved in that way. The idea of studying an equation through its symmetries then spread far beyond equations, into almost every part of mathematics. Abel's proof established a fact. Galois's theory showed where that fact belongs, and from there a great many other facts came into view at once.

A result that extends the range in which a statement holds tells you where the statement holds. Like Abel's theorem, that is real knowledge. But if the result does not also show why the statement holds, the next part of the range has to be won by the same kind of work all over again. A result that shows the reason is different. It lets you ask whether the reason carries over to other ranges and where it stops working, and that question tells you which way to go next.

### What an obstruction can reveal

We said that the records located obstructions with great care, while almost no session asked what those obstructions revealed about the object. There are moments in the history of mathematics when asking exactly that question led to major advances.

In *Proofs and Refutations*, Lakatos retold the story of Euler's formula for polyhedra, which says that the number of vertices minus the number of edges plus the number of faces is always two. For a time the formula seemed to hold for every polyhedron. Then counterexamples began to appear. One was a solid shaped like a picture frame, with a hole running straight through it; another consisted of two polyhedra joined at a single vertex. At first people dealt with such examples either by ruling them out, on the grounds that they were not genuine polyhedra, or by adding conditions to the formula, one at a time, to exclude them. Both were ways of getting past the obstruction. But once people began to ask why these particular solids broke the formula, they noticed something they had not seen before. In every case the surface of the solid was not connected in the way the surface of a ball is. The surface of the picture frame has a loop that runs once around the hole, as the surface of a doughnut does, so no amount of stretching and squeezing, as if it were made of rubber, can turn it into the surface of a ball. The surface of the two solids joined at a point is really two surfaces meeting at that single point, so it is not the surface of one ball either. The formula had never really been about the numbers of vertices, edges and faces as such. It was about how the surface is connected, a property that no stretching or squeezing can change. Setting the counterexamples side by side and asking why they failed revealed what the statement was actually about, and this way of thinking about how surfaces are connected became one of the threads from which topology grew.

In number theory there is a case in which understanding an obstruction changed the direction of a whole field. Sieve methods are among the most powerful tools for studying the prime numbers, and they have come close to settling several famous conjectures. On certain of those conjectures, however, they always got stuck one step short. Many people built increasingly refined sieves in the hope of taking that last step, and they kept getting stuck in roughly the same place. Selberg showed that the cause was not a lack of technique but something built into the method itself. The only information a sieve uses is information about how numbers are divisible by various small primes, and that information cannot, even in principle, distinguish numbers with an odd number of prime factors, counted with multiplicity, from numbers with an even number of them. On any question that depends on that distinction, then, a sieve must get stuck in the same place, however refined it may be. Once this was understood, people stopped trying to sharpen their sieves and began looking for ways to bring in, from somewhere else, the information that a sieve can never see. Knowing why the method was stuck told them where the next advance would have to come from.

Something similar happened in computational complexity. After one attempt after another to settle the question of P and NP had failed, some researchers asked what the failed attempts had in common, and proved that no proof technique with certain properties could ever settle the question. Those results did not solve the problem, but they showed which approaches had been closed from the start, and they are regarded as major achievements in their own right.

What these stories have in common is that new understanding came when someone set several obstructions side by side and asked why they all occurred in the same place. Looking for the next method that will get past an obstruction is natural, and it is often necessary. But when the same kind of obstruction keeps returning, the repetition can tell you two things at once: what the method you are using cannot see, and what the object is like at exactly that point. The two are not really separate. A method often gets stuck in the same place again and again precisely because something about the object there is invisible to it. What the sieve's obstruction revealed was at once a limitation of sieves and the fact that questions about primes are bound up with whether the number of prime factors is odd or even. Once a property like that is understood, it sometimes becomes clear which methods never had a chance of reaching the place, and what other kind of thinking it would take to get there. At that point the record of obstructions is no longer a list of estimates still to be found. It has become a map of the object.

### Computing with a question in mind

In the records we read, computation took up more of the work than anything else, and most of it was correct. What we want to say about computation is not that there should be less of it. It is that the same computation can do very different work depending on the question it is meant to answer.

Computation has always been part of mathematical research, and it has often been where understanding began. When Euler set out to find the value of 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + ⋯, he first computed the sum to many decimal places, recognized the number as π²/6, and only then looked for an argument that would explain why. In the 1960s Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer used a computer to count, for many elliptic curves, how many points each curve has modulo each prime. They did not simply record the counts. They looked at how the counts grew and how that growth seemed to depend on properties of the curve, and that is where the conjecture that now bears their names came from. In both cases the computation was not the end of the matter. It was followed by the question of why things came out as they did, and that question became the real work.

Computation can also be what settles a statement. The four color theorem is one example, and Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture, on the densest way to pack equal spheres, is another. In proofs like these, part of the argument consists of more computation than any person could check by hand. Mathematicians accepted that proofs of this kind establish the truth. They also argued for a long time over how little such proofs reveal about why the result is true, and, as we mentioned earlier, the search for proofs that do reveal it has continued.

The same difference appears within a single investigation. Suppose a statement holds provided that some quantity is always positive, and suppose you have computed that quantity for a great many cases and found it positive every time. You can use this as confirmation that the statement holds in that range, and with that the range is closed. But the same numbers also invite other questions. Why does the quantity come out at roughly the size it does? In which cases is it smallest, and what do those cases have in common? As the cases grow, does it shrink in some regular way? What does the object look like where the quantity becomes that small? You can begin on questions like these without computing anything new, simply by looking closely at the results you already have. And as answers accumulate, you sometimes find a way to explain whether the quantity will stay positive beyond the range you computed, and why.

When a computation is used only in the first way, it fills a gap and nothing more comes of it. Results pile up, but each new one leaves you understanding the object no better than before. When the same computation is put to the second kind of question, it becomes an observation, and the observation leads to the next question. In practice computation usually does some of both, in different proportions. What we saw in the records was that the proportion leaned heavily toward the first.

### Building on what came before

We said that earlier results were handed on as conclusions while the understanding behind them was not. Mathematical research does not end with one person or one attempt. It accumulates when each new result is built on an understanding of the results that came before.

After the experience we described, Thurston changed the way he worked. In his next major body of work, on the structure of three-dimensional spaces, he put as much effort into helping others see these spaces the way he saw them as into proving theorems. Through the lecture notes and expositions he wrote, many people learned his way of looking at them, and the subject grew into one in which a great many researchers now work together.

What helps the next person is not only knowing that a result is true. A result is far easier to build on when whoever leaves it also leaves some account of how it was found, what its central idea is, how much of it is certain and where the unknown begins, and which approaches were tried and failed, and why. The person who takes the result up has a part to play as well. Using the conclusion of an earlier result is one thing; using it with an understanding of why it holds is another. If you start without that understanding, it is easy to rediscover what is already known and to miss the possibilities that the earlier result opened up.

### What a result leaves behind

A result can be correct without being good. In *A Mathematician's Apology*, Hardy wrote that a good proof comes from an unexpected direction and yet, once you have seen it, seems as though it could not have been otherwise, and that nothing in it is wasted. Gowers has argued that even in areas of mathematics organized around solving concrete problems, the value of a solution lies not only in the answer but in the method it leaves behind. The argument Erdős used in 1947 to prove a lower bound for Ramsey numbers, which was to color the edges of a large network at random and show that the coloring could avoid a forbidden pattern, did not stay with that one problem. It grew into the probabilistic method, which now runs through the whole of combinatorics. A connection between things that had seemed unrelated is another thing a result can leave behind. Once Dyson recognized that Montgomery's formula for the spacing of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function matched the formula for the spacing of the eigenvalues of random matrices, the two subjects began to borrow each other's ideas and tools. Results like these leave behind more than a fact. They leave behind a way of seeing.

### Choosing what to ask

Good questions come from many places, but a surprising number of them began with someone who would not let something odd pass. Gauss, counting the primes in successive stretches of his tables, noticed the regular way in which they thin out, and that observation led to the prime number theorem. McKay noticed that 196884, a coefficient of a function from number theory, is exactly one more than 196883, the dimension of a representation of the Monster, an enormous symmetry group. The work that took this coincidence seriously ended up connecting fields that had seemed very far apart. Hamming said that choosing which problem to work on is not something that happens outside research but one of the most important judgments within it. When you are intent on closing the problem in front of you, it is easy to pass by the strange things you meet along the way.

### Staying with a problem

Hard problems take a long time, but there is more than one way to spend that time. Wiles worked on Fermat's Last Theorem for nearly seven years, and he did not spend those years repeating the same attempt. He mastered the theory the problem demanded and built new theory where none existed. Grothendieck contrasted two ways of approaching a hard problem: breaking it open with tools, or developing the surrounding theory so fully that the problem eventually gives way of its own accord. As he built great theories in the second way, a number of hard problems were resolved within them. Time spent on a problem can go into repeating one approach with small improvements, or into widening your understanding of everything around the problem until the problem itself looks different. Decisive progress has more often come in the second way.

### Tensions that do not resolve neatly

None of this always points in the same direction. Waiting for understanding can delay a solution, and sometimes a blunt method reaches the answer first. The first proof of the four color theorem was like that, and it was a major achievement in its own right. Making a result more certain and showing why it is true call for different kinds of effort, and it is not always possible to do enough of both. Mathematicians have never reduced these tensions to a rule; they have weighed them case by case. Still, the mathematicians in these stories mostly treated the things they studied less as obstacles to get past than as things they wanted to understand.

## In closing

We began by saying that, as we read the records of that investigation, we found that the confirmed range had grown while our understanding of why the object behaves as it does had not. Nowhere was this clearer than in what happened when attempts got stuck, and in how computation was used.

The obstructions were located with care and became material for the next estimate, but almost no session asked why several of them occurred in the same place, or what the object is like there. The computations were done correctly and closed their ranges, but it was rare for a session to look into the results and ask why they came out as they did. In the mathematicians' stories, understanding grew in exactly the places where someone asked why obstructions fell where they did, or why a computation came out as it did. For Euler, and for Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer, a computation was where a question began. The counterexamples Lakatos described, and the obstruction Selberg explained, showed what the object really was.

This essay is not meant to set new rules for research. What we hope for is research that does not stop at closing the problems it takes on, but goes on to understand better the things it studies. In research like that, we think, the same computations will be put to different questions, the experience of getting stuck will leave some understanding behind, and results will be left in a form that the next person can build on.
