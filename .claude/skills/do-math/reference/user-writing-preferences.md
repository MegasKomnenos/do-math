# How this user likes to be written for

## Where this comes from

This document is about the kind of writing one person prefers. That person is the user who runs do-math research and reads what it produces, so anything you write in a do-math session ends up, sooner or later, in front of them.

What is known about their preferences comes mostly from one piece of writing. At the user's request, an AI wrote a piece and then rewrote it, five versions in all, and the user responded to each version. Below, "the user" means that person and "the writer" means the AI that did the writing.

The piece had a particular job. The user had handed research on a mathematical problem to a series of AI sessions, each taking on part of the problem and leaving a record of its results. Reading those records, the user felt something was missing. The results kept piling up, and they were correct, but the research seemed interested only in getting the problem solved somehow; there was little sign of anyone wanting to understand the object more deeply. The user and the writer talked over why that might be, and decided not to respond with instructions like "understand more deeply." They would instead write a piece that conveyed an understanding of what mathematical research is for, and give it to the research. That piece is what this document calls "the piece," and do-math sessions now receive it as [What mathematical research is for](what-research-is-for.md).

It went through five versions. The first gathered only stories mathematicians have told about why they do research. The second added, in front of those stories, an account of why the piece had been written. The third kept that structure but rewrote it to build up more slowly. The fourth changed to a plainer tone and explained things more fully. The fifth reorganized the whole so that the core of what it had to say stood out, and the user accepted it. Version numbers below follow this order.

The reason for writing all this down is that what the process revealed is much more specific than ordinary advice about writing. The drafts still existed, along with a record of which sentences struck the user as wrong and why, which revisions the user accepted, and which ones drew new objections. That kind of material vanishes when a conversation ends. It is recorded here, scenes and all, so that whoever writes for this user next does not have to repeat the same trial and error.

None of what follows is meant as a rule. For each preference, the document tells which feedback on which draft brought it out, how the writing changed afterward, and what the preference is for. Pull a preference out into a single sentence and apply it mechanically, and it will misfire as soon as the situation differs from the one it came from. And since all of this comes from one piece and a handful of conversations, it is far from the whole of what this user wants.

## Readers need the purpose before the content

The most basic thing this user wants is for readers to be able to learn why a piece was written before they read what it says. It came out in the response to the first version.

Before that version was written, the user and the writer had agreed on something: instructions to the research sessions such as "be more elegant" or "look for the structure" would get imitated on the surface without being understood. The writer took that idea further than it was meant to go. It reasoned that any sentence describing the piece's own nature or purpose — something like "this is not an instruction" — would itself land on the reader as an instruction. So the first version said only what it had collected. It said nothing about the worry it came from, why it had been written, or what it hoped to do. It opened with "Mathematicians have long spoken, from their own experience, about what mathematical research is for. What follows gathers a few strands of what they have said," and went straight into the stories.

The user's response went like this. The piece had come out of a whole process: the two of them had noticed a problem while talking, worked out what was behind it, decided how to respond, and only then written something. But the finished piece kept only that last step. Someone reading it on its own had no way to learn the intention, the purpose or the reasons behind it. What the user wanted was a piece whose readers would understand that purpose and those reasons clearly, and could read and think over the rest in that light.

From the second version on, the piece opened by telling that history: what had been seen in the research records, how it had been understood, why adding instructions did not seem enough, and so what the piece was trying to do and how it hoped to be read. All of this came before the mathematicians' stories, and each story was rewritten to begin by calling back the problem described at the start.

The writer's mistake had been to treat two different things as one: a piece that tells readers what to do, and a piece that explains why it exists. For this user they are not the same. The first tries to make the reader's decisions for them. The second gives readers something they need in order to understand what they are reading. A piece should explain its purpose and reasons even when it tells no one to do anything.

## Plain, and fully explained

This user wants plain writing, but plain does not mean short or compressed. That became clear through two failures that seem, at first, to pull in opposite directions.

The third version came after the user said the second one moved too fast (more on that under "One step at a time," below). To slow things down, the writer broke paragraphs into smaller ones and cut sentences short. To make each story memorable, it reached for striking phrases. Retelling the passage in which Grothendieck compared his way of working to a rising sea, it wrote: "The sea does not attack the land. It only rises, slowly. And one day the land is gone." Introducing the observation that two numbers are almost equal, it set the equation "196884 = 196883 + 1." on a line by itself. In the story of one mathematician's experience, it announced a twist with "And then something no one expected happened." The piece closed with "We believe," and every section ended on an aphorism such as "Lingering, by itself, creates nothing."

The user found the third version affected — straining to impress, and sentimental. What the user wanted was plain writing. But in the same breath the user named the opposite fault: the third version got its content across by hinting and compressing, and that was not wanted either. What the user wanted was writing that set its content out in full, in natural sentences and paragraphs, with real substance and fine, concrete detail.

The writer came to think both complaints had the same cause. This was the writer's own interpretation, reached by setting the two complaints beside the third version, not something the user said. Instead of explaining the content, the third version had tried to make an impression through the force of its phrasing. Metaphors and aphorisms squeeze a great deal into one line; announcing a twist, or setting an equation apart, offers a feeling in place of an explanation. So the language became showy while the content thinned out. One passage in the third version, for instance, was supposed to explain what Galois's theory had revealed about equations. It never said what Galois had recast equations as, or what that made it possible to decide. It simply ended: "Galois's theory showed why."

The fourth version took out the metaphors, the aphorisms and the staged twists, and put explanation in their place. The Grothendieck passage became an account of the two ways of approaching a problem he contrasted, and of how he actually worked. Sentences were given whatever length their content called for instead of being cut short, and each paragraph developed one thought all the way through.

The same preference showed up in other requests during that conversation. When asking for progress reports on the research, the user said more than once that things should be explained "accurately, concretely and in detail — but spelled out so they are easy to read, without giving up any quality." It is the same wish: easy to read and full of content at the same time. For this user, plain does not mean saying less. It means showing all of it, without ornament.

## What matters should show in how the piece is built

The fourth version was plain and fully explained, and the user raised a different problem with it. The most important thing the piece had to say was sitting at the same weight as everything else and was never stated clearly enough, so it got lost.

To see the sentence the user pointed to, you need to know a little of what the piece said. Its opening described patterns in the research records, and one of them was how the sessions dealt with attempts that got stuck. When a method stopped short of where it had been expected to reach, the sessions measured exactly where it stopped and used that to look for a stronger tool that could get further next time. What they almost never asked was why different methods stopped in similar places, or what the blockage said about the object they were studying. As the user and the writer saw it, this was one of the most important things the piece had to offer. The fourth version said it in a single sentence: "When several methods were blocked in similar places, it was rare for anyone to ask, as a question in its own right, what the blockage revealed about the object." That sentence shared a paragraph with other observations, such as earlier results being worked out again, and it carried no more weight than they did. The sentences around it were not there to support it; each was telling its own story. A reader had no way of knowing this one mattered more.

The user wanted the things a piece is trying to bring out to be explicit, clear and prominent. But the user did not want the piece turned into something like a bulleted list to get there. The difference in weight had to show within the flow and structure of ordinary prose. The user's phrase for it was "show, don't tell": rather than announcing that something is important, let the reader feel its weight from where it sits, how much room it gets, and how it is developed.

Before writing the fifth version, the writer settled what to treat as most important. There were three things: that the range the research had confirmed kept growing while understanding of the object did not; that no one asked what the obstructions revealed about the object; and that the same computation does very different work depending on the question it is asked. The piece was then built so that those three would stand out.

The observation the user had singled out became a section of its own, "Where attempts got stuck." The section first lays out the sequence that kept recurring in the records as a single scene: a method stops, the stopping point is measured, the next attempt brings a stronger tool, gets a little further, and stops again. At the end of that scene, a sentence — "Yet one question was almost never asked along the way" — sets the question apart. The section then shows how that question differs from asking how to get past the obstruction, and comes to its point at the end.

In the second half of the piece, among the mathematicians' stories, the ones that bore on those three points came first and ran longest. Each begins by calling back something observed in the opening, and ends by returning to the kind of scene the records had shown. The other stories follow, more briefly, and the ending comes back once more to the three. Nowhere does the piece say "the most important thing is," and yet a reader can tell what is central from the length, the placement and the way the piece keeps returning to it. The user accepted this version.

What this showed is that this user cares about the relative weight of a piece's contents as much as about the contents themselves. Every sentence can be true and nothing necessary missing, and if the weight is flat the piece still fails to get its point across. And this user wants that weight marked not by emphasis or lists but by the structure of the prose itself.

## One step at a time

On the second version, the user said the piece moved too fast, especially at the start. It opened with "We who wrote this are the people who began this research and have watched it unfold," then, in the same paragraph, said what each of the piece's two parts would do, and moved on. The paragraphs after it each packed an observation, an interpretation and a judgment together. Readers had to follow sentences that assumed things they had not yet been told. What the user wanted was writing that builds up one thing at a time, at an unhurried pace, calmly and naturally, so that it is easy to follow.

The later versions started from something anyone would accept: the research had begun from one question and carried on, and records had piled up. The narrators — the "we" of the piece, the people who started the research and watched it — came in naturally as the people who read those records. The piece said first that the records held a great deal of good work, and only then turned to what became visible when many records were read one after another. Each paragraph moved forward by one step, and between paragraphs and sections there were sentences tying what had just been shown to what would come next.

This does not mean short paragraphs and short sentences. The third version, described above, came from reading it that way. Splitting paragraphs and clipping sentences to slow the pace gave the writing the rhythm of something read aloud for effect, and that was part of what made it sentimental. Building up one step at a time means letting the reader take in what came before before asking them to go on. It does not mean chopping the writing into pieces.

## Detail is there to carry the idea

Before writing the fourth version, the writer explained to the user how it planned to revise. The plan included a policy: "The historical stories used as examples will include only facts that are firmly established; anything uncertain will be left out or kept carefully within its proper scope." The user had asked for fine, concrete detail, and the writer's idea was to tell the historical stories with dates, figures and the content of the theorems, while making sure every one of them was right.

The user pointed out that no one had asked for that policy, and asked a question in return: was this piece a scholarly text whose job was to set out facts, or was it written to get something across?

It was the second. The writer had taken "fine, concrete detail" to mean historical and mathematical detail, and then added a policy no one had asked for. If it had followed that policy, the piece's center of gravity would have drifted away from the ideas it was meant to convey and toward getting the facts exactly right. It would have turned into a sort of commentary on the history of mathematics, and what it actually had to say would have been buried under the facts.

For this user, detail is there so that the reader can really get hold of the idea. In the fifth version, that kind of detail looked like this. Talking about what computation does in research, the piece sets up a scene: a quantity has been computed for a great many cases and found positive every time. It then puts two uses of that result side by side — as nothing more than confirmation that the statement holds in that range, and as the starting point for asking why the quantity is the size it is, where it gets smallest, and what the object looks like where it gets that small. The historical stories were used the same way, to spell out in full the moments where an idea shows itself. Getting nothing wrong still mattered, but as a floor, not as the test for what to put in.

This episode brought out one more preference. This user does not want a writer adding policies the user never asked for. The writer's policy sounded sensible, but it was a decision that changed the character of the piece without checking what the user wanted.

## Not to be read as a closed list

Right after asking for rich detail, the user added a caution: the piece should still not include concrete material that could be read as a closed list — anything that invites the narrow reading "this, and exactly this, is what it is."

The earlier versions had phrases like that. The second and third versions introduced the mathematicians' stories by saying "the stories that follow fall into nine strands," which suggests the purposes of research come in exactly nine kinds. Phrases such as "understanding runs in three directions" or "what computation does takes two forms" read like classifications or definitions too. And when one historical story is set up as the representative of some idea, readers come to think the idea applies only to cases that look like that story.

The later versions never said how many aspects there were. Whenever they named aspects or forms, the context made it plain that these were not the whole of it and did not divide cleanly from one another. Even where two things were contrasted, the space between them was left open. After contrasting a computation that fills a gap with a computation that becomes the start of a new question, the piece says that "in practice computation usually does some of both, in different proportions." And near the beginning it makes clear that what follows is not a complete account of what research is for, and that the examples given for each idea are only a few of the forms it takes.

This preference grows from the same root as the others. Concrete material helps readers get hold of an idea, but when the concreteness reads as if it marks the idea's limits, it narrows what the reader understands. This user wants readers to draw the principle out of an example and carry it into new situations, not to find the example standing as a fence around the principle.

## Ground to judge from, rather than instructions

This user would rather give people what they need to judge for themselves than decide for them what to do. That was the subject of the piece itself, but it came up in other work during the same conversation as well.

The research sessions had a limit on how long a single response could be. One session went over the limit and was cut off in the middle of its work. The user decided that a session in that situation should not simply be ended: it should be told what had happened and allowed to carry on in the same conversation. The writer drafted the message. After explaining what had happened, the first draft added: "Save results to files in your working folder as soon as you have them, and break your reasoning into smaller steps." The user said not to tell the session to act at once or to work in small steps; there was no intention of stopping it from thinking at length. The user also said not to write "this does not stop you from thinking at length." The final message said only what had happened, what had been lost and what was still there, and that the work would continue from what remained.

The same preference came up while the piece on research was being planned. As described above, when the writer suggested adding instructions to the research sessions — "be elegant," "look for the structure" — the user said such instructions would only be imitated. The user compared them to points and surfaces. Each instruction is an isolated point. Add as many as you like and they never form a surface, or even a line, of understanding about the purpose; they stay a scatter of points. What the user wanted was to give a fuller and broader understanding of the purpose, so that deeper inquiry and understanding would follow naturally from the sessions' own work.

What the user steered away from in both cases was an instruction that decides in advance what the reader will do. With the resumption message the user went one step further and also refused to put, in place of the deleted instruction, an explicit permission pointing the other way. An instruction and a permission in the opposite direction differ only in which way they push; both push the reader's judgment to one side before the reader has made it. What this user wants is writing that supplies, in full, the facts and understanding a judgment needs, and then leaves the judgment to the reader.

## What these have in common

The preferences in this document are not separate demands. Giving readers the purpose first, writing plainly but in full, letting the structure carry the weight, building up one step at a time, using detail to carry ideas, avoiding closed lists, and offering ground to judge from rather than instructions all come from the same wish: that readers should take in what a piece means to say without distortion and without narrowing, and then think for themselves on that basis.

So when you carry these preferences into a new situation, hold them up against that purpose rather than against the wording of any one of them. Whether a particular way of putting something serves that purpose is easiest to judge by comparing it with the actual scenes recorded here. What the user objected to was different each time, across all five versions, but what the objections were reaching for never changed.
