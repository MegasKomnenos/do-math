## Description

Every column $k\ge265$ has a positive step in a row $j>k$ with $(j-k-1)^3\le k^2$, that is, in a row at most one step beyond the curve $k^2=(n-k)^3$: $b(j,k-1)<b(j,k)$, where $b(n,\ell)$ is the number of parking functions of length $n$ with maximum displacement exactly $\ell$.

## Statement

Let $b(n,\ell)$ be the number of parking functions of length $n$ with maximum displacement exactly $\ell$ (`Parking.b`), and let $d_{n,k}=b(n,k)-b(n,k-1)$ be the step of the column $k$ in the row $n$. For every natural number $k\ge265$ there is a natural number $j$ with
$$k<j,\qquad(j-k-1)^3\le k^2,\qquad d_{j,k}>0 .$$

## Explanation

**What the statement says.** Call $n-k$ the deficit of a position $(n,k)$, call the set of positions with $k^2=(n-k)^3$ the curve, and say that a position with deficit $r$ is behind the curve when $r^3\le k^2$. The condition $(j-k-1)^3\le k^2$ says that the position $(j-1,k)$, one row above $(j,k)$ in the same column, is behind the curve. So the statement bounds the first row in which the column $k$ has a positive step: it comes at most one row after the last row behind the curve. The proof gives the row explicitly, $j=k+r$ with $r$ the least natural number such that $k^2<r^3$, that is $r=\lfloor k^{2/3}\rfloor+1$.

**What it is used for.** The statement is called the curve increase. It is the one statement that the proof of the staircase (`Parking.staircase`) takes from the argument described below, and it is used in two places.

*The first use: the diagonal rule.* `Parking.diagonal_rule` says that for $n\ge2$ and $1\le k\le n-1$, $d_{n,k}\le0$ implies $d_{n+1,k+1}<0$. Consider a position in the first layer of a column $k\ge265$, that is $k+1\le n\le2k-1$, with $d_{n,k}\le0$, and let $j$ be the row of the present statement. If $j\le n$, the persistence of the sign (`Parking.step_persists_iter`: a column that is positive in a row of its first layer is positive in every later row of that layer) would carry the positive step from the row $j$ to the row $n$. So $n<j$, hence $n-k\le j-k-1$ and $(n-k)^3\le k^2$: the position is behind the curve. There the diagonal step is proved, as `Parking.diagonal_step_curve`. In short, the statement places every step that is not positive, in the first layer of a column $k\ge265$, behind the curve.

This use needs the bound as sharp as it is. `Parking.diagonal_step_curve` is proved for positions behind the curve, and its proof does not reach far beyond them: it compares the two positions of the diagonal step term by term and ends in an inequality between closed forms, `Parking.ledger`, which is stated behind the curve and proved with constants fitted to the curve at the smallest column $k=265$. The record of `Parking.ledger` says how little of that inequality remains beyond the curve. A bound for the first positive row that allowed a noticeably larger multiple of $k^2$ would therefore not serve the diagonal rule as it is proved.

*The second use: the early positive row.* For the columns $k\ge82$, the persistence of the sign in the first layer (`Parking.step_persists`) and the positive sign of the steps beyond the first layer (`Parking.step_pos_large`) both start from a row $k+r$ with $1\le r\le27k/100$ in which the column is positive. That row is the content of `Parking.positive_row_early`, and for $k\ge265$ it is the row $j$ of the present statement: with $s=j-k-1$, the bound $s^3\le k^2$ and $k\ge265$ give $100\,(s+1)\le27\,k$. Here much less is used than the statement says, since the distance $j-k$ is of the order $k^{2/3}$ and the bound that is asked for is linear in $k$. For $k=265$ the statement gives $j\le k+42$, where $27k/100$ would allow $k+71$; the first positive step of that column is in the row $k+36$ (an exact computation with integers from the last-car recursion, not part of the proof).

The two uses do not depend on each other in a circle. The proof of the present statement estimates the step at one position and uses no statement on persistence; persistence is derived from the present statement; and the diagonal rule uses both.

**The proof as one argument.** The statement follows in a few lines from `Parking.curve_step_pos`, which asserts the positive step at a position given by its deficit: for $r\ge41$ and $(r-1)^3\le k^2\le r^3$, $d_{k+r,k}>0$. Everything else is the proof of that statement, and it is one argument in five layers. Each layer is carried by statements with records of their own, where the proofs are; here is how they fit together. Fix $k$ and $r$, and put $R=r+1$, $N=k+R$ and $s=\sqrt R$, so that the position is $(N-1,k)$. Write $a_k=k^{k-1}/k!$ (`Parking.cay`), $P(m)=e^{-m}m^m/m!$ for the Poisson mass (`Parking.poissonMass`), and $\Lambda_m(y;N)$ for the chain sum, the total weight of the compositions of $m$ in which a cut point $t$ carries the factor $y/(N-t)$ (`Parking.Lam`, `Parking.thr`).

*1. The criterion.* The identity `Parking.dq_frozen`, applied to the column $k-1$, writes the normalised step $d_{n,k}/n!$ in the rows $k\le n\le2k-3$, the first layer of the column $k-1$, as a curvature term minus a boundary term. The curvature term is the second difference at $y=k$ of a polynomial in $y$ with nonnegative coefficients, the frozen polynomial of the column $k-1$, and the boundary term is $(a_k/k)\,\Lambda_R(k+1;N)$. In the frozen polynomial a composition with $q$ parts contributes to the coefficient of $y^q$, and $k^2$ times the second difference of $y^q$ at $y=k$ is at least $q(q-1)\,k^q$. The number $q(q-1)/2$ counts the ways to mark one or two of the $q-1$ cut points of the composition. With an exchange of sums that collects what lies below the larger marked point into the environment of the column, this bounds the curvature term from below by a sum over pairs of marked cut points $a<b$, the pair sum
$$\mathcal{P}(k,R)=\sum_{0<a<b\le R}\Lambda_a(k;N)\,\frac{k}{N-a}\,\Lambda_{b-a}(k;N-a)\,\frac{k}{N-b}\,z^{(k)}_{N-b-1}$$
(`Parking.pairSum`), in which $z^{(k)}_n=A(n,k-1)/n!$ is the environment of the column (`Parking.zr`) and $A(n,k)$ the number of parking functions of length $n$ with maximum displacement at most $k$. The result is `Parking.curv_ge_pair`, and with it the pair–impulse criterion `Parking.dq_pos_of_pair`: for $1\le R\le k-2$, if
$$k\,a_k\,\Lambda_R(k+1;N)<2\,\mathcal{P}(k,R),$$
then $d_{N-1,k}>0$. The step itself no longer occurs in this inequality. The chain sum on the left is called the impulse.

*2. The lower bound of the pair sum.* Every cut factor in $\mathcal{P}(k,R)$ has the form $k/(N-t)=\exp\bigl(-\log(1+\frac{R-t}{k})\bigr)$ and is at most $1$. Jensen's inequality for the chain sums (`Parking.Lam_jensen`) and for the environment (`Parking.zr_jensen`) bounds the term of the pair $(a,b)$ from below by $a_k\,e^R\,P(a)\,P(b-a)\,e^{-X_{ab}}$, where the exponent $X_{ab}$ collects the mean values of the logarithms $\log(1+\frac{R-t}{k})$ over the cut points of the term, and two further terms that come from the environment $z^{(k)}_{N-b-1}$: the mean of $\log(t/k)$ over the sizes $k<t<N-b$ that the environment visits, and an end term $\log\bigl(a_k\,e^{R-b}/a_{N-b}\bigr)$. Then $e^{-X}\ge e^{-\mu}(1+\mu-X)$ for every real $\mu$, and the logarithm lies below its tangent at any point, which brings in a second parameter $\theta\ge0$. Summed over the pairs this gives `Parking.pairSum_ge`: for $k\ge1$, $R\le k$, $\theta\ge0$ and real $\mu$,
$$a_k\,e^R\,e^{-\mu}\Bigl((1+\mu)\,W_\Sigma-\Bigl(\bigl(\log(1+\theta)-\tfrac{\theta}{1+\theta}\bigr)\,G+\tfrac{1}{k(1+\theta)}\,\mathcal A\Bigr)\Bigr)\ \le\ \mathcal{P}(k,R).$$
Here $W_\Sigma$ (`Parking.Wsum`) is the total of the weights $P(a)P(b-a)$, and $G$ and $\mathcal A$ (`Parking.Gsum`, `Parking.Asum`) bound the total of the exponents; all three are finite sums of the convolutions $\kappa_2=P\ast P$ and $\kappa_3=\kappa_2\ast P$ of the Poisson mass (`Parking.kc2`, `Parking.kc3`).

*3. The upper bound of the boundary term.* In $\Lambda_R(k+1;k+R)$ every cut factor $(k+1)/(k+R-t)$ is at most $1$ and increases with $k$, so $k$ may be replaced by a larger threshold $K$ that depends on $R$ alone. With all cut factors equal to $1$ the chain sum would be $R^R/R!=e^R P(R)$. The first violating point identity `Parking.Lam_defect` writes the difference as a sum over a cut point $t$ of the factor $1-f(t)$, where $f(t)$ is the cut factor, times the chain sum of $t$ with the same cut factors and the weight $(R-t)^{R-t}/(R-t)!$ of the remaining $R-t$: when every factor $1$ is split as $f(t)+(1-f(t))$, $t$ is the first cut point at which the part $1-f(t)$ is chosen; the tangent inequality `Parking.Lam_tangent` bounds the chain sums inside it from below, and moment identities of the convolutions bound the two sums that result. This gives `Parking.impulse_le`: an upper bound of $e^{-R}\Lambda_R(k+1;k+R)$ of the form $P(R)$ minus a positive multiple of $\kappa_2(R)$ plus a positive multiple of $\kappa_3(R)$, with coefficients that are explicit in $R$, in $K$ and in a free parameter of the tangent inequality.

*4. The closed forms.* Both bounds are now expressions in $P$, $\kappa_2$ and $\kappa_3$. The identities $\kappa_2(m)=P(m)\,(s_m-1)$ and $\kappa_3(m)=(m-1)P(m)-2\kappa_2(m)$ (`Parking.kc2_eq`, `Parking.kc3_eq`) reduce them to the Poisson mass and the mean $s_m$ of the birthday law (`Parking.sbirth`). For the first, Stirling's bound gives $P(m)\le c_0/\sqrt m$ with $c_0=1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ (`Parking.poissonMass_le_c0`). For the second, the lower half of Ramanujan's estimate, proved as `Parking.sbirth_ge`, gives $\kappa_2(m)\ge\frac12-\bigl(\frac43+\frac1{15m}\bigr)P(m)$ (`Parking.kc2_lower`). The sums over $b\le R$ then become sums of $b^{\pm1/2}$ and $b^{\pm3/2}$, which are bounded by comparison with integrals. The outcome is three bounds in the variable $s$ alone, valid for $R\ge42$ and $s^3\le1.07593\,k$:
$$W_\Sigma\ge W_0(s)=\tfrac{s^2}{2}-1.06385\,s+0.7184,\qquad G\le2s\,q_0(s),\qquad\mathcal A\le s^3\,q_0(s),\qquad q_0(s)=0.478731\,s^2-0.974453\,s+0.642741$$
(`Parking.Wsum_ge`, `Parking.Gsum_le`, `Parking.Asum_le`).

*5. The comparison in $s$.* The hypothesis $(r-1)^3\le k^2\le r^3$ gives $k\le s^3\le1.07593\,k$ (`Parking.k_window`). With $\theta=R/(2k)$ and a suitable $\mu$, the bound of the second layer becomes
$$\mathcal{P}(k,R)\ \ge\ a_k\,e^R\cdot0.38385\,\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{10s}\Bigr)\frac{k}{s^3}\,W_0(s)$$
(`Parking.pair_numeric`), and the bound of the third layer, taken at $K=(R-1)(2R-1)/(2s)$, becomes
$$e^{-R}\,\Lambda_R(k+1;k+R)\ <\ \frac{2\cdot0.38385\,W_0(s)\,\bigl(1+\frac1{10s}\bigr)}{s^3}$$
(`Parking.impulse_numeric`). Multiplied by $k\,a_k\,e^R$, the two inequalities are the hypothesis of the criterion. Both end in inequalities between polynomials in $s$ on the half-line $s\ge6.48$, each proved by writing $s=6.48+t$ and observing that every coefficient in $t$ is nonnegative.

**Where the curve enters, and where the argument is tight.** The constant $0.38385$ is a lower bound of $e^{-0.9575}$, and it stands for the factor by which the cut factors reduce the pair sum: by the bounds of the fourth layer the total of the exponents is about $0.9575\,(s^3/k)$ times the total weight. This is bounded exactly when $k$ is at least of the order $s^3=R^{3/2}$, which is the curve. On the other side the bound of the third layer puts the boundary term below $e^RP(R)$ by a fixed fraction of $e^RP(R)$ when the threshold $K$ is of the order $s^3$. So at a position on the curve the two sides of the criterion are of the same order, each a constant times $k\,a_k\,e^R\,c_0/s$, and the proof is a comparison of constants. Evaluated in floating point, which is not part of the proof, the two closed forms of the fifth layer divided by $c_0/s$ are $0.6826$ for the boundary term and $0.6897$ for the pair sum at $R=42$, a room of one per cent; they tend to about $0.59$ and $0.96$ as $R$ grows. This is why the estimates of the fourth layer keep two or three terms of each expansion in $s$, with the lower half of Ramanujan's estimate supplying the second term of $\kappa_2$, and why the numerical constants are given to five or six digits.

**The threshold.** The hypothesis $k\ge265$ is used only to obtain $r\ge42$, while `Parking.curve_step_pos` asks for $r\ge41$, which holds from $k=253$ on. The number $265$ is the column from which the diagonal rule uses the analytic argument; the first layer of the columns up to $264$ is covered by `Parking.diagonal_rule_small`. `Parking.positive_row_early` divides the columns at the same place, because the table from which it takes its row ends at the column $264$.

**The Lean form.** The statement is `∃ j, k < j ∧ (j - k - 1) ^ 3 ≤ k ^ 2 ∧ b j (k - 1) < b j k`. The subtraction $j-k-1$ of natural numbers is the ordinary one because $k<j$, and so is $k-1$. The sign of the step is written as a comparison of the two natural numbers $b(j,k-1)$ and $b(j,k)$. It is equivalent to the positivity of the real number $d_{j,k}/j!$ (`Parking.dq_pos_iff`), which is the form in which `Parking.positive_row_early` states the sign.

## Proof

Let $k\ge265$.

*The row.* There is a natural number $r$ with $k^2<r^3$, for instance $r=k+1$, since $k^2<(k+1)^2\le(k+1)^3$. Let $r$ be the least such number. Then $k^2<r^3$, and $(r-1)^3\le k^2$ because $r-1$ is smaller than $r$ and so does not have the property.

*$r\ge42$.* If $r\le41$, then $r^3\le41^3=68921<70225=265^2\le k^2$, against $k^2<r^3$.

*The step.* So $r\ge41$ and $(r-1)^3\le k^2\le r^3$, and `Parking.curve_step_pos` gives $b(k+r,k-1)<b(k+r,k)$. Put $j=k+r$. Then $k<j$, because $r\ge1$, and $j-k-1=r-1$, so $(j-k-1)^3=(r-1)^3\le k^2$.
