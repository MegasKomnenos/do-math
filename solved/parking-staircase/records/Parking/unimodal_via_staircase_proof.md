## Description

The proved main theorem: for every integer $n\ge1$ the sequence $(b_{n,0},b_{n,1},\dots,b_{n,n-1})$ is unimodal, where $b_{n,\ell}$ is the number of parking functions of length $n$ whose maximum displacement is exactly $\ell$. It is obtained here as a corollary of the staircase (`Parking.staircase`), a statement on the strict shape of every row and on how its peak moves when the length grows by one.

## Statement

**Definitions.** Let $n\ge1$ be an integer. A *preference sequence* of length $n$ is $\alpha=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\{1,\dots,n\}^n$. Spots $1,\dots,n$ lie in a row and are all empty at the start. Cars $1,2,\dots,n$ arrive in this order, and car $i$ looks from spot $a_i$ onward, in the direction of increasing spot numbers, and parks in the first empty spot it meets. If the spots $a_i,a_i+1,\dots,n$ are all occupied, car $i$ cannot park. If every car parks, $\alpha$ is called a *parking function*, and the set of all parking functions of length $n$ is written $\mathrm{PF}_n$.

For $\alpha\in\mathrm{PF}_n$, let $\mathrm{pos}_i(\alpha)$ be the spot in which car $i$ parks. The *displacement* of car $i$ is $d_i(\alpha)=\mathrm{pos}_i(\alpha)-a_i\ge0$, and the *maximum displacement* of $\alpha$ is $M(\alpha)=\max_{1\le i\le n}d_i(\alpha)\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}$. For $0\le\ell\le n-1$ put
$$b_{n,\ell}=\#\{\alpha\in\mathrm{PF}_n:\ M(\alpha)=\ell\}.$$

A finite sequence $(c_0,\dots,c_m)$ is *unimodal* if there is some $j^*\in\{0,\dots,m\}$ such that $c_{j-1}\le c_j$ holds for $1\le j\le j^*$ and $c_j\ge c_{j+1}$ holds for $j^*\le j\le m-1$. Equality is allowed.

**Theorem.** For every integer $n\ge1$ the sequence $(b_{n,0},b_{n,1},\dots,b_{n,n-1})$ is unimodal. That is, for every $n\ge1$ there is some $\ell^*\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ such that $b_{n,\ell-1}\le b_{n,\ell}$ for $1\le\ell\le\ell^*$ and $b_{n,\ell}\ge b_{n,\ell+1}$ for $\ell^*\le\ell\le n-2$.

## Explanation

This is the proved form of the root proposition. In the packaged library the proposition as posed is the declaration `Parking.unimodal_via_staircase`, with its proof left open, and the theorem proved in the module `Staircase.Main` is carried under the name `Parking.unimodal_via_staircase_proof`, with the identical Lean statement. In the source tree of the library, before packaging, that theorem is the declaration named `unimodal_via_staircase`, and no declaration named `unimodal_via_staircase_proof` exists there. The record of `Parking.unimodal_via_staircase` reads the Lean statement against the proposition, binder by binder, and that reading is not repeated here.

**A proof through a statement that says more.** The proof does not aim at the theorem directly. It proves the staircase, `Parking.staircase`: every row $n\ge2$ rises strictly up to a position $p_n$, has at most one pair of equal neighbouring entries, which can stand only directly after $p_n$, and falls strictly after that; and $p_{n+1}$ is $p_n$ or $p_n+1$. The theorem is what remains of the staircase when the strict inequalities and the relation between consecutive rows are given up, and its own proof, below, takes a few lines. What follows here is a map of the argument that leads to the staircase: the statements from which it is assembled, by name, and the places where it is hard. The account of the argument, region by region, is in the record of `Parking.staircase`, and each proof is in the record of its statement. The notation is that of the library: $b(n,\ell)=b_{n,\ell}$.

### The map of the argument

Arrange the numbers $b(n,k)$ in an array whose rows are indexed by the length $n$ and whose columns by the displacement $k$, $0\le k\le n-1$, and write $d_{n,k}=b(n,k)-b(n,k-1)$, $1\le k\le n-1$, for the *step of the column $k$ in the row $n$*. The rows $k+1\le n\le2k-1$ are called the *first layer* of the column $k$.

**From two rules to the staircase.** The staircase follows from two rules about the signs of the steps, both for $n\ge2$ and $1\le k\le n-1$.

- The column rule, `Parking.column_rule`: if $d_{n,k}>0$, then $d_{n+1,k}>0$.
- The diagonal rule, `Parking.diagonal_rule`: if $d_{n,k}\le0$, then $d_{n+1,k+1}<0$.

That the two rules give the staircase is a statement about arbitrary arrays, `Parking.peak_induction`, proved by induction on the row: the column rule carries the rising part of the row $n$ to the row $n+1$, the diagonal rule carries the rest one column to the right and makes it strictly falling, and the one step of the row $n+1$ that neither rule reaches decides whether the peak stays or moves.

**The column rule** is assembled from two statements, according to the row $n+1$ of its conclusion.

- In the first layer, $n+1\le2k-1$: the persistence of the sign, `Parking.step_persists`.
- Beyond the first layer, $n+1\ge2k$: `Parking.column_rule_beyond`.

**The diagonal rule** is assembled from statements about three regions, according to the position $(n,k)$ of its hypothesis.

- Beyond the first layer, $n\ge2k$: `Parking.diagonal_rule_beyond`.
- In the first layer of a column $k\le264$: `Parking.diagonal_rule_small`.
- In the first layer of a column $k\ge265$: call the set of positions $(n,k)$ with $k^2=(n-k)^3$ *the curve*, and say that a position with $(n-k)^3\le k^2$ lies *behind the curve*. The statement `Parking.curve_crossing`, called the curve increase, gives a row $j>k$ with $(j-k-1)^3\le k^2$ in which the column is positive: the column has a positive step at most one row beyond the curve. The iterated persistence `Parking.step_persists_iter` then places a step that is not positive in a row $n<j$, that is, behind the curve. At a position behind the curve the rule is the diagonal step `Parking.diagonal_step_curve`.

**Persistence and the large columns.** The persistence of the sign and the positive signs beyond the first layer in the columns $k\ge82$ have a common root. Every column $k\ge82$ has a positive step in a row $k+r$ with $1\le r\le27k/100$, `Parking.positive_row_early`; for $k\le264$ the row is read from a computed table, and for $k\ge265$ it is the row of the curve increase. From the first positive row of the column, which is then at most $k+27k/100$, the step stays positive to the end of the first layer, `Parking.one_sign_change`. This gives `Parking.step_persists` for $k\ge82$, and it is the start of the induction on the row by which `Parking.step_pos_large` shows that the step of such a column is positive in every row $n\ge2k$. The curve increase is thus used twice: in the diagonal rule, where its bound is needed as sharp as it is, and as the source of the early positive row. Its own proof uses no persistence.

**Computation and analysis.** Beyond the first layer the step is positive at every position except eleven, which lie in the columns $k\le7$ (`Parking.step_pos_beyond`). The two statements for the region beyond the first layer follow from these positive signs together with negative signs at positions next to the eleven, which a computed table gives (`Parking.Tab.negative_cells`). A step beyond the first layer that is not positive lies at one of the eleven positions, and its diagonal neighbour has a negative step; a conclusion of the column rule can fail only at one of the ten positions other than $n=2$, $k=1$, and the step one row above it is negative. The negative steps used are those in the row $2k-1$ of the columns $2\le k\le8$ and in the row $2k$ of the columns $2\le k\le6$. For the column $1$ the positivity is one computed comparison and an induction on the row; for the columns $2\le k\le81$ it comes from computed tables and from an analytic propagation of a positive step down the column; for $k\ge82$ it is the analytic theorem `Parking.step_pos_large`. In the first layer, persistence is read from a computed table for $k\le81$ and is an analytic argument for $k\ge82$, which starts from an early positive row of the column; that row is taken from a computed table for $k\le264$ and from the curve increase for $k\ge265$. The diagonal rule in the first layer rests on computed tables for $k\le264$ and on two analytic arguments for $k\ge265$, and for $k\ge9$ in addition on persistence. The tables are exact computations with the numbers of parking functions in the rows up to $300$.

**Where the proof is hard.** There are three places.

The first is the diagonal rule in the first layer of the columns $k\ge265$. The diagonal step is proved only at the positions behind the curve $k^2=(n-k)^3$, by comparing, term by term, two expressions for the step at a position and at its diagonal neighbour; the inequality between closed forms that closes the comparison has little room at the column $265$. To apply it one has to know that a column has turned positive by the time it leaves that region, to within one row. This is the curve increase, a second and separate estimate. Since the diagonal step is proved behind the curve only, a weaker bound for the first positive row would not do.

The second is the persistence of the sign in the first layer, and with it the positivity beyond the first layer for $k\ge82$. These are two long analytic arguments. `Parking.persist` shows that a column which has turned positive in the first third of its first layer stays positive to the end of the layer. The induction step of `Parking.step_pos_large` carries the positive sign to every later row, although the rows above the first positive row of the column contribute terms that are not positive to every one of them.

The third is strictness. Both rules conclude with a strict inequality, and the diagonal rule starts from a step that may be zero, so a step equal to zero has to be excluded wherever a rule is read off from signs. Where no argument excludes it, the signs are computed. This is the reason for the three tables of strict signs that the record of `Parking.staircase` lists. The largest of them reads, for $9\le k\le264$, every step of the column $k+1$ from its first row to the row in which the column $k$ is known to be positive.

### Earlier work

The statement proved here was posed as a conjecture by Celano, Elder, Hadaway, Harris, Martin, Priestley and Udell [celano2025, Conjecture 6.5]. Their paper calls a parking function an $\ell$-interval parking function when its maximum displacement is at most $\ell$ [celano2025, Definition 2.7]. The number $b_{n,\ell}$ of parking functions of length $n$ with maximum displacement exactly $\ell$ is therefore the number of $\ell$-interval parking functions of length $n$ that are not $(\ell-1)$-interval, and the conjecture is that for every $n\ge1$ the sequence of these numbers for $\ell=0,\dots,n-1$ is unimodal. The paper tabulates the numbers for $n\le9$ [celano2025, Table 1], and it gives the two ends of each row, $b_{n,0}=n!$ and $b_{n,n-1}=n^{n-2}$ [celano2025, Example 2.8 and Section 6]. In the sentence before the conjecture it states, for all $n\ge1$ and as consequences of its enumerative results, the inequalities $b_{n,0}\le b_{n,1}$, $b_{n,1}\le b_{n,2}$ and $b_{n,n-2}\ge b_{n,n-1}$, and it then adds that experimentally the pattern seems to continue [celano2025, Section 6]. Read for all $n$ for which their terms exist, the first and the second of these three inequalities do not hold for the smallest $n$: the rows of $n=2,3,4,5$ are $(2,1)$, $(6,7,3)$, $(24,51,34,16)$ and $(120,421,377,253,125)$, so that $b_{n,0}\le b_{n,1}$ fails for $n=2$ and $b_{n,1}\le b_{n,2}$ fails for $n=3,4,5$.

The works that were read contain, among other things, exact descriptions of the cumulative counts and a limit theorem for the maximum displacement. Write $A_{n,\ell}=b_{n,0}+\dots+b_{n,\ell}$ for the number of parking functions of length $n$ with maximum displacement at most $\ell$. One description is a sum over outcomes. The outcome of a parking function is the permutation $\sigma$ whose $i$-th entry is the number of the car that stands in spot $i$; for a permutation $\sigma$ and a spot $i$, let $\lambda_i$ be the length of the longest run of consecutive spots that ends at spot $i$, spot $i$ included, and whose cars all have numbers at most $\sigma_i$. The parking functions with outcome $\sigma$ and maximum displacement at most $\ell$ then number $\prod_{i=1}^{n}\min(\ell+1,\lambda_i)$, and $A_{n,\ell}$ is the sum of these products over all permutations $\sigma$ [celano2025, Section 4.1, (6)–(8)]. Another description is a recursion in $n$, obtained by fixing the spot of the car that arrives last: $A_{n,\ell}=\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{x}\min(x+1,\ell+1)\,A_{x,\ell}\,A_{n-1-x,\ell}$ with $A_{0,\ell}=1$ [aguilarfraga2024, Theorem 3.7]. The proof does not rely on the article for this recursion: the recursion is proved from the definition of the parking process (`Parking.A_succ`, which states it with $n+1$ in place of $n$), and the records call it the last-car recursion. The limit theorem is a theorem of Petersson on hashing with linear probing, stated in the summary of his doctoral thesis. It concerns a table with $m$ cells and $m-1$ items, conditioned on its last cell remaining empty, and says that the maximum displacement $d_{\max}$ of the items satisfies $\Pr\bigl((m-d_{\max})/m^{2/3}\le z\bigr)\to1-\psi(z^{3/2})$ as $m\to\infty$, for $0<z<\infty$, where $\psi$ is a function that the theorem defines by a power series [petersson2009, Theorem 2.1]. That this series is $\psi(s)=\mathbb E\,e^{-sA}$, where $A=\int_0^1\max_{s\le t}B_s\,dt$ for a standard Brownian motion $B$, is [jansonpetersson2009, Corollary 2.4], and the thesis summary states it as its Theorem 2.9 and Corollary 2.11. For $m=n+1$ such a table is a uniform random parking function of length $n$; this identification is the reading of the model made here and is not a quotation from the thesis summary. Under it, the theorem says that all but an arbitrarily small fraction of the sum of the row of $n$ lies at a distance of order $n^{2/3}$ from the end $\ell=n-1$ of the row. The theorem is a statement of convergence in distribution, and it compares no two neighbouring terms of a row. Three further works that were read prove, each for an object other than the rows $b_{n,\ell}$, a statement on the shape of a sequence of counts, on the maximum displacement, or on the number of the members of a family of parking functions. The generating polynomial of the sum of the preferences, taken over the parking functions of length $n$, is log-concave for every $n\ge1$ [pappe2024, Theorem 3.1], and the note says that this statistic is essentially equivalent to the displacement [pappe2024, p. 1]; this concerns a sum over the cars of a parking function and not the maximum of their displacements. For non-decreasing parking functions the maximum displacement is the height of the corresponding Łukasiewicz path [seligzhu2024, Theorem 2.1], whereas for general parking functions the maximum displacement and that height differ in general [seligzhu2024, Remark 2.3 and the example given in it]; indeed sorting the entries of a parking function can change its maximum displacement, as the lists $(1,2,1)$ and $(1,1,2)$ show, whose maximum displacements are $2$ and $1$. And one preprint counts, for each $\ell$, the $\ell$-interval Fubini rankings, the Fubini rankings in which at most $\ell+1$ competitors tie at a rank [agerhart2026, Section 4 and Corollary 4.1], and recalls that all Fubini rankings are parking functions [agerhart2026, p. 3]; it does not call these rankings $\ell$-interval parking functions, and it does not speak of displacement.

Several results that the proof uses are classical. The rest of this section treats those for which a source is cited. For each of them it says what the source contains and how the proof stands to the result: a declaration may state the result and prove it; the result may be proved only as a step inside the proof of another declaration; or the proof may not prove it and take what it needs from Mathlib, the library of formalised mathematics on which the proof is built. In outline, the proof states and proves as declarations, in the forms given below, the formula $(n+1)^{n-1}$ for the count of the parking functions, Abel's binomial identity, the value of the sum of the probabilities of the Borel distribution with parameter $1$, the theorem of de Bruijn and Erdős on renewal sequences, and the upper bound of Robbins for the factorial. Of the estimate of Flajolet, Grabner, Kirschenhofer and Prodinger it proves two one-sided consequences, one as a declaration and one as a step. It does not use the lower bound of Robbins, and it does not prove the correlation inequality of Fortuin, Kasteleyn and Ginibre: in the place of the first it uses a weaker bound for the factorial, and for the one instance of the second that it needs it uses the four functions theorem, and both of these come from Mathlib. Classical facts that the records name without a source, for example the weighted inequality of Chebyshev (`Parking.lr_sum_le_finset`), are not treated here. Of the works cited below, the note of Abel and the articles of Aldous and Pitman and of Konheim and Weiss were consulted at the places cited, and what is said of these three works concerns those places; the other works were read.

The count $(n+1)^{n-1}$ of the parking functions of length $n$ is proved by Pollak's argument in Foata and Riordan [foatariordan1974, Section 2]: the street is replaced by a circle of $n+1$ spots, on which each of the $(n+1)^n$ sequences of $n$ preferences among the $n+1$ spots leaves exactly one spot empty; by symmetry every spot is left empty by equally many of these sequences, and the parking functions are the sequences that leave the spot $n+1$ empty. The count is also contained in the article of Konheim and Weiss [konheimweiss1966, Lemma 2 and Section 6]. Their Lemma 2 is $f(N,r)=N^{r-1}(N-r)$, written here with $N$ for the number of cells, where $f(N,r)$ is the number of ways to assign a cell to each of $r$ balls, among $N$ cells, such that the last cell stays empty when each ball in turn is placed in the first free cell at or after its assigned cell, the cells being taken cyclically. Their Section 6 gives the probability that $c$ cars all park in a street of $p$ places, each car preferring each place with probability $1/p$, as $f(p+1,c)/p^c$. By Lemma 2, $f(n+1,n)=(n+1)^{n-1}$. For $p=c=n$ the probability is therefore $(n+1)^{n-1}/n^n$, so that $(n+1)^{n-1}$ of the $n^n$ preference sequences of length $n$ are parking functions. The proof does not use Pollak's argument. It proves $A_{n,\ell}=(n+1)^{n-1}$ for $n\le\ell+1$ (`Parking.A_eq_pow`; `Parking.zr_eq_cc` is the same statement for the normalised counts $A_{n,\ell}/n!$) from the last-car recursion and from Abel's binomial identity. For the lengths up to $\ell+1$ the factor $\min(x+1,\ell+1)$ of the recursion is $x+1$, and the numbers $(n+1)^{n-1}$ satisfy the recursion with this factor, which is Abel's identity for particular arguments (`Parking.cc_rec`, stated for the numbers $(n+1)^{n-1}/n!$). For $n\le\ell+1$ the number $A_{n,\ell}$ is the count of all parking functions of length $n$, because none of them has a displacement larger than $n-1$. No declaration states this identification. What the proof has about a maximum displacement $k\ge n$ is the statement that $b_{n,k}/n!=0$, that is $b_{n,k}=0$, for $k\ge n$ and $k\ge1$ (`Parking.bq_eq_zero`), and it derives this statement from the formula.

Abel's binomial identity, $\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk\,w_k(x)\,(y-k)^{n-k}=(x+y)^n$ with $w_0(x)=1$ and $w_k(x)=x\,(x+k)^{k-1}$ for $k\ge1$, is the case $\beta=-1$ of the identity that Abel states [abel1826, p. 159] and proves by an induction on $n$ that starts at $n=0$. In Abel's notation the identity that he states is $(x+\alpha)^n=\sum_{\mu=0}^{n}\binom n\mu\,\alpha\,(\alpha-\mu\beta)^{\mu-1}(x+\mu\beta)^{n-\mu}$ for arbitrary $x$, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and every positive integer $n$, the term with $\mu=0$ being $x^n$; the identity displayed first is its case $\beta=-1$ with $x$ and $y$ in the places of Abel's $\alpha$ and $x$. The proof proves the identity for real $x$ and $y$ and every $n\ge0$ (`Parking.abel_identity`), by an argument that is not an induction on $n$. It uses the identity in two places. At $x=1$ and $y=n+1$ the identity gives the recursion for the numbers $(n+1)^{n-1}$ of the previous paragraph (`Parking.cc_rec`). With a general $x$ it gives the coefficients of the powers of the series $\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)^{n-1}X^n/n!$ in closed form, and from these the proof bounds from below the renewal sequence of the kernel described in the paragraph on de Bruijn and Erdős below (`Parking.Gres_lower`).

The numbers $e^{-j}j^{j-1}/j!$, $j\ge1$, are the probabilities of the Borel distribution with parameter $1$, and that their sum is $1$ says that this distribution has total mass $1$. Aldous and Pitman state two facts from which this follows, and they do not write the sum out [aldouspitman1998, Section 2.2 and (10)]. The first, in their Section 2.2, is that the total progeny of a Galton–Watson process is finite with probability $1$ if and only if the mean $m$ of the offspring law is at most $1$. The second, their formula (10), is that when the offspring law is the Poisson law with mean $m$, the total progeny equals $j$ with probability $(mj)^{j-1}e^{-mj}/j!$; this is the Borel distribution with parameter $m$. The proof proves the value of the sum (`Parking.hasSum_borel`) without branching processes, by a renewal argument on partial sums. From it the proof derives $\sum_{m\ge1}e^{-m}\varepsilon_m=1-e^{-1}$ for the numbers $\varepsilon_m=(m-1)^{m-1}/m!$ (`Parking.hasSum_eps`), and in the diagonal step it bounds the partial sums of the Borel probabilities $e^{-j}j^{j-1}/j!$ by $1$ (in the proof of `Parking.kap_diag_le`).

The theorem that the renewal sequence of a log-convex kernel is log-convex is Theorem 1 of de Bruijn and Erdős [debruijn1953, pp. 161–162]. Here the renewal sequence of a kernel $c_1,c_2,\dots$ is the sequence $u$ with $u_0=1$ and $u_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_iu_{n-i}$ for $n\ge1$ (`Parking.IsRenewal`). In the notation of the article, where $c$ is the kernel and $f(n+1)$ is the $n$-th term $u_n$ of its renewal sequence, the theorem reads: with $c_k\ge0$, $\sum_k c_k=1$, $f(1)=1$ and $f(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_kf(n-k)$, if $c_{k-1}c_{k+1}>c_k^2$ for all $k>1$ then $f(n-1)f(n+1)>f(n)^2$ for all $n>1$; the Remarks after it give the form in which both inequalities are non-strict and the consequence that $f(n+1)/f(n)$ increases, and the article says that other theorems of the same type were proved by Kaluza. The proof proves the form with non-strict inequalities, for a kernel all of whose terms are positive, and without the condition $\sum_kc_k=1$, which the article assumes and which its proof of Theorem 1 does not use (`Parking.renewal_logConvex`). It applies the theorem once, in the persistence of the sign in the first layer of the columns $k\ge82$, to the kernel whose terms are $\tfrac32e^{-1}$ for $i=1$ and $e^{-i}\varepsilon_i$ for $i\ge2$ (`Parking.fker`). The sum of this kernel is at most $1-\tfrac12e^{-1}$, by the value of $\sum_{m\ge1}e^{-m}\varepsilon_m$ given in the previous paragraph, so the condition $\sum_kc_k=1$ does not hold for it, and the proof needs the theorem in the form without that condition. The log-convexity of the renewal sequence of this kernel is what the comparison `Parking.extreme_column_le` rests on, and `Parking.caseFar` uses that comparison for the rows beyond $k+3k/5$ of the first layer.

The two-sided bound for the factorial, $n!=\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n e^{r_n}$ with $1/(12n+1)<r_n<1/(12n)$ for $n\ge1$, is in Robbins [robbins1955, p. 26, (1) and (2)]. The proof uses the upper bound, $n!<\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n e^{1/(12n)}$, and proves it (`Parking.factorial_lt_robbins`), starting from two facts that Mathlib provides about the sequence $n!/(\sqrt{2n}\,(n/e)^n)$: a series for one step of its logarithm, and its limit $\sqrt\pi$. The upper bound enters in two places: through `Parking.poissonMass_ge`, the lower bound $(1-\tfrac1{12n})/\sqrt{2\pi n}$ for the Poisson mass $e^{-n}n^n/n!$, and through `Parking.sbirth_le`, the upper bound for the mean of the birthday law of which the next paragraph speaks. The proof does not use the lower bound of Robbins. From below it needs only the weaker bound $\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n\le n!$, which it does not prove: this bound comes from Mathlib (`Stirling.le_factorial_stirling`) and enters through `Parking.poissonMass_le`, the matching upper bound $1/\sqrt{2\pi n}$ for the Poisson mass.

For the quantity $\theta_n$ defined by $e^n/2=\sum_{i<n}n^i/i!+\theta_n\,n^n/n!$, Flajolet, Grabner, Kirschenhofer and Prodinger prove $\theta_n=\tfrac13+\tfrac{4}{135(n+k_n)}$ with $\tfrac{2}{21}\le k_n\le\tfrac{8}{45}$ for all integers $n\ge0$ [flajolet1995, Theorem 7]. The proof meets $\theta_n$ through the mean $s_n=\sum_{j=1}^{n}n(n-1)\cdots(n-j+1)/n^j$ of the birthday law, for which $s_n+\theta_n=e^n\,n!/(2n^n)$. It uses two one-sided statements about $\theta_n$. Both are contained in the theorem just quoted, and the proof takes neither from the article. The first is $\theta_n\ge\tfrac13$ for $n\ge1$. It is proved in the form $e^{-n}\sum_{i\le n}n^i/i!\le\tfrac12+\tfrac23\,e^{-n}n^n/n!$, as an auxiliary step of the proof of `Parking.sbirth_le` and not as a declaration with a record of its own. The statement of `Parking.sbirth_le` is the upper bound $s_n\le\sqrt{\pi n/2}\,e^{1/(12n)}-\tfrac13$ for $n\ge1$, which the diagonal step uses. The second is $\theta_n\le\tfrac13+\tfrac1{15n}$ for $n\ge2$. A declaration states and proves it, as the lower bound $e^n\,n!/(2n^n)-\tfrac13-\tfrac1{15n}\le s_n$ that the curve increase needs (`Parking.sbirth_ge`).

The correlation inequality for a positive log-supermodular measure on a finite distributive lattice, under which two non-decreasing functions are non-negatively correlated, is Proposition 1 of Fortuin, Kasteleyn and Ginibre [fortuin1971, Proposition 1]. The proof does not prove this inequality, and none of its declarations states it. It needs one instance of it, on the lattice of the subsets of a finite set, in the diagonal step. There the proof compares sums over the compositions of a number $q$ in which a composition has as its weight the product of the numbers $a_j=j^{j-1}/j!$ over its parts $j$ and of one nonnegative factor for each of its cuts, a factor that depends on the position of the cut. A composition of $q$ is given by its set of cut points, a subset of $\{1,\dots,q-1\}$. The instance says that when the factors of the cuts are replaced by larger ones, the weighted mean of a nonnegative function of the set of cut points that is monotone for inclusion does not decrease; the proof states this without division, as an inequality between products of sums. The proof of `Parking.Lam_gain`, which bounds from below the factor by which such a sum grows from a position of the array to its diagonal neighbour, derives the instance in an auxiliary step, which is not a declaration with a record of its own, from the four functions theorem, and that theorem comes from Mathlib (`Finset.four_functions_theorem`). The hypothesis of the four functions theorem holds because the product of the numbers $a_j$ over the parts of a composition is log-supermodular as a function of the set of cut points, and this rests on the log-convexity of the numbers $a_j$ (`Parking.cay_logconvex`).

## Proof

Let $n\ge1$.

*The case $n=1$.* Take $\ell^*=0$. Then $\ell^*\le n-1=0$, and both conditions are empty: no $\ell$ satisfies $1\le\ell\le0$, and no $\ell$ satisfies $\ell\le n-2$.

*The case $n\ge2$.* By `Parking.staircase` there is a natural number $p$ such that the row $n$ of the array $b$ has its peak at $p$ in the sense of `Parking.IsPeak`:
$$p\le n-1;\qquad b_{n,k-1}<b_{n,k}\ \text{ for }1\le k\le p;\qquad b_{n,p+1}\le b_{n,p}\ \text{ if }p+1\le n-1;\qquad b_{n,k}<b_{n,k-1}\ \text{ for }p+2\le k\le n-1.$$
The second part of the staircase, about the row $n+1$, is not used. Take $\ell^*=p$. For $1\le\ell\le p$ the second condition gives $b_{n,\ell-1}<b_{n,\ell}$, hence $b_{n,\ell-1}\le b_{n,\ell}$. Let $p\le\ell\le n-2$. If $\ell=p$, then $p+1\le n-1$, and the third condition gives $b_{n,p+1}\le b_{n,p}$. If $\ell>p$, then $p+2\le\ell+1\le n-1$, and the fourth condition with $k=\ell+1$ gives $b_{n,\ell+1}<b_{n,\ell}$, hence $b_{n,\ell+1}\le b_{n,\ell}$.

`Parking.staircase` is in turn `Parking.peak_induction` applied to the array $b$, with `Parking.column_rule` and `Parking.diagonal_rule` as its two hypotheses, as its record explains.

In the Lean proof the first case is $n<2$, and the hypothesis $n\ge1$ is not used: in the Lean statement the range of the second condition is written $\ell+1\le n-1$, and for $n=0$, where $n-1$ is read as $0$, the statement holds with $p=0$ for the same reason as for $n=1$. The hypothesis belongs to the statement, where it makes $n-1$ the ordinary difference.
