## 2. The problem (fact)

The root statement of the first instance, which the new instance will reuse in English with the same mathematical content, is the following. Korean original (file `C:/math/.skill-workspace/do-math/parking-unimodality-fresh-20261105-150803/root.md`):

> 양의 정수 n에 대해 주차 자리는 1부터 n까지 일렬로 있다. 차도 1부터 n까지 있으며 번호 순서대로 도착한다. 선호 목록 α=(a₁,…,aₙ)는 각 aᵢ가 1부터 n 사이인 정수열이다. 차 i는 aᵢ가 비어 있으면 그곳에, 차 있으면 그보다 오른쪽의 첫 빈자리에 주차한다. 오른쪽에 빈자리가 없으면 실패한다. 모든 차가 성공하는 목록을 주차 함수라고 하며 그 집합을 PFₙ이라 한다. 차 i가 실제로 주차한 자리를 sᵢ라 할 때 변위는 dᵢ=sᵢ−aᵢ이고, 최대 변위는 M(α)=maxᵢ dᵢ다. A_{n,ℓ}=#{α∈PF_n: M(α)≤ℓ}, b_{n,ℓ}=#{α∈PF_n: M(α)=ℓ}. A_{n,−1}=0, b_{n,ℓ}=A_{n,ℓ}−A_{n,ℓ−1}; A_{0,ℓ}=1 for ℓ≥0. P. 모든 정수 n≥1에 대해 어떤 정수 c∈{0,…,n−1}가 존재하여 b_{n,k}≤b_{n,k+1} (0≤k<c), b_{n,k}≥b_{n,k+1} (c≤k<n−1) 가 성립하는가? 봉우리가 하나의 원소일 필요는 없으며, c는 n에 따라 달라도 된다. 이 문제의 양화는 모든 n이며, 특정 n이나 특정 형태의 목록에 한정한 결과로 전체 해결을 대신하지 않는다.

English rendering: For a positive integer $n$ there are parking spots $1,\dots,n$ in a row and cars $1,\dots,n$ arriving in index order. A preference list $\alpha=(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ has each $a_i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$. Car $i$ parks at $a_i$ if that spot is free, otherwise at the first free spot to the right of $a_i$; it fails if there is none. A list in which every car parks is a parking function; the set is $\mathrm{PF}_n$. If $s_i$ is the spot car $i$ actually takes, its displacement is $d_i=s_i-a_i$ and the maximum displacement is $M(\alpha)=\max_i d_i$. Let $A_{n,\ell}=\#\{\alpha\in\mathrm{PF}_n : M(\alpha)\le\ell\}$ and $b_{n,\ell}=\#\{\alpha\in\mathrm{PF}_n : M(\alpha)=\ell\}$, with $A_{n,-1}=0$, so $b_{n,\ell}=A_{n,\ell}-A_{n,\ell-1}$; the empty list gives $A_{0,\ell}=1$ for $\ell\ge0$. Question P: for every integer $n\ge1$, is there an integer $c\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ with $b_{n,k}\le b_{n,k+1}$ for $0\le k<c$ and $b_{n,k}\ge b_{n,k+1}$ for $c\le k<n-1$? The peak need not be a single term and $c$ may depend on $n$; the quantifier is over all $n$, and no result restricted to particular $n$ or particular lists counts as a solution.

This is Conjecture 6.5 of Celano, Elder, Hadaway, Harris, Martin, Priestley and Udell, "Statistics on $\ell$-interval parking functions", arXiv:2507.07243 (v1 2025-07-09; v2 updated 2026-09-19), who write $|\mathrm{IPF}_n(\ell)^{\max}|$ for $b_{n,\ell}$ and tabulate the counts for $n\le9$. The endpoints are $b_{n,0}=n!$ and $b_{n,n-1}=n^{n-2}$.

## 6. Working hypothesis (hypothesis, not decision)

The operator's candidate explanation, to be tested and, if necessary, revised or replaced by the constructing agent:

The parking-function counts are weighted sums over rooted labeled forests in which the weight of a forest is a product over its vertices of a function of the subtree size alone: $A_{n,k-1}=\sum_F\prod_{v\in F}\min(t_v,k)/t_v$ over rooted forests $F$ on $[n]$, with $t_v$ the size of the subtree rooted at $v$ (CB.md (6.6), to be re-derived). A uniform rooted labeled forest on $[n]$ is a critical Poisson(1) Galton–Watson forest conditioned on its total size (Janson 2012, Example 10.2, to be verified), and the law of the conditioned forest does not depend on the Poisson mean. Every computation about such a multiplicative size functional therefore lives on one object, the critical Poisson Galton–Watson forest, and uses three operations on it: conditioning on size, marking a vertex, and cutting an edge. Cayley's counts are the enumerative face of the object; the Borel and Borel–Tanner laws are the size laws of its components when the conditioning is released; the tree function $T(z)=ze^{T(z)}$, Lagrange inversion and the Lambert W function are the generating-function bridge between the two faces; the birthday law of the depth of a marked vertex and the spine decomposition are the marking face; uniform edge deletion and the additive coalescent are the cutting face; the $m^{-3/2}$ tails and the $n^{2/3}$ scale of the largest component are the critical behaviour of the same object. Different methods met the same tools repeatedly, and overlapping, because they met the same object through different operations, and the tools overlapped where two operations were combined, for instance conditioning after marking (Borel–Tanner blocks along a spine) or cutting after conditioning (Pavlov's representation of component sizes). What is specific to the unimodality problem is the particular functional $\min(t,k)/t$, whose saturation at the threshold $k$ creates the two-signed response (positive two-mark curvature against negative saturation loss) that the unimodality question is about; the structure explains why the tools recur, and it isolates the inequality that the structure alone has not yet settled.

Predictions that would test this hypothesis: every inventory row should be classifiable as one of the three operations (or a composition of two) applied to the object and read through one of its faces; a tool that appears at a site and cannot be so classified refutes the hypothesis as stated. A statistic of parking functions that is a multiplicative size functional should meet the same tools; one that is not (for example a statistic depending on labels beyond subtree sizes) need not. The Poisson mean must be irrelevant to every conditioned statement and relevant to every unconditioned one. Known theories that may already contain the whole hypothesis are listed in §8; if one does, the document says so and cites it.

## 7. Status vocabulary (fixed)

Every mathematical statement in the deliverables carries exactly one of the following labels, and statements with proofs state what they depend on.

- Cited: the statement is a theorem of the literature; the label names the source and the theorem number, and the agent has confirmed the statement in the source text.
- Derived: a consequence of cited statements with a proof given in the document.
- Proved here: a statement proved in the document from stated hypotheses, not found in the literature by the agent; the label does not assert novelty beyond "not found".
- Computed (exact): a finite statement confirmed by a program in exact integer or rational arithmetic, with the script and output preserved under `computation/`.
- Counterexample: a false stronger statement with the explicit witness, verified exactly.
- Conjecture: a statement believed and tested but unproved; the label states the tested range.
- Unverified: a claim taken from the CB corpus or elsewhere that the agent could not confirm; the document says why.
