# How the unimodality of the maximum-displacement counts of parking functions was proved: a history of the research

The sources that this account cites are published with it, except those that `SOURCES.md` names as not published and the archive of the earlier instance, which at 349 MB is left out of the repository. The file `SOURCES.md`, in the folder of this document, says for each kind of coordinate where the source is to be found: in one of the two archives of the instances' published knowledge, in the folder `sources/` beside this document, or in a tracked file of the repository; and it lists the sources that are cited and not published, with the reason for each. Paths in this account are relative to the root of the repository, with two exceptions: a path that begins with `sources/` is relative to the folder of this document, and a path that begins with `knowledge/`, `control/`, `runner/` or `sessions/` is relative to the directory of the instance concerned.

## 1. What this document is

Between 5 November and 23 November 2026 two runs of an autonomous research system worked on one statement, a conjecture of Celano and co-authors about parking functions. The first run reached a proof that its own reviewers accepted but that rested on large certified computations and was never formalized. The second run, started six days after the first had stopped, reached a different proof, shorter and with far less computation, and had it checked in the Lean proof assistant. This document tells how that happened: what was tried and in what order, what worked, what failed and why, how the structure of the final proof emerged, and how the work passed from the first run to the second.

The account was assembled from the primary record of the two runs: their version-control histories, in which every publication is a dated commit; the published records with their reviews; the texts that the user and the operator submitted to the running research; the logs of the programs that drove the sessions; and, for the days between the two runs, the working files of the sessions that wrote the intermediate documents. Twenty-four assistants, called *source readers* below, each read one part of this material and reported on it. The account was written from their reports, checked against the sources by others who had not written it, read through by one who had nothing but the text, and then extended in its mathematical passages from the records themselves. Section 9 says what these checks covered and what they could not cover.

Three conventions hold throughout. Times are Korean Standard Time (UTC+9) on the 24-hour clock, and all dates are in 2026. A statement taken from a source carries a coordinate that a reader can open; a statement that is an inference of this account, and not something a source says, is marked as an inference. Where the sources do not say something that the account needs, the account says that it was not found and does not supply a guess.

The coordinates are of the following kinds. "E" stands for the earlier run and "L" for the later one; their directories are named in section 2.3. "E seq 133" is the 133rd publication of the earlier run; the folder `knowledge/events/` of each run holds one file for each publication, with its sequence number and time, and each publication is one commit (with one exception: in the earlier run the set of questions of 9 November, E seq 188, was committed together with the next record). ## 2. The statement, and the vocabulary of the system

### 2.1 The statement

Fix an integer n ≥ 1. There are n parking spaces in a row, numbered 1 to n, and n cars that arrive one after another. Car i has a preferred space a_i in {1, …, n}. It drives to a_i; if that space is free it parks there, and otherwise it takes the first free space after a_i. If every space from a_i onward is taken, the car fails to park. The sequence (a_1, …, a_n) is a *parking function* if every car parks. There are (n+1)^(n−1) parking functions of length n; this number is written p_n below.

When car i parks in space s_i, its *displacement* is s_i − a_i, the number of spaces it had to drive past its preference. The *maximum displacement* M of a parking function is the largest displacement of any of its cars; it lies between 0 and n−1. The maximum displacement depends on the order in which the cars arrive, so it is not determined by the multiset of preferences. For 0 ≤ ℓ ≤ n−1 let

  b(n,ℓ) = the number of parking functions of length n with M = ℓ,
  A(n,ℓ) = the number with M ≤ ℓ, so that b(n,ℓ) = A(n,ℓ) − A(n,ℓ−1).

The sequence b(n,0), b(n,1), …, b(n,n−1) will be called the *row* of n. For n = 7 the row is 5040, 42253, 62145, 57398, 46142, 32359, 16807. Its two ends are known in general: b(n,0) = n!, and b(n,n−1) = n^(n−2) for n ≥ 2.

A finite sequence is *unimodal* if it rises (weakly) to some position and falls (weakly) from there on. The statement under research is:

> **For every n ≥ 1 the row b(n,0), …, b(n,n−1) is unimodal.**

It is Conjecture 6.5 of Celano et al., "Statistics on ℓ-interval parking functions", arXiv:2507.07243. Both runs call this statement the *root*.

Almost everything in the history is phrased through the *step*

  d(n,k) = b(n,k) − b(n,k−1),  1 ≤ k ≤ n−1,

the difference of two neighbouring entries of a row. A row is unimodal exactly when there is no pair of positions i < j with d(n,i) < 0 < d(n,j), that is, no decrease followed later by an increase. It is useful to picture the steps d(n,k) arranged in the plane, with the *row* n (the length) running one way and the *column* k (the level of displacement) running the other. Unimodality of every row says that in each row the signs of the steps are first non-negative and then non-positive. Much of the later history consists of statements about how the sign of d(n,k) behaves when one moves along a column (k fixed, n growing) or along a diagonal (n and k growing together). Two numbers measure where a position (n,k) lies in its row: the level k itself, counted from the left end, and the *deficit* r = n − k, counted from the right end. "The level" below always means the k of the step under discussion, and "row" means the sequence b(n,·) or, where steps are concerned, its index n. The peak of a row lies near the right end, at a deficit of order n^(2/3) (section 4.2).

### 2.2 The research system

The system is called do-math. One run of it on one statement is an *instance*: a directory with its own Git repository. Inside an instance, many AI *sessions* work at the same time and one after another, each in one of several roles. An *attempt* session takes a statement and tries to prove it. An *explore* session investigates freely, without a statement to prove. A *formalizer* session writes a Lean proof of something that has been proved informally. Whatever such a *producer* session leaves behind is given to a *peer* session, which checks it, repairs and extends it, and writes it up as a candidate record; by the attributions that the records themselves make, the peer often contributes as much mathematics as the producer, and in places the decisive step (sections 4.6, 4.8 and 6.9 have examples). A *review* session then judges the candidate. If the review decides to publish, the candidate becomes a *record*, and its publication is one commit of the instance's repository. The sequence of producer, peer and review for one piece of work is a *pipeline*. *Organizer* sessions maintain an index of the published records. A program called the *runner* launches the sessions, up to a set number at once.

A record consists of a mathematical exposition, a list of *claims* (its separate assertions), and the review's verdict on each claim: *supported* or not. A record can also propose changes to the instance's map of the problem, which is called the *frontier*. The frontier consists of *nodes* and *relations*. A node is a precisely stated proposition; the root is one. A relation is an implication between nodes that a review has accepted, for example "these two nodes together imply the root". A record that claims to have proved a node proposes a *solution*; the review gives the solution a level, *informal* (a proof in words that the review accepts) or *formal* (a Lean proof that the review has compiled itself and compared with the node's statement), or refuses it. A node is accordingly in one of the states *unresolved*, *informal_proved* or *formal_proved*. The state propagates along relations: when all the premises of an accepted relation are proved, its conclusion counts as informally proved. A node is formally proved only through a Lean proof of its own statement. The frontier also carries *questions*, which are open-ended and are either open or resolved.

Two kinds of outside party act on an instance. The *user* is the person who commissioned the research. The *operator* is an AI session, outside the instance, that starts and tends it on the user's behalf. Either can submit a *standing input*: a text that every session started afterwards receives along with its task. Standing inputs are the channel through which the user's judgment reached the sessions, and section 7.1 lists them all. Those of the later instance are referred to by their order of submission, as that instance's records refer to them: the first is the handoff with which it started, the fourth brought in a paper, and the fifth to the twelfth are the user's later rulings (two notices of the operator are not counted). The records call them standing conditions; this account says input, and "the fifth condition" in a quotation is the fifth input.

One more term is needed for the earlier run. An attempt session does not have to take a whole node. It can register a *section* of a node, a part of the node's statement that its approach addresses, and other attempts can then register other parts. Sections are bookkeeping for the division of labour; they are not part of the frontier.

## 3. The course of the research in brief

The history has three parts, and the reader may find it useful to have their outline before the detail.

**The earlier instance (5 to 13 November)** started from the statement alone and reached, in six days, a proof that its reviews accepted. Its shape is that of a problem taken apart by regions. Each row is cut at its centre. The right half is closed by an induction on n along the chain of largest trees (section 4.2), started from a certified computation. The left half is closed column by column, and within a column by ranges of rows, down to one comparison in a range of rows called the second layer (section 4.8). Every row with n ≤ 20000 is covered by computation, and many of the inequalities above that range are themselves verified by machine. The proof was never formalized, because the user paused formalization on the fifth day; and by the judgment of its own reviews it does not say why a row is unimodal. On the fifth day the user told the instance that this "why" was the thing most wanted, and the instance spent its remaining days partly on that question without answering it.

**Between the instances (12 to 20 November)** the earlier proof was written up as a manuscript; a night's analysis of that manuscript, on 19 November, produced a document of structure and five reports which reduced the conjecture to statements about how the sign of the step d(n,k) moves along columns and diagonals (the earlier instance had proved a reduction of the same kind, and had not followed it), and named the places where the analysis was stuck; a handoff document summed these up. Separately, a paper was written that proves three theorems on the conjecture and reduces it to one open inequality. The later instance was started from the handoff and received the paper on its second day. It did not receive the earlier instance's records.

**The later instance (19 to 23 November)** worked under rules that the earlier one had not had: computation only for small cases, nothing handed over to be taken as proved, and success defined as a short explanatory proof with a Lean formalization. Within two days it had informal proofs of the right half; these were then set back, repeatedly, by the user's requirement that a proof which can still be made shorter is not yet accepted. Under that requirement the proofs shrank and their computations shrank, and the structure of the final proof settled: unimodality of every row follows from three statements about columns, that a column k which has turned positive in one of its rows up to 2k − 1 stays positive in those rows, (C); that a column cannot turn positive before the column to its left has, (Mono); and that every column k ≥ 6 is non-negative from row 2k + 1 on, (L). The one row between, 2k, is covered by a further inequality that the proof of (L) yields, and the columns below 6 by a short table (section 6.9). The first Lean proof came on the third day, the informal proof of the root on the evening of the fourth, and the formal proof on the morning of the fifth, 23 November at 08:10. It rests on the definition of the parking process, one recurrence, and 6,118 small facts that the Lean kernel computes, all in rows n ≤ 300.

## 4. The earlier instance, 5 to 13 November

### 4.1 The terms of the start

The earlier instance was created on 5 November at 15:08 (commit `d31ae5b4d`). Its settings at that commit allowed eight sessions at a time, on one model with a second as fallback, and named Lean 4.32.0 with Mathlib as the proof assistant, with the three standard axioms `propext`, `Classical.choice` and `Quot.sound` as the only ones a formal proof might use (`git show d31ae5b4d:settings.json`).

A standing input was submitted with the creation (input of 5 November 15:08, `1793858913461626000-…`). It set the terms. The text is unsigned and speaks of the user in the third person; the sources do not say who drafted it. The instance was to start from the statement alone: no earlier instance, session, proof, computation or summary of the literature was to be opened, and only knowledge produced and reviewed inside the instance was to pass between sessions. The input defined two levels of success. "Mathematical resolution" meant a proof for all n, or an exact refutation; a wider computed range or a result for particular n was a partial result. "Academic completion" asked in addition for an account of what the result adds to the literature and of the structure or obstacle that produces the conclusion, and it sets three questions for a reviewer: whether the result has been compared, hypothesis by hypothesis, with existing theorems that might contain it; which decomposition, correspondence or inequality the proof rests on and which phenomenon of the problem it explains; and whether the range and the limits of the result are stated exactly, beyond computations and examples. Separately, the whole statement was required as a Lean theorem about the original parking process, compiled by an independent review, before full achievement could be declared. The main product was to be a mathematical exposition in Korean that a person could follow.

The records read for this account show the mark of this input: each begins by restating the original process with cars in arrival order, separates what is known in the literature from what is its own, and closes by saying what of the two levels of success remains unachieved.

The input gives no reason for the clause on starting afresh. It is to be read against the fact, which the sources attest, that the same conjecture had been attempted before, more than once. A directory beside the instance holds an older run of 22 October, of an older generation of the system, which published 165 records in one day and ended with the root unresolved (`.skill-workspace/do-math/parking-unimodality-fresh-20261022-071132`, 168 commits from 07:13 to 23:58); its own goal file already forbids the use of still earlier research. A further instance was started on 23 October and has operator files up to 2 November (`.skill-workspace/do-math-inputs/parking-unimodality-fresh-20261023-014524/`); its idle runner was stopped on the evening of 5 November (M-0641). These runs were not read for this account. The sources do not say why the research was begun again from nothing on 5 November. Memory notes record that the system itself had been rebuilt, its new baseline committed that morning (M-0631) and its final verification committed at 15:04, four minutes before the instance was created (M-0640); the nearness of the two times is all that connects the rebuilding with the fresh start.

### 4.2 The first day: the tools, and a first reduction

The first two sessions, an attempt and an exploration, each finished in about twenty minutes, within a minute of each other, and their records were published two seconds apart at 15:55 (E seq 1 `r81e7051b`, seq 2 `r15524fdd`). Independently of each other they had built the same set of exact tools, and almost everything that follows rests on these.

The first is the *last-car recurrence*. If the last car to arrive parks in space j+1, that space was empty until then, so no earlier car drove past it; the cars to its left and to its right form two smaller parking functions whose displacements are unchanged, and the last car has min(j+1, ℓ+1) preferences that keep its displacement at most ℓ. Hence

  A(n,ℓ) = Σ_{j=0}^{n−1} C(n−1,j) · min(j+1, ℓ+1) · A(j,ℓ) · A(n−1−j,ℓ),  A(0,ℓ) = 1.

Both records identify this with a known theorem (Aguilar-Fraga et al., arXiv:2311.14055, Theorem 3.7). It allows exact rows to be computed by integer arithmetic, and it is the combinatorial fact about parking on which the Lean development of the later instance was to be built, sixteen days later (section 6.6).

The second tool looks at the outcome: the permutation σ recording which car stands in which space. For the car in space i let h_i, its *capacity*, be the distance back to the nearest space on its left that holds a later-arriving car (or to the start of the row). Given σ, the displacements of the n cars can be chosen independently, the i-th in {0, …, h_i − 1}. So the parking functions with outcome σ and M ≤ ℓ number Π_i min(h_i, ℓ+1). This product formula is in Celano et al. It suggests proving unimodality one outcome at a time, and both records close that road at once: the outcome (1,2,3,5,4,6,7) for n = 7 has capacities 1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 6, 7, hence 1008 parking functions, and the numbers of them with M = 0, 1, …, 6 are 1, 31, 130, 222, 216, 264, 144, which fall from 222 to 216 and rise again. The factors min(h_i, ℓ+1) stop growing at different levels ℓ, and a single outcome need not be unimodal.

Both records also found, from exact rows (computed up to n = 120 in one and up to n = 150 in the other), that the usual stronger property fails. A positive sequence is log-concave if c_i² ≥ c_{i−1}c_{i+1}, and log-concave sequences are unimodal; but b(80,40)² < b(80,39)·b(80,41), and the first record proves that log-concavity fails for every sufficiently large n. Whatever proves unimodality has to be weaker than log-concavity.

The second record proposed the instance's first reduction, forty-seven minutes after the start. Call Q the statement that, for every n, once a row has strictly decreased it is log-concave from there on: b(n,ℓ) > b(n,ℓ+1) implies b(n,ℓ+1)² ≥ b(n,ℓ)·b(n,ℓ+2). Then b(n,ℓ+2) ≤ b(n,ℓ+1)²/b(n,ℓ) < b(n,ℓ+1), so a row that has begun to fall keeps falling, and Q implies the root. The review accepted Q as a node and "Q implies the root" as a relation (commit `dbee55e98`, 15:55). Six days later it is through this relation, among others, that the frontier would count the root as proved.

During the rest of the day the sessions added the picture in which most of the earlier instance's analysis is set. A record of 16:52 (E seq 4, `r42d9a173`) replaced parking functions by labelled rooted forests on n vertices: if each vertex v, with a subtree of s_v vertices below and including it, is given an independent mark uniform on {0, …, s_v − 1}, then the largest mark has the same distribution as the maximum displacement of a uniform parking function. In particular the probability that M ≤ ℓ is the expectation of Π_v min(s_v, ℓ+1)/s_v over uniform random forests. In this product a vertex contributes a factor different from 1 only if its subtree has more than ℓ + 1 vertices. Two disjoint subtrees cannot both have more than n/2 vertices; so at a level in the right half of the row the subtrees that matter are nested: the largest tree of the forest, then the largest of the subtrees hanging from its root, and so on down for as long as the size stays above ℓ + 1. This sequence of sizes is the *chain of largest trees*, or simply the chain, and it is what is meant wherever this account speaks of largest trees or of nested large subtrees. (The record of 16:52 notes the nesting of the subtrees larger than n/2 and that outside that range disjoint large subtrees appear, so that the chain no longer counts everything; the description of the chain as a sequence of largest trees is that of two records of 8 November, E seq 159 and 164.) This account reads the fact as the reason why positions in the right half of a row proved so much more tractable than positions in the left half, and why the decompositions in this history cut the row near its centre. The same record gave the first sign result for a range that grows with n: the last R entries of a row decrease strictly as soon as n ≥ 20·R^(3/2). Records of 17:57 and 18:59 (E seq 8 `r1aa0be60`, seq 12 `r91f681e0`) proved local limit theorems showing that the peak of the row sits at a deficit of order n^(2/3), and a record of 19:11 (E seq 13, `r39ea75d8`) derived a recurrence along the chain of largest trees, with the Borel distribution Bor(m) = e^(−m) m^(m−1)/m! as its kernel, and from it the profile of the cumulative counts A(n, λn) for 1/2 < λ < 1; that the row itself increases in that range was still a conjecture in this record. Its numerics for n ≤ 1200, which are not certified, put the smallest margin of increase near ℓ ≈ n/2 for the larger n; the review refused the statement that this is so always (at n = 100 the minimum lies elsewhere).

Two things about how the day went are worth recording. The same results were found several times over, because sessions running side by side cannot see each other's unpublished work: three sessions independently cut a parking function at its breakpoints, the positions j < n such that exactly j cars prefer one of the first j spaces, into *prime* parking functions, those with no breakpoint, and found that the statement corresponding to the root is false for primes: for every n ≥ 21 the numbers q(n,ℓ) of prime parking functions of length n with M = ℓ satisfy q(n,n−3) > q(n,n−2) < q(n,n−1), so that the row of the primes dips just before its right end (E seq 3, 5, 6; the statement is given as in the first of the three). Since the primes are asymptotically a fixed fraction e^(−2) of all parking functions and have the same limit law, that record concludes that no argument using only the limit law can prove unimodality. And in the evening the program driving the sessions was changed: between 20:32 and 23:21 nothing ran, and the runner was restarted with a version in which an attempt registers a section of a node, so that several attempts can share one node (M-0642; the change shows in the session files, where the commands offered to a session change from one for taking a whole node, `claim` (unrelated to the claims of a record), to `lease`, `section`, `release`). Until then a node could be claimed only whole, and at most one attempt had worked on the root and one on Q at any time, so that of eight possible sessions about three were at work.

By the next morning two computer-assisted statements were in hand, the first of them for every n: Q holds on the last 102 entries of every row (E seq 18, `ra88ba51b`), and every row with n ≤ 800 is strictly unimodal, by exact integers (E seq 19, `r39ba7c11`). The first is a statement for all n obtained from finitely many certificates: an entry at a fixed distance from the right end of row n is, for all large n, a fixed finite combination of the numbers p_(n−1), p_(n−2), … with coefficients that are polynomials in n, so that for each of the one hundred positions concerned (an inequality involves three consecutive entries, whence 102) the required inequality becomes the negativity of one explicit polynomial expression, certified once in exact rational arithmetic for all n beyond an explicit point, the rows below that point being checked directly. The second of these records also proved that b(n,k) > b(n,k−1) whenever n ≥ 2k² + k, so that each column of steps is eventually positive.

### 4.3 The second day: every region asymptotically, and a Lean proof that is not on the path

On 6 November, between 05:48 and 05:54, three attempt sessions each registered a section of the root: the part of each row to the right of the centre m = ⌈n/2⌉, the part to the left, and the pairs of positions that straddle the centre (the registrations are preserved in `knowledge/records/ra3e85e42…/evidence/producer/work/updates-final.json`). A fourth session then found nothing left to register, and its report on why became the published record of the logic of the split (E seq 42, `ra3e85e42`): a sequence is unimodal exactly when there is no strict decrease followed later by a strict increase, and cutting the positions of such a pair at a chosen point of the row gives three conditions, left, right and crossing. The division of labour made here is, with the crossing part later split in two, the decomposition of the root that the frontier would carry from the next day; it existed for thirty-one hours only as sections.

Working under these sections, the sessions obtained on that day the sign of the step in almost every region of the row, but only for all sufficiently large n. The right half is unimodal for large n (E seq 47, 54). The single step at the centre is eventually positive (E seq 55). The row increases in a band of width of order n^(2/3) below the centre (E seq 64), on an initial segment of about n·loglog n/(log n)² entries (E seq 49, 57, 88), and, by the evening, at all positions between any fixed fraction of n and the centre: for each fixed λ₀ in (0, 1/2) and all sufficiently large n, b(n,q) > b(n,q−1) whenever λ₀·n ≤ q ≤ ⌈n/2⌉ (E seq 108, 22:44, a result that the peer obtained in a turn resumed after it had finished; section 7.5 describes that mechanism). None of these has an explicit starting point, and every record says so. The explicit statements of the day were all at the two ends of the row, for example: the last R entries decrease for n ≥ (16/5)·R^(3/2) (E seq 70), and the first 121 entries increase for n ≥ 143 (E seq 80). The second of these records certified more than its headline: for each column 2 ≤ k ≤ 120 the step is negative from row k up to a row τ_k and positive from it on, and τ_k does not decrease with k (claim `exact-size-thresholds`). (Row k is counted as the first row of column k: b(k,k) is read as 0, so that d(k,k) = −b(k,k−1) is negative.) The row at which a column turns from negative to positive will be called its *crossing row*. The records have several symbols for it: τ_k here, N(k) and N_k in the records of 10 November (section 4.8), and q₀(k) in the later instance; they differ in whether the first positive or the first non-negative step is meant, which is the same row wherever no step vanishes. That every column crosses once, at a row that does not decrease with k, is the pattern of signs to which section 4.8 returns. One exact identity of the morning recurs through the rest of this history (E seq 51, `r631b0f4`, 08:09). By a sign-reversing involution on the parking functions with a given outcome,

  d(n,k) = 2·T(n,k) − B(n,k−1),

where T(n,k) counts the parking functions with M = k in which the maximum is attained by a positive even number of cars (the records write E(n,k); the letter is changed here because E also stands for the earlier instance; it has nothing to do with the Target T of section 5.3), and B(n,k−1) counts those with M = k−1 in which every car attaining the maximum stands in a space of capacity exactly k, so that its displacement could not have been larger. The sign of a step is thus a contest between two positive counts, ties created and maxima blocked. At the centre of the row these two counts were found to agree to leading order, with a relative difference of order k^(−1/3) (E seq 115, 124), and the same was reported for the range between the centre and the peak (E seq 135, which was not opened for this account). This account reads that near-cancellation as the reason soft arguments fail on the problem; it is met again in every later phase.

### 4.4 An evaluation from outside, and the turn to explicit statements

On the evening of 6 November a standing input of a new kind was submitted (20:43; file name `1793965433272480800-…`). It carried the conclusions of an AI session outside the instance that had read the published records up to then and written an evaluation. The input is no longer in the instance's `control/` directory; it was withdrawn between 13:48 and 13:56 the next day, and it can be read in the `input.json` of the sessions started in between and in a copy kept by the operator (`sources/operator/external-evaluation-route-20261106.md`). Its diagnosis was that the records were correct as far as checked, but that dividing a row into windows and proving limits in each "structurally yields only sufficiently large n", with thresholds that are not explicit or are far beyond computation. Its proposal was to control one identity with explicit constants and to cover the range below the explicit threshold by finite computation. The identity comes from the product formula. A subtree with more than k vertices contributes to A(n,·) a factor proportional to the level near k, and a subtree with exactly k vertices, the *threshold size*, a factor that stops growing exactly at level k − 1. Taking the second difference in the level therefore gives

  d(n,k)/A(n,k−1) = 𝔼[(1+1/k)^Z + (1−1/k)^Z − 2] − 𝔼[(1−1/k)^Z·(1 − (1−1/k)^H)],

where 𝔼 is the expectation over a random forest weighted by the product formula at level k − 1, Z is the number of subtrees with more than k vertices and H the number with exactly k. The first term is non-negative; it is the *curvature term* of this account, and the second is the loss from threshold subtrees. (The formula is given in the exact form of E seq 124, §3.1, of the next morning; according to that record the evaluation had the two terms as a heuristic expansion.) The input presented itself as reference knowledge and not as an instruction.

The records of the following night and day take it up by name. The evaluation had proposed to prove the left half through a comparison of moments, 𝔼[Z²] ≥ C·k·𝔼[H] with a constant C > 1. One record showed that this fails eventually for every fixed C > 1, with C = 1.5 already at n = 1327, and that with C = 1 it holds only by a relative margin that tends to zero, of order k^(−1/3) at the centre (E seq 115); one turned its heuristic expansion into an exact lower bound and found it negative for 16 ≤ n ≤ 21 (E seq 116); one showed that the second-moment inequality to which such a route leads is 2·T₂(n,k) ≥ B(n,k−1), with T₂ the number of parking functions in which exactly two cars attain the maximum k, which is the identity d = 2T − B with the ties of four or more cars discarded, hence slightly stronger than d ≥ 0 and no simpler (E seq 117); and the record of 08:12 on 7 November wrote the evaluation's two terms without remainder (E seq 124). More broadly, from this day the instance's work is aimed at statements with explicit ranges, closed below by certified computation. The source reader of this part notes that explicit-range work had begun before the input and that the records attribute only the passages just cited to it, so that how much of the turn is the input's doing is not established.

### 4.6 The right half is closed

The right half was closed in the night of 8 to 9 November, at 01:16 (E seq 177, `r8a9b11e7`, commit `05d97ddce`). The proof is an induction on n, and its ideas came in three steps over the preceding day.

Consider a position in the right half, k ≥ (n+1)/2. A tree with more than k vertices is then the largest tree of the forest and the only one that can produce a mark above the level, so the step of row n is a mixture over the size t of the largest tree:

  d(n,k)/p_n = Σ_{t=k}^{n} π_n(t)·χ_t(k).

Here π_n(t) is the probability that the largest tree of a uniform random forest on n vertices has t vertices, and χ_t(k) is the same step for a single tree: the probability that the largest mark in a uniform random rooted tree with t vertices equals k, minus the probability that it equals k − 1. Removing the root of that tree leaves a forest on t − 1 vertices, and χ_t(k) is, up to explicit positive terms, the step of row t − 1 at the same level. So the induction hypothesis is the statement W itself in the smaller rows: wherever row t − 1 is known not to decrease at level k, χ_t(k) is positive and bounded below by an explicit positive "source". It is negative for the sizes t in a window of width about 0.6·k^(2/3) just above k, which correspond to the rows in which level k lies beyond the peak, and χ_k(k) = −1/k for a tree of exactly k vertices.

Used directly, this mixture gives nothing: in the expansion along the chain that earlier records had used, the chains passing through the window contribute to the positive and to the negative part alike, each larger than their difference by a factor of order n^(1/3) (so the record that introduced the remedy says of the earlier form). The first idea (E seq 159, `r58b4caa6`, 8 November 02:03) was to sum by parts in t. The kernel π_n is increasing and convex on the right half, so the step equals π_n(k) times the sum of all the χ_t(k), plus the increments π_n(s+1) − π_n(s) times the tail sums Σ_{t>s} χ_t(k). Inside a tail sum the negative χ_t of the window are added to the positive χ_t of the larger sizes before any weight is applied, and what multiplies the result is an increment of the kernel and not its value; the window then costs an amount of the same order as the main term and no longer n^(1/3) times it. The rearrangement has a free parameter: before summing by parts one may subtract from the kernel any linear function α·t with 0 ≤ α ≤ π_n(k+1) − π_n(k), at the price of a term α·Σ_t t·χ_t(k). The record also set up the frame of the induction, showing that the non-negativity it needs in the rows below n follows from W for the rows 20001 to n − 1, from published statements near the peak, and from the certified rows up to 20000.

What remained was the sum of all the χ_t(k), a difference of two nearly equal quantities. The second idea (E seq 164, `r23f10e9`, 07:03) bounded it without cancellation. The probability that M = m − r in row m is a sum over the possible chains s_1 > s_2 > … of products of Borel weights Bor(j) of the gaps between consecutive sizes. Adding 1 to every size maps the chains for row m and deficit r one-to-one onto those for row m + 1 and the same deficit, and leaves every gap, hence every Borel weight, unchanged; comparing the remaining factors term by term shows that, for deficits r ≤ m/2, the probability of deficit r in row m + 1 is at least 1 − β times that in row m, with an explicit β of order 1/m. With this the step of row n is bounded below by an expression in which every term has one sign: the positive source, minus the window loss, minus the loss π_n(k)/k from one tree of exactly k vertices, the last multiplied by 1 + ℰ, where ℰ is a weighted sum of the entries of column k − 1 over the rows of its right half.

The third idea came from the peer of the closing record (E seq 177). Taking α = 0 removes all the other terms that would need an upper bound; one remaining term, the entry of the top row n − 1, is contained with a larger coefficient in the positive source and is simply subtracted from it; and ℰ, which had resisted estimates row by row, becomes explicit when the rows are summed. For a fixed row the chain is a random walk with Borel steps conditioned on where it starts; summing over the starting rows with Borel weights removes the conditioning and leaves an unconditioned walk with independent Borel steps, started just above the level and read upwards, for which all expectations are products of values of the Borel generating function. This gives ℰ ≤ 7.72·(k−1)^(−1/3) for every k ≥ 25001, which are all the levels that occur in the induction.

The proof is not self-contained. Its induction starts at n = 50001. Below that it rests on computation: the right half of every row 20001 ≤ n ≤ 50000 (E seq 168, `r59c82d7`: 524,955,000 positions, in double-precision arithmetic with a bound on the rounding error proved beforehand, which is not interval arithmetic), and the rows up to 20000 by the integer-interval certificate of 7 November. Near the peak, at positions with 1.4 ≤ (n−k+1)/k^(2/3) ≤ 10, it uses a computer proof by interval arithmetic of the increase (E seq 173, `ra4c01fc`), in which peer and review between them found and bounded seven defects of rigour. In the remaining range the inequality to be shown is that the certified lower bound for "positive source minus window loss" is at least the certified upper bound for the loss from one tree of exactly k vertices. The review of the closing record found one defect in each of two large interval certificates behind these bounds, re-ran them corrected, and accepted the solution with the corrected constants. At the positions up to two thirds of the row, k ≤ 2n/3, the ratio of the two sides, which has to be at least 1, is certified as at least 1.0727 where the record's own text prints 1.0807; the minimum is attained at the centre of the row n = 50001, where the true ratio is about 2.09. At the positions beyond 2n/3 the same inequality is proved by hand. The review describes what it accepted as an analytic proof with interval-arithmetic certificates, two of which it had corrected by one line each and run again, resting on published computer proofs, with no independent re-implementation of the certificates and no Lean. Of the proof near the peak on which it depends (E seq 173), that record's own review says that it is an interval-arithmetic computer proof with corrections made after the fact, not a clean re-run and not Lean. A second, independent proof of the same range followed at 04:40 (E seq 183, `r5f5cf733`): it proves at these positions the inequality 2·T₂(n,k) > B(n,k−1) between the count of ties of exactly two cars and the blocked count of section 4.3.

The system started a formalizer on each of the two nodes that had received a solution of their own, within a minute of it: the increase statement W at 01:17 and this two-count inequality at 04:41. The other right-half nodes were proved through relations and received none.

### 4.8 The left half: columns, layers and one comparison

The left half took three more days. Since there the subtrees above the level need not be nested, the sessions worked column by column: fix the level k and follow the step d(n,k) as the row n grows from 2k+1. The path to the proof can be told as a sequence of reductions of the set of positions (k, n) still open.

By the morning of 9 November computer-assisted records had closed all rows n ≤ 20000 (E seq 157, `r15df76b5`, two independent interval computations), all columns k ≤ 2000, a strip of rows just above 2k, and all rows beyond about 2·k^(3/2) (E seq 162 to 179). What remained was a window: columns k ≥ 2001 and rows from max(2k+103, 20001) to about 2·k^(3/2).

A record of 08:07 (E seq 185, `r317289416`) put the column into a form in which rows can be treated one at a time. The last-car recurrence cuts a parking function of length t at the space of its last car into a left and a right part, which are again parking functions; repeating this gives a binary tree of *blocks*, each a parking function on a stretch of consecutive spaces. Writing r_t = d(t,k)/A(t,k−1) for the normalised step of row t, the recurrence becomes

  r_t = src_k(t) + the average, over the position of the last car, of r_a + r_b for the two parts, of sizes a and b,

with an explicit source term src_k(t) whose only negative part comes from a last car standing exactly at the k-th space of its block. Now collect with the source the steps of those parts whose size lies between k and 2k, and call the sum (★)(t). The remaining parts are larger than 2k and smaller than t, or smaller than k, and the latter have step 0. Hence, by induction up the column: if (★)(t) ≥ 0 for all rows t with 2k < t ≤ n other than those where the comparison is already known, then d(n,k) ≥ 0. The record calls this an absorption induction, because rows closed by other means are absorbed into it and need not satisfy (★). The statement (★)(t) ≥ 0 concerns one row t at a time, and the only steps that enter it are those of the rows k ≤ m ≤ 2k of the same column. These rows are what the records call the *first layer* of column k; they are the rows in which level k lies in the right half, where the signs were known by then. The rows 2k < t ≤ 3k are the *second layer*.

On 9 November the statement (★) was certified for the columns 2001 to 3130 (E seq 190, `r207f1244`; the test on which the certificate rests passes at column 3130 by a margin of 2.4·10⁻⁴ and fails at column 3131). A second certificate, of a moment inequality that implies the comparison, reached column 3131 that afternoon, with five sample columns up to 20000, and was stopped there by the user's decision on computation (E seq 195; section 4.7). Both are double-precision computations with error bounds fixed beforehand, not interval arithmetic.

For the columns from 3131 on, a record of the evening treated the rows above 3k and the second layer separately (E seq 196, `r79ee9e02`; the words are older, a record of 9 November 02:33 already speaks of the second layer). In a row t ≥ 3k + 1, whenever one of the two parts has first-layer size, the last car of the block stands beyond the block's k-th space; the weights in (★) are then regular, and (★)(t) > 0 follows from two statements. One is that a part of the source is positive in every such row, which the record proved for all k ≥ 3131. The other is an inequality on the first layer alone, in which t no longer occurs, and which it left open:

  Σ_{m=j}^{2k} [(k+1)·b(m,k) − (k−1)·b(m,k−1)]/(e^m·m!) ≥ 0 for all k ≤ j ≤ 2k.

In the second layer this does not work, because there the last car of a block can stand at or before its k-th space with a first-layer part to its right; the record located the real cancellation there, in a strip of rows just beside 2k. The columns 3131 to 6666 need only the rows above 3k: for them 3k < 20001, so their rows up to 3k lie inside the range n ≤ 20000 already certified. Apart from column 3131 and the sample column 5000 of the second certificate, no record found for this account computes them column by column, and the proof does not use those two computations either: all these columns were closed when the first-layer inequality was proved, in a weaker form that suffices, for all k ≥ 3131 on 11 November (below).

In the night of 10 November the pieces already published were put together into a first statement about the root itself, though not the one wanted: every row is unimodal for all n beyond some N₀ that is not explicit (E seq 211 `r22df70d6`, 00:25; found independently by E seq 217 `r9e82bb0c`, 00:57). Its review remarks that the ingredients had been in print since the evening before and that the record's share was to have assembled them; and, under a heading on the user's question, that this eventual validity is a patching together of results that differ from regime to regime and does not show by one principle why a row rises once and falls once. The second of the two records states what had become of the left half: it "now remains in one form only: explicit constants".

On 10 November a record gave the second layer its working form (E seq 236, `r6f5f48f2`, 07:41). The record works with the forest: its trees are the stretches of consecutive spaces that end at a car which arrived later than all the cars to its right, and a tree of size s can be arranged in min(s, ℓ+1)·A(s−1,ℓ) ways with M ≤ ℓ. For n ≤ 3k − 1 at most two trees can have k or more vertices, and the step is then exactly a sum of five pieces,

  d(n,k) = −Θ_n + FL_n + BV_n + KD_n + PAIR_n,

where each term counts arrangements according to the one or two trees that reach the level. Θ_n = C(n,k)·k^(k−2)·A(n−k,k−1) is the loss from a tree with exactly k vertices, the threshold size, whose last car is at displacement k − 1 and cannot move further. FL_n is the net contribution of one tree with k + 1 to 2k + 1 vertices. A tree with m + 1 ≥ 2k + 2 vertices contributes (k+1)·b(m,k) − (k−1)·b(m,k−1) = k·d(m,k) + b(m,k) + b(m,k−1), times the number of ways to choose and complete it; the record splits this into KD_n, the sum of the parts k·d(m,k), and BV_n, the sum of the parts b(m,k) + b(m,k−1), which it calls the stem gain of the large tree (it does not expand the abbreviations BV and KD). PAIR_n ≥ 0 is the contribution of two trees that both reach the level. Θ is the only pure loss. KD_n is a combination with positive coefficients of the steps of the same column in the rows 2k + 1, …, n − 1, so it is non-negative by induction up the column; and FL_n ≥ 0 follows from the first-layer inequality displayed above, by a summation by parts. Under that hypothesis the second layer is therefore reduced to a contest without cancellation, BV_n ≥ Θ_n: does the stem gain of large trees pay for one threshold tree? For k → ∞ the record finds the limit of the ratio BV_n/Θ_n, by an argument that it marks as informal on each range and as heuristic between them. At a fixed distance ν = n − 2k from the start of the layer the limit is 2·Pr(X < ν) with X a Borel variable, about 1.84 in the first open row n = 2k + 103 of a column; further up the layer it rises to about 4, and it falls back towards 2 at the end of the layer. In that first row the computed ratio is 3.03, 2.40 and 2.14 for k = 1600, 6400 and 25600.

The same morning, in two records that were not on the path of the proof, the instance proved the reduction on which the later instance's proof would be built, and set it aside. An exploration that held the question of the root as a whole, published at 08:23 (E seq 239, `r32a94494`, claim `c-staircase-lemma`), noted that, if no step is zero, all rows are unimodal with a peak that never moves back as n grows exactly when every column of steps changes sign once, from negative to positive, at a row N(k) that never decreases with k; and it found in exact rows up to n = 170 that the signs lie so, the peak moving by zero or one from each row to the next. A record published at 12:00 (E seq 252, `raf9e09e0`) had begun from the wish to prove the single change of sign in each column by a soft argument, and its peer stated and proved the reduction. Let N_k be the first row n ≥ k with d(n,k) ≥ 0. If every column, once non-negative, stays non-negative in all later rows, and N_k is non-decreasing in k, then every row is unimodal (Proposition 6.1(b) of the record, with no assumption on zeros; claim `staircase-lemma`, supported). Conversely, if no two neighbouring entries of a row are equal, all rows are unimodal and the peak never moves back, then both column properties hold; and in that case the peak moves by at most one from each row to the next exactly when N_k is strictly increasing, which is the same as the rule that a negative step stays negative along its diagonal (Proposition 6.1(c)). In the terms that the later instance would use, part (b) corresponds to the relation (Col) ∧ (Mono) ⇒ root, the two counting a step that is exactly zero differently, and the rule of part (c) is (Diag) with strict signs (sections 5.2 and 6.2).

The soft argument that the record had hoped for failed, and the record shows why. Its starting point is an exact equation for one column: the step of row n equals a source term S_n (not the quantity S of section 4.5), which in every column that was computed is negative in the first rows of the column, about √k/2 of them, and positive afterwards, plus a combination Σ_{m<n} K(n,m)·d(m,k) of the steps of the lower rows with positive coefficients K(n,m) = k·C(n,m+1)·A(n−m−1,k−1). Let Γ be the matrix that solves the equation, d = Γ·S; Γ(n,m) is the total weight of all ways of climbing from row m to row n in several jumps. If K or Γ were totally positive of order 2, that is, if all their 2×2 minors were non-negative, a single change of sign of S would force a single change of sign of d. The peer proved that neither is. For Γ the reason is structural: a climb from m to n′ either passes through an intermediate row n or jumps over it, so Γ(n′,m) > Γ(n′,n)·Γ(n,m), which is the reverse of what total positivity requires, and a source with one change of sign can be written down whose solution has two. For K the reason is specific to the problem: for jumps of length up to k + 1, and up to factors that do not affect the comparison, K as a function of the length n − m of a jump is the Borel law, which is log-convex because of its heavy tail, so that a late row gives relatively more weight to the earliest rows of the column, exactly where the source is negative. The record concludes that the column statement cannot be had from the sign pattern of the source and the positivity properties of the kernel alone: the size of the positive source has to be used. It observed that the column statement asks more than the left half that was then being proved, since it also requires that in the right half the peak does not move back as n grows, which was "not in the published results"; and it did not propose it as a node (§6.1, last paragraph; §7.2). The reduction remained a supported claim in two records, and the instance went on with its layers.

### 4.10 What kind of proof this was

The sources describe the proof precisely, and the description matters for what followed.

It is an informal proof spread over several dozen records, each link accepted by a review session, with no formal proof of any link on the path. Every row with n ≤ 20000 is covered by certified finite computation. The general argument is used only for n ≥ 20001, and it is itself computer-assisted at many points: the right half rests on a certified base up to n = 50000 and on interval-arithmetic proofs of explicit inequalities; the left half rests on certificates for the columns up to 3130, in integer interval arithmetic up to column 500 and in double precision with error bounds fixed beforehand above it, and above them on statements proved for all larger columns at once, which themselves rest in part on the certified rows n ≤ 20000 and on evaluations in double-precision arithmetic that the reviewers recomputed with independent code but that are not interval-certified. Margins are sometimes thin where the truth is not: in the right half, a certified ratio of 1.07 against a true ratio of 2.09 at the centre of the row n = 50001. Several certificates were found defective as first run and were repaired by a peer or a review; in one case none of the 443 units into which a certificate was divided had verified its hypothesis, because a bound was coded k times too small (E seq 174). In every such case the theorem survived the repair.

And by the judgment of its own reviews, repeated in each closing record, it gives no reason why a row is unimodal. It shows, region by region of the (n,k) plane, that one positive quantity exceeds one negative quantity, by a different argument in each region; the manuscript's own map of the proof lists them (`임시/주차함수_증명/장v2/장1.md`, §1.4).

### 5.2 The night of 19 November: a structure document, five reports, a handoff

On 19 November at 02:10 the user opened a new session with the manuscript and a complaint; "the night of 19 November" below always means the hours from then to the morning of that day. By 06:44 that session had written a *structure document* (`sources/between/structure-document/단봉성의_구조.md`), from the manuscript, from summaries of its chapters, from a note on the literature, and from exact computations of its own up to n = 220. Neither the structure document nor the documents that followed it that morning, five reports and a handoff, which are described below, mention the earlier instance or cite a record of it. Two of the session's working files do: the chapter summaries keep the record identifiers that the manuscript attaches to its statements, and the note on the literature describes the public deposit of the instance's records, of which it says it read the landing page only. Whether the session opened the instance itself was not established for this account.

The view of the problem that the structure document sets out is this. Let M be the maximum displacement of a uniformly random parking function of length n. In the forest picture of section 4.2, with t_v the size of the subtree of a vertex v (the s_v of that section), every vertex carries an independent mark uniform on {0, …, t_v − 1} and M is the largest mark. The document counts each mark down from the top of its range instead, and obtains a minimum: n + 1 − M has the distribution of the minimum over all vertices v of G_v + U_v, where G_v = n + 1 − t_v measures how far the subtree of v falls short of the whole, and U_v is uniform on {1, …, t_v} (its Theorem 3.1). In the notation of section 2.1 the event n + 1 − M = r + 1 is the event that M stands at the position of deficit r, so this is a statement about the row read from its right end. The document reads it as a point process: each vertex puts a point at the place G_v, near the origin when its subtree is large, and throws from there one uniform mark over a range of length t_v; the deficit of M is the first place that is hit. If the numbers of points at the places g = 1, …, n were independent Poisson variables with means μ(g), the row would have a closed form: the probability that no place up to r is hit is exp(−Σ(r)) with Σ(r) = Σ_{g<r} μ(g)·(r−g)/(n+1−g), and the probability m_r of deficit r is exp(−Σ(r))·(1 − exp(−ν(r))) with ν(r) = Σ_{g≤r} μ(g)/(n+1−g). The quotient μ(g)/(n+1−g) is the mean number of points at g divided by the length of the range over which each spreads its mark, that is, the rate at which the points at g hit any one given place. The document proves (its Theorem 4.1), for any means μ, that if this rate does not increase for g from 1 up to some R, then m_1, …, m_R are log-concave, and that they decrease, whatever μ is, once ν(r) exceeds log 2; the two together give a unimodal row. The peak is where two rates balance: the number of points that can hit the next place grows with r, and the probability that nothing has been hit yet falls. For the true mean number of subtrees of each size the balance falls at a deficit of about 0.73·n^(2/3), and this is the document's account of why the peak lies at a deficit of order n^(2/3) (the true constant in the limit is 0.60).

The true points are not independent, and the document says where the idealization fails. For the positions in the right half of a row only the subtrees with more than n/2 vertices matter; these are nested, and their places G_1 < G_2 < … have the law of the partial sums of independent gaps with the Borel distribution, multiplied by a factor that depends on the last point: a renewal sequence with Borel gaps, slightly tilted. The condition of the theorem holds for its true means up to about g = n/6. What is lost is the step of the proof that uses independence: in a renewal sequence the probability that nothing has been hit up to r and the number of points that can hit the next place are functions of the same points, and their joint expectation has to be handled directly. On the left half the large subtrees are not nested, and the points form a branching structure. The document proves the minimum representation and the theorem on the idealization, and says plainly that it contains no complete proof for all n. It leaves two conjectures. The first (its Conjecture 6.4) is the missing statement on the right half: that the minimum of place plus uniform mark has a unimodal distribution on the deficits up to n/2, for every renewal sequence whose renewal density does not increase and, as a separate statement, for the tilted Borel sequence of the forest. Its experiments show that some condition on the gaps is needed: with Borel, geometric or power-law gaps the computed distribution was unimodal; with gaps all equal to 5, or with two widely separated gap lengths, it was not. The second (Conjecture 6.3) belongs to the left half and is stated below with the result that uses it.

At 04:10 the user had the session send subagents at the open places (five were sent), with the instruction to face the core of the problem and to reason about the mathematical objects in preference to computing. Their five reports were finished between 05:33 and 06:38 (`sources/between/structure-document/시도/`), and the session passed corrections among them while they worked, so that they are not fully independent of one another. Three of their results matter for what followed.

The first is the *staircase reduction* of the report on free exploration (its Theorem A). Consider the two statements

 (Col) for all n and k: if d(n,k) ≥ 0 then d(n+1,k) ≥ 0,
 (Diag) for all n and k: if d(n,k) ≤ 0 then d(n+1,k+1) ≤ 0.

The first says that a step which is non-negative stays so in the next row of its column; the second that a step which is non-positive stays so one place down its diagonal. Suppose row n is unimodal: its steps are non-negative up to some position κ and non-positive after it. Then (Col) makes the steps of row n + 1 non-negative up to κ, and (Diag) makes them non-positive from κ + 2 on. Only the step at κ + 1 is left open, and whichever sign it has, row n + 1 is unimodal, with its turning position at κ or at κ + 1. So if (Col) and (Diag) hold from some row on and that row is unimodal, every later row is unimodal and the turning position moves by zero or one from each row to the next: the signs of d form a staircase. Since both statements are weak inequalities, the argument allows steps that are zero. The report also proved a converse for the case that no step is zero (if all rows are unimodal and the peak moves by zero or one, then (Col) and (Diag) hold), and found no violation of either statement, and no zero step, in exact rows up to n = 140.

The second is the corresponding reduction for the right half alone, in the report on the Borel chain. It concerns the positions with n ≤ 2k − 1, where the subtrees above the level form one chain, and the statements

 (C) if d(n,k) > 0 and n + 1 ≤ 2k − 1, then d(n+1,k) > 0,
 (D) if d(n,k) ≤ 0, then d(n+1,k+1) ≤ 0.

These are (Col), with strict signs, and (Diag), each restricted to that range. Together they imply that no row rises again after falling in its right half: if d(n,k) ≤ 0 there, then d(n−1,k) ≤ 0 by (C) read backwards, and then d(n,k+1) ≤ 0 by (D). Both became nodes of the later instance, the first under the same letter and the second under the name (Diag₁); the letter (D) was given there to another statement (section 6.4), and from section 6 on this account uses it only for that one. The report also derived, for a fixed column k and 0 ≤ r ≤ k − 1, the equation

  d(k+r,k)/(k+r)! = Σ_{j=0}^{r} h_{r−j}·c_j, with h_i = (i+1)^i/i!,

in which c_0 = −k^(k−2)/k! is one negative "shock", coming from the trees with exactly k vertices, and each further c_j is an explicit combination of two entries of the smaller row k + j − 1. Statement (C) says that the solution of this equation, once positive, stays positive. The report explained why arguments by order fail on it: the weights h_i and the Borel masses behind them are log-convex, so that the shock is forgotten only at the rate of a power of r, and the comparison of weights that a single-crossing argument needs goes the wrong way.

The third is the *critical-tilt propagation* of the report on the left half. Fix a column k ≥ 2, and let δ_t = 0 for t < k, δ_k = −k^(k−2), and δ_t = (k+1)·b(t−1,k) − (k−1)·b(t−1,k−1) for t ≥ k + 1. This is the contribution to the step of a single tree with t vertices, the quantity that the earlier instance had met in its five pieces (section 4.8); the structure document calls it the curvature of a tree of size t. Choosing one tree of a forest and leaving the rest gives the lower bound

  d(n,k) ≥ Σ_{t=k}^{n} C(n,t)·δ_t·A(n−t, k−1),

in which what is dropped is non-negative. Let x_k = (k/(k+1))^(k−1) and let n₀ ≥ 2k + 1. The theorem has three hypotheses: (A1) the sequence δ_t changes sign once for k ≤ t ≤ n₀, from non-positive to non-negative; (A2) the numbers A(N,k−1)·x_k^N/N! do not increase with N; (A3) the sum Σ_{t=k}^{n₀} δ_t·x_k^t/t! is non-negative. Its conclusion is that d(n,k) ≥ 0 for all n ≥ n₀, and that δ_t > 0 for all t > n₀. The proof multiplies the lower bound by x_k^n/n!. It becomes a sum of the terms δ_t·x_k^t/t!, which change sign once, against the weights A(n−t,k−1)·x_k^(n−t)/(n−t)!, which by (A2) do not decrease in t; such a sum is at least the weight at the change of sign times the unweighted sum, and that is non-negative by (A3). A non-negative step d(n,k) in turn makes δ_(n+1) positive, so both the sign pattern and the non-negative sum pass to the next row. Hypothesis (A2) is the growth inequality of the earlier instance (section 4.8), which the report takes from a lemma of the manuscript, stated there for k ≥ 43, and from exact computation for the smaller columns (the earlier instance's own record has it for every k ≥ 2). What is left open in each column is therefore (A1) and (A3), and with n₀ = 2k + 1 both speak of rows up to 2k only: the whole left half of a column would follow from two statements about its first layer. Conjecture 6.3 of the structure document is (A1) for all t. In the report's computations, exact up to k = 220 and in floating point up to k = 2000 and at three larger columns, (A3) holds at n₀ = 2k + 1 precisely for the columns k ≥ 186; that it holds for every k ≥ 186 the report leaves as a conjecture. The columns 2 ≤ k ≤ 220 were closed for n ≥ 2k + 1 with exact computation, except at the four positions (5,2), (7,3), (9,4) and (11,5), where the step is negative. The reports also added exact counterexamples to a list of stronger properties one might hope for.

Statements about the sign of d along columns thus came, on this night, to stand at the centre as reductions of the root. They were not new. A proposition of the same kind had been proved in the earlier instance on 10 November and had not been made a node (section 4.8). The two are not the same statement. Both use the column statement. The earlier record's implication therefore needs no first unimodal row and concludes unimodality only; the diagonal rule, and the fact that the peak moves by at most one, appear in it only in the converse, where they are shown to be equivalent to each other under the assumption that no two neighbouring entries of a row are equal. One statement of that night was not in the handed-over material, and the root's proof would in the end rest on it. Write q₀(k) for the crossing row of column k, the first row n ≥ k + 1 at which the step d(n,k) is positive. In words: as long as column k has not turned positive, column k+1 has not either. Together with (Col), which says that a column that has turned non-negative stays so, this gives the root: in any row the columns that have not yet turned form a final segment, so the row's non-positive steps come after its positive ones. The reduction was not new to the research as a whole. What happened here for the first time is that it was put on the frontier, as a node with a relation to the root, and worked on.

The record of 02:22 (L seq 9) also contains the working form of the equation of a column. Write D_k(x) = Σ_n d(n,k)·xⁿ/n! for the exponential generating function of column k, and A_{k−1}(x) for that of the parking functions whose displacements are all smaller than k. The handed-over reports, and the instance's first records after them, had the identity D_k = A_{k−1}·𝒯_k, in which 𝒯_k is a series built from single trees, and had worked with it in this form. The record divided by A_{k−1} instead. Let c(m,k) be the number of prime parking functions (section 4.2) of length m with all displacements below k; then 1/A_{k−1} = 1 − Σ_m c(m,k)·x^m/m!, and comparing coefficients in D_k/A_{k−1} = 𝒯_k gives, for n ≥ k + 1,

 d(n,k) = (k+1)·d(n−1,k) + 2·b(n−1,k−1) + X(n,k) + Σ_{m=1}^{n−k} C(n,m)·c(m,k)·d(n−m,k),

where X(n,k) ≥ 0 vanishes for n < 2k, and b(k,k) is read as 0, so that the column begins with the entry d(k,k) = −b(k,k−1) = −k^(k−2). Every coefficient is non-negative, and after a suitable exponential tilt the kernel c(m,k)·x^m/m! is non-increasing in m. The only negative input is that first entry: the "shock" of the tree of exactly the threshold size. In the form multiplied by A_{k−1} the kernel had been log-convex, and arguments by order had pointed the wrong way, as the record remarks. A column of steps is thus the response of a positive linear system to one negative impulse, with positive sources added at each row; (C) and (Col) say that the response, once it has recovered, does not dip again. The record also proved a diagonal lower bound, b(n,k) ≥ (1 + 1/k)^(k−2)·n·b(n−1,k−1) for all k ≥ 2 and n ≥ k + 1, with equality only at n = k + 1, from the fact that the maximum displacement of a random parking function of length n lies, in distribution, between that for length n − 1 and that plus one.

### 6.6 The first Lean proof

At 15:02 on 21 November the instance's first formal proof was published: the theorem `Parking.mono265`, the statement of (Mono_265) (L seq 62, `r3e5dcd7e`, commit `17b014e`). It has 26 modules and about twelve thousand lines, uses no table, and depends on no axioms beyond the three standard ones.

How it was built matters for everything formal that followed, since every later formal record starts from the same file of definitions. The definitions are those of the root: a preference is a function from Fin n to Fin n, cars park by the first-free-space rule, and b and A are cardinalities of sets of such functions (`lean/Parking/Defs.lean`, which every later formal record copies unchanged and every later review compares with the root). From the process the development proves one combinatorial fact, the last-car recurrence, in the form A(n+1,k) = Σ_j min(j+1,k+1)·C(n,j)·A(j,k)·A(n−j,k). Beside it, four elementary facts are proved directly from the definitions: b(n,0) = A(n,0); b(n,ℓ+1) + A(n,ℓ) = A(n,ℓ+1), which in Lean is a lemma, both counts being defined as cardinalities; A(0,k) = 1; and A(n,k) ≤ A(n,k′) for k ≤ k′. Everything else is algebra of sequences and formal power series built on that recurrence: a "single-chain lemma" gives, for n ≤ 2k + 1, the column equation, the diagonal lower bound, and the expansion of a step as a curvature term minus a boundary term. The estimate of the cross product is a statement about weighted compositions. Cut a segment of length m into consecutive parts, give a part of length j the weight j^(j−1)/j! and each cut point s a factor f(s), and let V_q be the total weight of the compositions with q parts. The proof needs three comparisons between V_q and V_{q+1}, for three choices of the factors, which the informal proofs had obtained by separate arguments, the erasing rule by a walk on compositions and the comparison with the middle factor by an involution that exchanges the lengths of two parts. In Lean all three come from one identity: if P is the strictly upper triangular matrix with entries P_{ij} = (j−i)^(j−i−1)/(j−i)! for i < j, and δP the matrix with entries (j−i)·P_{ij}, then (δP)·P = P·(δP) = δP − P, which is a case of Abel's binomial identity; V_q is an entry of a product of copies of P and of the diagonal matrix of the factors, and the identity is applied between the factors of that product. Forests, on which every informal proof of these facts rests, do not occur in the Lean source at all. The same holds of the final proof of the root: the audit of what `Parking.root_unimodal` depends on lists, from the modules that speak of the process, the definitions, the recurrence with the lemmas of its proof, and the four facts just named (`r3d3d37ac`, `evidence/peer/builds/modules/Parking.LP.DepAudit.stdout.txt`; one of the checkers of this account also searched the 130 Lean files of that record for the objects of the process and for graphs, forests and permutations, and did not read the proof bodies of the other modules).

Two thirds of the lines came from a formalizer session that had been cancelled. It had been started on the first proof of (Mono_265) at 23:42 on 20 November and stopped at 01:37, as that proof was being set back (the commit that records the set-back is of 01:39); its unpublished work was the base on which a second formalizer formalized the shortened proof.

### 6.7 The right half stands

In the night of 21 to 22 November the nodes began to stand under the settled standard, and by 10:48 the right half was proved.

At 23:31 (D) and (CB) received their first accepted solutions (L seq 78, `r26911cae`, commit `7114454`). Each is one premise of an accepted relation, (D) towards (T_R) and (CB) towards (L), and each relation has a premise that was never proved, the node (E) in the first and the node (Base) in the second. The proof of (D) is a theorem that holds in every column k ≥ 3. Fix k and the tilt x_k. Say that three positive numbers u, v, w are convex in the tilted sense if e^λ·w + e^(−λ)·u ≥ 2·e^(λ/k)·v for every λ ≥ 0; for λ = 0 this is u + w ≥ 2v, and for λ > 0 it asks that the convexity survive an exponential tilt, with a margin. The theorem has three steps. First, the tilted Cayley sequence ω_n = (n+1)^(n−1)·x_kⁿ/n! is convex in this sense at every position: any three consecutive terms ω_{n−1}, ω_n, ω_{n+1} satisfy the inequality. Second, the property passes to sums of pairs: for each ρ with 1 ≤ ρ ≤ k − 2, put q_M = Σ ω_a·ω_b over a + b = M with a, b ≥ ρ; then the three numbers q_M at M = k + ρ − 2, k + ρ − 1 and k + ρ are convex in the tilted sense. Here the range of the sum grows with M, and an identity for convolutions makes the terms at the ends of the range cancel exactly; the record notes that this step has no computation in it at all. Third, by the last-car recurrence the cumulative counts of the first layer are built from such sums by positive combinations and by factors that preserve the property, a reduction published on the first evening (L seq 2 and 3); one further comparison is needed on the way, which the record closes by hand for k ≥ 7 and by exact arithmetic in ten small cases for 3 ≤ k ≤ 6. The inequality (D) then follows for every k ≥ 3 (the record's Corollary A2). The three proofs of (D) published before had all been confined to k ≥ 148 or k ≥ 151, thresholds that, in the record's words, came from the bookkeeping and not from the object. The node asks only for k ≥ 301, so each of them had proved it; what was new is a proof with no threshold and, in the node's range, no table. Two further independent proofs offered the same night were declined as solutions because this one existed.

(CB) says that the entries of column k − 1, weighted by x_kⁿ/n!, do not increase from row 2k + 1 on. Its proof does not work on these entries but on the prime parking functions whose maximum displacement is exactly k − 1. Their weighted counts satisfy a linear recurrence in which both the kernel and the source decrease, and in such a recurrence a value that is no larger than all the values before it is never exceeded again; a count over an interval of rows shows that such a value occurs before row k + ⌈3k/5⌉, and a criterion published with the node carries the resulting descent to column k − 1 in every row from 2k + 1. This was the argument of the record of 07:59 that morning (L seq 45), which had brought the range down from k ≥ 30000 and had been declined because two of its estimates could be replaced by shorter ones. A second version in the afternoon (L seq 64) moved the interval of the count, so that one line bounding the kernel replaced the longest estimate, and was declined for a single paragraph that proved something the induction did not need; the version accepted at 23:31 is that one without the paragraph and without one further refinement of a constant. In the end nothing rested on (CB). The relation for which it had been posed never received its other premise, and neither the informal nor the formal proof of (L) uses it.

At 04:11 (C) and (Col₁) were accepted (L seq 87, `rb18f76a7`, commit `659c34f`), by a proof that contains no new argument and assembles three stages published since the first night. (In this paragraph and the next δ, u, ε, γ and κ are the letters of these records and are used only here.) Divide the column equation of section 6.2 by n!: with δ_n = d(n,k)/n!, u_n = b(n,k−1)/n! and ε_m = (m−1)^(m−1)/m!, it reads in the first layer δ_n = (2/n)·u_{n−1} + ((k+1)/n)·δ_{n−1} + Σ_m ε_m·δ_{n−m}. The step at row n is a positive source, an entry of column k − 1, plus what the earlier rows of the column feed back; the rows before the crossing are negative, so what they feed back is a debt, and (C) says that after the crossing the debt never again outweighs the source and the rows that have already turned. The first stage is a theorem of the record that had posed (Mono) (L seq 9, its Theorem P). Weight row j by γ^(−j), where γ = (1 + 1/k)^(k−2) is the constant of the diagonal lower bound. In this weighting that bound says two things: the net debt of column k up to any row (the sum of its steps so far, with the sign reversed) is at most the weighted entry of column k − 1 in that row, and that entry cannot fall, between the crossing row and a later row, by more than the sum of the positive steps in between. The weights κ_m = γ^(−m)·ε_m of the feedback decrease as long as m is below about 3k/5, and their ratios κ_{m+1}/κ_m increase. From these facts alone, with no estimate of any size, a step at a row n ≤ 2k − 1 after the crossing row q cannot be the first to fail to be positive, provided the weights are still decreasing at lag n − k and n·κ_{n−k} ≤ q·κ_{q−k}. The condition concerns two rows only, the crossing row and the row in question, which is why the records call it the two-point criterion; and since (k+r)·κ_r decreases up to r ≈ 0.47·k, every column keeps its sign from the crossing up to that deficit (at least up to deficit ⌊2k/5⌋ + 1), without computation and for every k ≥ 2. The second stage (L seq 30) carries the sign on to deficit 3k/5, where the weights stop decreasing, by a variant of the criterion that needs only that the positive stretch since the crossing is at least about 0.8·k^(2/3) rows long when the window ends; this holds for k ≥ 64 when the crossing lies within deficit ϖ(k) = ⌊2k/5⌋ + 3 − ⌈0.8·k^(2/3)⌉. Beyond deficit 3k/5 signs no longer suffice. The third stage (L seq 51 and 68) uses the equation once more: if the rows since the crossing are non-negative, then δ_n ≥ (2/n)·u_{n−1}·(1 − Ξ), where Ξ collects a loss for the positive stretch being only so long and the feedback from lags beyond 3k/5, the latter compared with the one entry u_{n−1} through the equation that column k − 1 itself satisfies. An explicit bound gives Ξ < 1 in every row up to 2k when the crossing lies within deficit 27k/100, first for k ≥ 95 and then for k ≥ 82, the last column that the hand arithmetic reaches: in the worst row Ξ is 0.99965 at k = 81, too close to 1 for the closed bounds, and exceeds 1 at k = 80, where the criterion itself fails.

All three stages take the place of the crossing as given. It is known without computation for k ≥ 265, by the strip bound of section 6.5, and for 125 ≤ k ≤ 264 by a theorem of 21 November that puts the crossing within deficit k/4 (Theorem J, section 6.8); for 82 ≤ k ≤ 124 the proof reads one sign per column from the paper's table, 43 signs in rows 101 to 149. So for k ≥ 82 column k is non-positive before q₀(k) and positive from there to row 2k, which contains both nodes. For k ≤ 81 only the first stage is available: inside its window the proof cites it, and outside it reads the instances from the table, 1,632 positions for (C) in rows up to 161. This is the form the ninth input had made possible, six published claims and a finite remainder. What the table decides in it is where the crossing lies in the columns 82 to 124 and why the sign persists beyond the window in the columns up to 81.

The record's own new structural argument, which bounds the feedback of a column by that of an extreme column using the log-convexity of a resolvent kernel, was published as supported claims and declined as the solution: its part for the columns from 49 on could be replaced by the citations and the table of the other, and it proved again a theorem already published; the reviewer's first draft had decided the other way and was withdrawn.

At 10:48 (CI) was accepted, by a shortened form of an argument declined twelve hours before (L seq 89, `rdc057f21`, commit `5f39bdb`): the step is bounded below by a count of ordered pairs of heavy vertices, the vertices of the forest whose subtree has more than k vertices, minus the share of a single vertex whose subtree has exactly k.  for k ≥ 44: if the step of column k is non-negative at some row n ≤ k + k/3, then it is negative before the first such row, non-negative from it up to deficit 3k/5, and positive in every row after that, with d(n,k) ≥ 2·(1 − 5.86/√k)·b(n−1,k−1) for all n ≥ 2k + 1.

The proof works on the column equation in the critical tilt. With x = x_k, η_n = xⁿ·d(n,k)/n! and U_n = xⁿ·b(n,k−1)/n!, the equation of section 6.2 becomes

 η_n = σ_n + ((k+1)·x/n)·η_{n−1} + Σ_{m=1}^{n−k} κ_m·η_{n−m},  σ_n ≥ (2x/n)·U_{n−1},

a positive source, which is the neighbouring entry of column k − 1, plus a memory of the earlier rows of the column with positive weights κ_m. The rows before the crossing are negative, and the question is whether the source outweighs what the memory carries forward of them. Two mechanisms answer it in two ranges of rows, and the theorem is the observation that they can be joined. Just after the crossing the proof uses the two-point criterion of section 6.7 (in its own weighting, by powers of (k/(k+1))^(k−2)). The record that had proved (C) at 04:11 that day had shown, in the part of it that was declined as a solution, that the condition of the criterion holds for every row up to deficit 3k/5 whenever the crossing lies within deficit k/3. ## 7. Looking across the two instances

### 7.1 The user's interventions

The standing inputs have been described where they fell. Set side by side they show one concern pursued for two weeks through two instances, and they explain why the later instance did not simply repeat the earlier one.

In the earlier instance the user intervened on the conduct of the research and left the standard of review alone; each of the inputs of 9 November says so. The texts of 8 November describe a research that confirms more and understands no more, and decline to instruct. The decision of 9 November on computation asks for more thinking and less range-filling. The input of that night names what is wanted, the reason why the whole row is unimodal, and corrects the operator's suggestion that thin margins might mean there is no reason. The sessions responded as far as conduct can respond: they stopped certifying columns, they wrote sections on the question, their reviews recorded each time that the question remained open. But, as this account reads it, a proof by regions and certificates was by then half built, the acceptance of a proof did not depend on its being short or explanatory, and the root was closed two days later in the form it had.

In the later instance the same concern was built into the terms, and then, when that proved not to be enough, into the standard of acceptance itself. The rules of the handoff restricted computation and required everything to be proved anew. The second and third inputs, on the first day, again concern conduct: prove and do not measure; face the core; cite what exists and do not reinvent it; and, in the user's words in the third input, where something is already complete but its logic is untidy, work to make it concise, to remove premises, to delete conditions, to widen its range, to connect what had seemed separate. The fifth input, a day later, made that last request a condition of acceptance, and the seventh extended it to the proofs of relations. The fifth input states this link itself, in a section that the operator marks as its own reading and not the user's words: the third had asked it of the way sessions work, the fifth makes it part of what a review requires. The user's quoted sentence in the fifth does not mention the third. The sixth said how the judgment is to be made, from the proof and not from counts. The eighth, ninth and tenth then each took back something that had made the standard stricter than the user meant: the eighth, that shortening is for economy of formalization and that computation as such counts against a proof; the ninth, that a finite remainder of a few thousand facts read from the paper's table is computation of the unwelcome kind; the tenth, that a wrong side sentence sinks a correct claim.

The operator's part in this deserves a plain statement, because the sources are explicit about it. The operator wrote the inputs; from the third on it quoted the user and marked its own readings as its own. Four times it changed the published verdicts of reviews directly, on five solutions and two relations, each change marked in the file as the operator's and not a re-review (commits `5ee6d95`, `4383b3b`, `41a6aae`, `c7a1f6e`): three times on the user's instruction or decision, and the fourth time, in withdrawing the two relations, it applied to them, on its own judgment, the standard that the user had just extended to the proofs of relations, as the seventh input says. After 21 November 08:37 the operator changed no verdict; from then on the reviews applied the standard of the inputs themselves, and of the thirty-three solutions that stand refused at the commit considered here, twenty-eight were refused by reviews. ### 7.2 The role of computation

Computation did three different jobs in this research, and the history of the user's interventions is largely the history of separating them.

The first job is experiment: exact rows computed from the last-car recurrence, in which candidate properties are tested and counterexamples found. Both instances lived on it. The earlier instance's first records found the failure of log-concavity at (80,40) this way; the later instance and the night of 19 November refuted a long list of stronger properties by exact witnesses, and several structural theorems were first seen in tables. No input objects to this job. The second input of the later instance objects to a degenerate form of it, scanning large ranges to see where something breaks before having an argument for which the scan is needed.

The second job is the base case: a finite range that no general argument can reach because the statement behaves differently there. The central step is negative for 2 ≤ n ≤ 15; the increase of a column from row 2k + 1 on fails at four small positions, (5,2), (7,3), (9,4) and (11,5). Every proof in this history has such a base.

The third job is to stand in for an argument: to close a range that an argument might cover but does not yet. The earlier instance's proof used computation in this way throughout. All rows up to n = 20000, the right half up to n = 50000, whole columns up to k = 3130, and inequalities in the general range verified cell by cell in interval or double-precision arithmetic: these are where its general arguments begin, and, as this account reads the records, their ranges were set by where estimates with explicit constants happened to become strong enough. The user's decision of 9 November stopped the widening of such ranges but could not change the shape of a proof already built on them.

The later instance was founded on the refusal of the third job, and then had to find where the line runs. The handoff allowed tables for n ≤ 30 only. The fourth input admitted the paper's rows n ≤ 300, as something already computed. For a day proofs leaned on those rows, and on the paper's larger table to n = 600, as freely as they were allowed; the fifth and sixth inputs then set proofs back for what they could shed, and in that night the larger table and the paper's rational computation went out of the chain that leads to (Mono), and thresholds fell by orders of magnitude (a single-crossing theorem from k ≥ 10⁸ to k ≥ 350; the column bound (CB) from k ≥ 30000 to k ≥ 148). The larger table stayed in the proof of (Col) of that night, and in the diagonal statements until the evening of 22 November (L seq 103). The seventh input extended the standard to the proofs of relations the next morning. The eighth and ninth inputs fixed the line in the user's words: not the presence of computation, nor the number of cells read, but whether a part of a statement is being shown true by a large computation that does not explain why it is true. A remainder of a few thousand facts in rows up to 162 was ruled to be acceptable.

What computation remains in the two final proofs of the later instance can be stated exactly. The informal proof of (L), hence of the root on the left, reads from the paper's table 1,360 facts for the columns 21 to 44 and 48 signs for the crossings of the columns 49 to 96 (the columns 45 to 48 follow from q₀(49) = 64 and the strict increase of q₀, which are also table facts), and uses the base n ≤ 30 for the columns 6 to 20; for k ≥ 97 it uses no table. The formal proof has the kernel compute 6,118 facts in rows n ≤ 300. In the formal proof the share of computation is larger than in the informal one, the columns 45 to 81 being closed by table where the informal proof has a theorem. Both reviews name the small columns of (L) and the position of the crossing as unexplained; the review of the formal proof adds the persistence of sign in the small columns of (C).

### 7.3 The role of Lean

In the earlier instance formalization began on the second day and was stopped on the fifth. It produced one accepted formal theorem, about an auxiliary statement that is a consequence of the root, and a formal model of the parking process with the count (n+1)^(n−1) and the last-car recurrence proved for the actual counts. When the pause came, five formalizations of nodes on the path to the root were in progress and were ended unpublished. Their state shows what formalizing that proof would have required: for the central step alone, an analytic theorem for n ≥ 19999 and a certificate in thousands of rows, of which the formalizer had compiled the rows up to 1808 in two days. The reviews of the right-half proof say that a Lean proof of it would have to rebuild each of the computer proofs it depends on.

In the later instance formalization was a condition of success from the start, and the fifth input placed the user's standard of acceptance before it: a proof is to be made as simple as it can be before it is formalized (the reason, by the eighth input, being conciseness itself and not the cost of formalizing). ### 7.4 What was carried from the earlier instance, and what the later one did that the earlier had not

Three kinds of thing can be distinguished.

What reached the later instance from the earlier one, through the manuscript, the night's reports and the paper, and was proved again there: the exact structure of the problem (the outcome permutation with its capacities, the forest representation, the single chain in the right half, the decomposition of a step into a curvature term and a boundary term, which is the earlier identity of ties and blocked maxima in another form (section 4.5)); the cross product of two consecutive steps; the critical tilt x_k and the growth inequality for the cumulative counts that goes with it; and the knowledge that the row is not log-concave and that a list of stronger properties fail. The last-car recurrence is from the literature and is present at every stage.

What existed in the earlier instance and did not reach the later one, and was found again or not used: the reduction of unimodal rows to columns that change sign once at a row that does not decrease, which the first sessions of the later instance found again, the night of 19 November having found the neighbouring reduction through the diagonal statement; the two certified counterexamples found again that night (found again, that is, by the inference stated in section 5.2); and the earlier proof itself, which the later instance could read in the manuscript but was not allowed to use: its cut of the left half into layers, its induction on the right, its certificates.

What the later instance did that the earlier had not. The reduction of the root to statements about columns is not among these things: the earlier instance had proved it, had found that the soft argument it had tried could not give the column statement, and had not made that statement a node. What the later instance did was to take that reduction as its road. It made (Col), (Mono) and (C) nodes and proved them, and (L) as a single statement for all k ≥ 6. It wrote the column equation in a form with a positive kernel and one negative impulse, and bounded the row at which a column turns positive, without computation for k ≥ 97. It extended the paper's theorem on the persistence of a decrease from a curve to a strip, in a closed form short enough to formalize. It proved the first-layer convexity without threshold. It joined two mechanisms into the theorem that a column which has crossed early never returns. And it formalized the root, in a Lean proof built from (C), (Mono), (L) and the strip theorem, in which some of these arguments are replaced by tables that the kernel computes (section 6.9). The earlier instance had explicit estimates beginning at n = 20001, k = 6668 or k = 3131 and computation below; the later instance has arguments beginning at k = 44, 97 or 265 and a few thousand table entries below.

What neither did is to answer the question the user put on 9 November. The later proof is shorter, and in this account's judgment its three column statements say more plainly than the earlier proof's many regions what unimodality consists of; but its reviews, like the earlier ones, record that parts of it are true only because a table says so, and four of the five questions with which the instance began, which ask for the explanation, were still open when the root was closed.

## 9. What was checked

**How the account was made.** Twenty-four source readers divided the material. Twelve read the 438 publications of the earlier instance in order of time, eight the 120 of the later, two the documents written between the instances, and two the running of each instance: its logs, settings, session files and inputs, and the memory notes of the operating sessions. For each publication they had its event, the record's description and claims, and the review's verdicts; they read in full the expositions and reviews of the records they judged decisive, and not of the others. One assembler wrote the account from their reports. It read for itself several of the standing inputs of the later instance, the two root statements and a few records, and nothing else of the primary material.

**What "proved" means here.** No one in this process verified a proof, compiled a Lean file or repeated a computation. "Proved", "certified" and "formally proved" mean that a record says so and that its review accepted it; for a formal proof, that the review reports having compiled it. The mathematical passages say what the records state and how their arguments go, as far as the readers followed them.

**The check against the sources.** Five checkers who had not written the account compared it with the primary files, sentence by sentence, each for a group of sections and for the rows of the milestone table that fall in its period; between them they covered section 2.3, sections 4 to 7 and every row of the table. Each row was opened at its coordinate. They found 10 statements wrong, 89 imprecise (a time, a number, a range, a name, or a paraphrase that said more or less than its source), 12 inferences stated as fact and 3 statements without support, and all of these were corrected or marked. The errors of most consequence were in the comparison of the two instances, which no single source reader had been placed to see. The account had said that the reduction to columns on which the later proof is built was absent from the earlier instance, where the earlier instance had proved it on 10 November; it had identified a node of the later instance with an inequality of the earlier one that concerns a different count; and it had understated how much of the earlier proof the later sessions were able to read.

**The reading tests and the return to the records.** A reader given only the text then reported what it had understood and where it could not follow. Its main findings were that the assembly of the final proof was nowhere shown, that nodes were named without being stated, and that the mathematical passages named ideas where they should have stated them. Four further readers went back to the records for these places, and the mathematical passages of sections 4.2 to 4.10, 5.2 to 5.4 and 6.2 to 6.9 were rewritten, and section 6.10 added, from their reports. On the way they found some thirty more places where the earlier wording had been wrong, among them the definition of the layers, the statement of the pair inequality, the mechanism of the entry theorem, the statement of Theorem N′, and the difference between the reductions of 10 and of 19 November. Two checkers then compared the rewritten passages of sections 4 and 6 with those reports, and with the records where the reports left a doubt: they found 39 places to correct, of which the ones that mattered were a range lost from the certified ratio of section 4.6, a formula made to carry three limits in section 4.8, and a factor counted twice in the description of the node (D); these were corrected. A second reader with nothing but the text read the revised account, and the contradictions and broken references it found between old and new passages were removed. Its remark that the proofs of the earlier instance are told more fully than some of the later one was met only in part.

**The checks that were repeated.** When the account was first finished, three comparisons were missing: two checkers had been stopped by a safeguard of the system before they could report, and the passages supplied by the last of the four readers had been put in as they came. All three were then given to checkers on another model. The passages rewritten from the third of the four reports (sections 4.1, 4.7, 4.11 and 5 and the last paragraph of section 6.9) were compared with that report and, where it left a doubt, with the sources: 15 findings, 2 of them statements that were wrong, all corrected. The passages on the proofs of (C), (CB), (CI), (Diag) and of (L) in the large columns and on the middle step were compared with the records: 10 findings, 2 of them wrong, all corrected; this checker did not finish looking through sections 7 and 8 for sentences that state the same facts in other words. Of the passages replaced after the first check, the 48 that still stand in the text and concern the days between the instances and the later instance (sections 5, 6.1 to 6.6, 7.1, 7.4 and 7.5 and rows of the milestone table) were each compared with the sources: none was wrong, 9 were imprecise and were corrected, and 2 could not be checked because they rest only on the private transcript (the times and the wording of the user's messages of 04:10 and of 11:09 and 11:37 on 19 November).

**What was not checked.** The other 53 passages replaced after the first check that still stand in the text concern the earlier instance and the comparison of the two instances (in sections 2.3, 3, 4 and 7 and in the milestone table). A checker was sent to compare them with the sources a second time and was stopped again by a safeguard before it reported; they were not sent a third time. They rest on the checker of the first round who proposed them or, where the assembler wrote them, on the assembler alone, except that the mathematical passages of sections 4.2 to 4.10 and the comparison of the two reductions in sections 4.8 and 5.2 were afterwards rewritten and compared as described above.
