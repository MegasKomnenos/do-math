## Description

For every $n\ge2$ the row $b(n,0),\dots,b(n,n-1)$ of the numbers of parking functions of length $n$ by maximum displacement rises strictly up to a position $p$, has an entry after $p$, if there is one, that is not larger than $b(n,p)$, and falls strictly from there on; and the row $n+1$ has its peak at the same position $p$, or a strict peak at $p+1$.

## Statement

Let $b(n,\ell)$ be the number of parking functions of length $n$ whose maximum displacement is exactly $\ell$ (`Parking.b`). For every natural number $n\ge2$ there is a natural number $p$ with the following two properties.

1. The row $n$ of the array $b$ has its peak at $p$ (`Parking.IsPeak`): $p\le n-1$; $b(n,k-1)<b(n,k)$ for $1\le k\le p$; $b(n,p+1)\le b(n,p)$ if $p+1\le n-1$; and $b(n,k)<b(n,k-1)$ for $p+2\le k\le n-1$.
2. The row $n+1$ has its peak at $p$ as well, or it has a strict peak at $p+1$ (`Parking.IsStrictPeak`). That is, one of the following holds:
   - $b(n+1,k-1)<b(n+1,k)$ for $1\le k\le p$; $b(n+1,p+1)\le b(n+1,p)$; and $b(n+1,k)<b(n+1,k-1)$ for $p+2\le k\le n$;
   - $b(n+1,k-1)<b(n+1,k)$ for $1\le k\le p+1$; and $b(n+1,k)<b(n+1,k-1)$ for $p+2\le k\le n$.

As chains: the row $n$ satisfies
$$b(n,0)<\dots<b(n,p)\ \ge\ b(n,p+1)>\dots>b(n,n-1),$$
and the row $n+1$ satisfies
$$b(n+1,0)<\dots<b(n+1,p)\ \ge\ b(n+1,p+1)>\dots>b(n+1,n)$$
or
$$b(n+1,0)<\dots<b(n+1,p+1)>b(n+1,p+2)>\dots>b(n+1,n).$$
The first chain ends with $b(n,n-1)$ and the other two with $b(n+1,n)$; where the position of the peak is the last entry of its row, the part of the chain after it is absent.

## Explanation

This record holds the account of the proof as a whole. It says what the staircase asserts, how it is reduced to two rules about the signs of the steps, how the array divides into regions, which statement gives each rule in each region, and whether that statement is obtained from an exactly computed table or from an analytic argument. The proofs of the statements it names are in their own records.

### What the staircase says

Arrange the numbers $b(n,k)$ in an array whose rows are indexed by the length $n$ and whose columns by the displacement $k$, $0\le k\le n-1$, and write
$$d_{n,k}=b(n,k)-b(n,k-1),\qquad 1\le k\le n-1,$$
for the *step of the column $k$ in the row $n$*. The staircase says two things. About one row it says that the steps are positive up to a position $p$, that the step $p+1$ is zero or negative, and that all later steps are negative. So the row rises strictly to its largest entry $b(n,p)$ and falls strictly after it, with one possible exception: $b(n,p+1)$ may be equal to $b(n,p)$. About two consecutive rows it says that the peak of the row $n+1$ is at the position $p$ or at $p+1$, and that in the second case the row $n+1$ has no two equal neighbours.

The position $p$ is determined by the row, as the number of its positive steps (see `Parking.IsPeak`); write $p_n$ for it. The rows $n=2,\dots,6$ are
$$(2,1),\quad(6,7,3),\quad(24,51,34,16),\quad(120,421,377,253,125),\quad(720,3963,4594,3688,2546,1296),$$
with $p_2=0$, $p_3=p_4=p_5=1$ and $p_6=2$. When the length grows by one, the peak stays where it is or moves one position to the right, and the graph of $p_n$ against $n$ is a staircase.

The unimodality of every row, which is the root proposition, follows by giving up the strict inequalities and the relation between consecutive rows (`Parking.unimodal_via_staircase_proof`). In the other direction the staircase leaves two things open. It does not give $p_n$ as a function of $n$. And it does not decide whether the equality $b(n,p_n+1)=b(n,p_n)$ ever occurs: it confines an equality of neighbours to this one place, in a row whose peak is at the same position as that of the row before. A computation with exact integers from the last-car recursion, which is not part of the proof, found no step equal to zero among the positions $(n,k)$ with $n\le319$ and $k\le279$.

### The reduction to two rules

The staircase is obtained from two rules about the signs of the steps when the length grows by one. Both are stated for $n\ge2$ and $1\le k\le n-1$.

- **The column rule** (`Parking.column_rule`): if $d_{n,k}>0$, then $d_{n+1,k}>0$. A positive step stays positive one row down in the same column.
- **The diagonal rule** (`Parking.diagonal_rule`): if $d_{n,k}\le0$, then $d_{n+1,k+1}<0$. A step that is not positive is followed by a negative step one row down and one column to the right.

For an arbitrary array of natural numbers these two rules imply the conclusion of the staircase; this is `Parking.peak_induction`, an induction on the row that uses nothing else. If the row $n$ has its peak at $p$, the column rule keeps the steps $1,\dots,p$ of the row $n+1$ positive, the diagonal rule makes its steps $p+2,\dots,n$ negative, and the one step that neither rule reaches, the step $p+1$, decides whether the peak stays or moves. Read for the columns, the column rule says that a column which has turned positive stays positive, and the diagonal rule says that the column $k+1$ turns positive strictly later than the column $k$. The positive steps of the row $n$ are then the steps of the columns that have turned positive by the row $n$, and $p_n$ is the number of these columns; passing to the row $n+1$, at most one more column has turned positive.

All the content of the staircase that concerns parking functions is therefore in the two rules. Both rules conclude with a strict inequality, and the diagonal rule starts from a step that may be zero. This is more than the root proposition asks: unimodality allows equal neighbours everywhere in a row, while the staircase allows them in one place only. Wherever a rule is derived from the sign of a step at certain positions, a step equal to zero has therefore to be excluded there. This is the reason why the proof computes three tables of strict signs, which the section "What is computed" lists, and why it proves the propagation of the sign down a column with a strict conclusion (`Parking.propagate_pos`).

### The regions of the array

The column $k$ has a step in every row $n\ge k+1$. The rows $k+1\le n\le2k-1$ are the *first layer* of the column; some records call them the chain range of the column. The row $n=2k$ is the *middle row* of the column, and the rows $n\ge2k$, the middle row included, lie *beyond the first layer*. The first layer of the column $1$ is empty.

The two parts of a column behave differently. Beyond the first layer the step is positive at every position except eleven, which lie in the columns $k\le7$. In the first layer a column $k\ge9$ turns positive: there is a row of the layer in which its step is positive, by the table `Parking.Tab.two_signs` for $k\le264$ and by `Parking.curve_crossing` for $k\ge265$, and from the first such row to the end of the layer the step stays positive. The first row in which the step of a column is positive is called its *first positive row*; some records call it the crossing row of the column. The columns $2\le k\le8$ are negative in their whole first layer, by the table of negative cells described below, and turn positive in the row $2k$, $2k+1$ or $2k+2$.

Each rule is proved region by region. For the column rule the region is that of the position $(n+1,k)$ of the conclusion, and for the diagonal rule that of the position $(n,k)$ of the hypothesis. Within a region the small columns are treated by exact computation, entirely or up to a certain row, and the large ones by an analytic argument; in the columns $82$ to $264$ the two are combined. The dividing columns are $82$, for the persistence of the sign in the first layer and for the signs beyond it, and $265$, for the statements that need a theorem on the first positive row of a column. The column $82$ is the one from which the explicit estimates of the analytic arguments for persistence and for the rows beyond the first layer hold. The two analytic arguments about the first positive row and about the diagonal step, the curve increase and the diagonal step behind the curve, are carried out from the column $265$ on, and the tables reach exactly as far as is needed to meet them. The following sections go through the regions in this order.

### The column rule in the first layer

If the conclusion of the column rule lies in the first layer of the column, $n+1\le2k-1$, the rule is the persistence of the sign, `Parking.step_persists`: for $k\ge2$ and $k+1\le n\le2k-2$, $d_{n,k}>0$ implies $d_{n+1,k}>0$. Its iterated form `Parking.step_persists_iter` says that a column which is positive in one row of its first layer is positive in every later row of that layer. For $2\le k\le81$ the persistence is read from the table `Parking.Tab.small_table`. For $k\ge82$ it is proved by an analytic argument in three statements. `Parking.positive_row_early` gives a row $k+r$ with $1\le r\le27k/100$ in which the column is positive. `Parking.persist` shows: if the step of the column is not positive in the rows from $k$ to $q-1$ and positive in the row $q$, where $k+1\le q\le k+k/3$, then it is positive in every row from $q$ to $2k-1$. `Parking.one_sign_change` applies this to the first positive row $q$ of the column, which is at most the row $k+r$, and obtains the signs of the column from the row $k$ to the row $2k-1$: not positive above the row $q$, positive from the row $q$ on. Persistence in the column follows at once. The records of these statements give the account. Persistence starts from a positive step. It says nothing about a step that is zero, and the column rule does not need it to.

### The signs beyond the first layer

Both rules are settled beyond the first layer by one statement, `Parking.step_pos_beyond`: for $k\ge1$ and $n\ge2k$ the step $d_{n,k}$ is positive, unless $n=2k$ and $k\le7$, or $n=2k+1$ and $2\le k\le5$. These are the eleven positions
$$(2,1),\ (4,2),\ (6,3),\ (8,4),\ (10,5),\ (12,6),\ (14,7)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(5,2),\ (7,3),\ (9,4),\ (11,5),$$
seven in the middle row of their column and four in the row after it, and at each of them the step is negative. At the first of them, $n=2$ and $k=1$, this is the inequality $b(2,1)=1<2=b(2,0)$. The other ten are cells of the table `Parking.Tab.negative_cells`, which gives the negative sign of a column $2\le k\le9$ in every row from $k+1$ up to the row $2k+1$ for $k\le5$, $2k$ for $k=6,7$, $2k-1$ for $k=8$ and $2k-2$ for $k=9$. Among its $45$ cells are the ten positions and the seven positions $(2k-1,k)$, $2\le k\le8$, in the last row of the first layer.

The rules follow at once. For the column rule with its conclusion at a position $(n+1,k)$, $n+1\ge2k$ (`Parking.column_rule_beyond`): the conclusion holds unless $(n+1,k)$ is one of the eleven positions. The position $(2,1)$ cannot be the conclusion, since the rule starts at $n=2$; for the other ten, the step at $(n,k)$ is one of the negative cells, so that the hypothesis fails. For the diagonal rule with its hypothesis at a position $(n,k)$, $n\ge2k$ (`Parking.diagonal_rule_beyond`): the hypothesis holds only at the eleven positions, and their diagonal neighbours, $(2k+1,k+1)$ for $k\le7$ and $(2k+2,k+1)$ for $2\le k\le5$, are negative cells.

The positive signs have a different source in each range of columns.

*The column $1$.* The claim is $2A(n,0)<A(n,1)$ for $n\ge3$, where $A(n,l)$ is the number of parking functions of length $n$ with maximum displacement at most $l$ (`Parking.A`). It follows by induction on $n$ from the computed comparison $2A(3,0)=12<13=A(3,1)$, by comparing the last-car recursions for the bounds $1$ and $0$ term by term.

*The columns $2\le k\le81$.* The table `Parking.Tab.small_table` comes with a list of rows $n_0(k)\ge2k$, one for each of these columns (`Parking.Tab.n0`). The rows $2k\le n<n_0(k)$ are the *junction* of the column. The table `Parking.Tab.junction_table` gives the positive sign in every row of the junction that is not one of the eleven positions, and it states $n_0(k)\ge2k+3$, so that the last row $n_0(k)-1$ of the junction is not among them. From the row $n_0(k)$ on the sign is obtained by an analytic argument, the propagation of a positive step. Write $z^{(y)}_n=A(n,y-1)/n!$ (`Parking.zr`) and $\delta_n=d_{n,k}/n!$, and let $\xi_t$ be the tree curvature of the column (`Parking.xi`), an explicit combination of the numbers $z^{(k+1)}_{t-1}$, $z^{(k)}_{t-1}$ and $z^{(k-1)}_{t-1}$ that its record gives. The one-tree bound `Parking.one_tree_bound` states $\sum_{t=0}^{n}\xi_t\,z^{(k)}_{n-t}\le\delta_n$. Let $x>0$ be a number, called a tilt, for which $z^{(k)}_N x^N$ does not increase in $N$. If all the sums $\sum_{t=s}^{n}\xi_tx^t$, $k\le s\le n$, are nonnegative, a summation by parts, in which the weight $z^{(k)}_0=1$ of the last term is lowered to the weight $z^{(k)}_1x=x$ of the term before it, gives the *step bound* $(1-x)\,\xi_n\le\delta_n$ (`Parking.step_bound`); without the lowering of the last weight the same summation by parts gives only $\delta_n\ge0$. For $n\ge k+1$ one has $(n+1)\,\xi_{n+1}=(k+1)\,\delta_n+2\,b(n,k-1)/n!$, so a positive step in the row $n$ makes $\xi_{n+1}$ positive. If the sums are nonnegative at the row $n+1$ and $x<1$, the step bound then makes the step in the row $n+1$ positive, and the positive number $\xi_{n+2}$ that results keeps the sums nonnegative at the row $n+2$. So a positive step passes to every later row (`Parking.propagate_pos`). The argument is applied at the critical tilt $x_k=(k/(k+1))^{k-1}<1$, for which the monotonicity is `Parking.zrT_anti_crit`; the sums at the row $n_0(k)$ are nonnegative by the table `Parking.Tab.small_table`, and the positive step in the row $n_0(k)-1$ is the last cell of the junction.

*The columns $k\ge82$.* The positivity from the row $2k$ on is `Parking.step_pos_large`, an analytic argument that starts from the single change of sign in the first layer, `Parking.one_sign_change`. The column is positive from its first positive row $q$ to the row $2k-1$, and an induction on the row carries the positive sign to every row $n\ge2k$. The induction step uses the bound $q\le k+27k/100$ a second time; the record of `Parking.step_pos_large` gives the account.

### The diagonal rule in the first layer of the columns up to 264

This region is `Parking.diagonal_rule_small`, and it rests on tables and, for $k\ge9$, on persistence. For $9\le k\le264$ the table `Parking.Tab.two_signs` lists a number $\zeta(k)$ with $\zeta(k)+2\le k$ and shows that the column $k$ is positive in the row $k+\zeta(k)+1$, which lies in its first layer. (The records of the tables write $z(k)$ for this number; the letter is changed here because $z$ denotes the numbers $z^{(y)}_n$.) By persistence the column is positive in every later row of the first layer, so a step that is not positive lies in a row $n\le k+\zeta(k)$. What is then needed is that the column $k+1$ is negative in the rows $k+2,\dots,k+\zeta(k)+1$, and this is the table `Parking.Tab.next_column_neg`. The table of two signs gives only one of these signs: its second sign is the negative step of the column $k+1$ in the row $k+\zeta(k)$. Persistence, applied to the column $k+1$, shows that the steps of the earlier rows are not positive, but it cannot exclude a step equal to zero, and it gives nothing for the row $k+\zeta(k)+1$. For $2\le k\le8$ no sign of the column $k$ is used: the column $k+1$ is negative in every row from $k+2$ to $2k$, and these $28$ positions are cells of the table `Parking.Tab.negative_cells`.

### The diagonal rule in the first layer of the columns from 265 on

This is the region in which the diagonal rule is hardest, and it is covered by two analytic arguments that have to meet. Call $r=n-k$ the *deficit* of a position $(n,k)$, and the set of positions with $k^2=r^3$ *the curve*; a position with $r^3\le k^2$ lies *behind the curve*. The first argument shows that a step which is not positive lies behind the curve, and the second proves the diagonal rule there.

**The curve increase** (`Parking.curve_crossing`). For $k\ge265$ there is a row $j>k$ with $(j-k-1)^3\le k^2$ and $d_{j,k}>0$: the column is positive at most one row beyond the curve. The row $j$ lies in the first layer of the column, because $j-k-1\le k^{2/3}\le k-2$ for $k\ge265$. With persistence this places every step of the first layer that is not positive behind the curve: such a step lies in a row $n<j$, and then $(n-k)^3\le(j-k-1)^3\le k^2$.

The proof of the curve increase starts from the identity `Parking.dq_frozen`, which writes the normalised step $d_{n,k}/n!$ as a curvature term minus a boundary term, and does so in two ways: through a polynomial $K_{n,k-1}(y)$ and through a polynomial $K_{n,k}(y)$ in a real variable $y$ (`Parking.Kpoly`). The identity is stated at a position $(n,j)$ of the first layer of a column $j\ge2$, where it gives the steps of the columns $j$ and $j+1$, both through $K_{n,j}$; so the way through $K_{n,k}$ is available in the rows $k+1\le n\le2k-1$ of the first layer of the column $k$, and the way through $K_{n,k-1}$ in the rows of the first layer of the column $k-1$. The two ways split the same number differently, so that their curvature terms differ and their boundary terms differ. The curve increase uses the first way, which is available at the positions $(n,k)$ with $n=k+R-1$ and $1\le R\le k-2$. These rows, $k\le n\le2k-3$, are the first layer of the column $k-1$, and all of them but the row $n=k$ lie in the first layer of the column $k$ as well. With $N=k+R$, the curvature term is the second difference $K_{n,k-1}(k+1)-2K_{n,k-1}(k)+K_{n,k-1}(k-1)$, and the boundary term is $a_k/k$ times the chain sum $\Lambda_R(k+1;N)$. Here $a_i=i^{i-1}/i!$, and $\Lambda_m(y;N)$ is the total weight of the compositions of $m$ in which a part of size $i$ weighs $a_i$ and a cut at the position $s$ weighs $y/(N-s)$ (`Parking.Lam`, `Parking.thr`); the number $y$ is called the threshold. The polynomial has nonnegative coefficients. For a monomial $y^q$, $k^2$ times the second difference $(k+1)^q-2k^q+(k-1)^q$ is at least $q(q-1)\,k^q$, and $q(q-1)$ is twice the number of pairs among the $q$ factors of the monomial. Summed over the terms of the polynomial, this bounds $k^2$ times its second difference from below by twice a sum over the terms with two of their factors marked. That sum is the pair sum $\mathcal P(k,R)$ (`Parking.pairSum`, whose record writes it out). So $k^2$ times the normalised step is at least $2\,\mathcal P(k,R)-k\,a_k\,\Lambda_R(k+1;N)$, which gives a criterion in which the step no longer occurs (`Parking.dq_pos_of_pair`): the step at $(k+R-1,k)$ is positive as soon as $k\,a_k\,\Lambda_R(k+1;N)<2\,\mathcal P(k,R)$. The two sides are estimated separately. The pair sum is bounded from below by Jensen's inequality, applied to the cut weights written as exponentials (`Parking.pairSum_ge`), and the boundary term is bounded from above (`Parking.impulse_le`). Both bounds are expressed by the numbers $e^{-i}i^i/i!$, $1\le i\le R$, and two convolutions of them. By Stirling's formula these numbers are of the order $1/\sqrt i$, so that the comparison of the two bounds becomes an inequality between functions of $\sqrt R$; it uses Stirling's bounds and a lower bound, `Parking.sbirth_ge`, for the sum $\sum_{i=1}^{R}R(R-1)\cdots(R-i+1)/R^i$, the mean of the birthday law (`Parking.sbirth`). The result, written with the deficit $r=R-1$ of the position, is `Parking.curve_step_pos`: for $r\ge41$ and $(r-1)^3\le k^2\le r^3$, the step $d_{k+r,k}$ is positive. The curve increase applies it to the least $r$ with $k^2<r^3$, which is at least $42$ when $k\ge265$.

**The diagonal step behind the curve** (`Parking.diagonal_step_curve`). For $k\ge265$, $r\ge1$ and $r^3\le k^2$: if $d_{k+r,k}\le0$, then $d_{k+r+1,k+1}<0$. The identity `Parking.dq_frozen` is used here in its other way, through the polynomial $K_{n,k}$, whose second difference is written out (`Parking.dq_curv_sub_boundary`): with $N=k+r+1$,
$$\frac{d_{k+r,k}}{(k+r)!}=C-B,\qquad C=\sum_{m=1}^{r}\frac{\kappa_k(N-m)}{N-m}\,\mathsf C_m(k;N),\qquad B=\frac{a_k}{k}\,\Lambda_{r+1}(k-1;N),$$
where $\kappa_k(t)=a_t-\sum_{k<i<t}a_ia_{t-i}$ (`Parking.kap`) and the curvature sum $\mathsf C_m(y;N)=(y+1)\Lambda_m(y+1;N)-2y\,\Lambda_m(y;N)+(y-1)\Lambda_m(y-1;N)$ is the second difference in the threshold $y$ of $y\,\Lambda_m(y;N)$ (`Parking.curvSum`). Let $C'$ and $B'$ be the same two terms at the diagonal neighbour $(k+r+1,k+1)$; $B$ and $B'$ are positive. The claim is that $C\le B$ implies $C'<B'$, and the proof obtains it from the cross product $C'\,B<C\,B'$: with $C\le B$ this gives $C'\,B<B\,B'$, hence $C'<B'$.

For $r=1$ the term $C'$ is zero and nothing more is needed. For $r\ge2$ the cross product is proved term by term in $m$ (`Parking.term_step`): each term of $C'$, multiplied by $B$, is compared with the corresponding term of $C$, multiplied by $B'$. Three comparisons follow the three ingredients of a term from the position to its diagonal neighbour, and a fourth compares what they leave. The coefficient $\kappa_k(N-m)/(N-m)$ grows by at most an explicit factor (`Parking.kap_diag_le`). The curvature sum $\mathsf C_m$ grows by at most a second explicit factor (`Parking.curvSum_step`, by Hölder's inequality for the composition sums, a reflection of the composition and Jensen's inequality). The chain sum of the boundary term grows by at least a third explicit factor (`Parking.Lam_gain`, by an FKG inequality for the sets of cut points of a composition). The fourth comparison says that the product of the first two factors is smaller than the factor that the boundary term gains, which is the third factor times the ratio $(1+1/k)^{k-2}$ of $a_{k+1}/(k+1)$ to $a_k/k$ (`Parking.scalar_step`); after logarithms it is an inequality between closed forms in $k$, $r$ and $m$, the ledger (`Parking.ledger`). The hypothesis $r^3\le k^2$ is the range in which the ledger is proved. Its constants are fitted to the column $265$ and leave little room, which is why the diagonal step is available behind the curve only.

### One bound for the first positive row of a column $k\ge265$, and its two uses

For a column $k\ge265$ the proof has one theorem that bounds the first positive row, the curve increase `Parking.curve_crossing`: there is a row $j>k$ with $(j-k-1)^3\le k^2$ and $d_{j,k}>0$. It is used in two places, and the two uses ask different things of it.

The first use is in the diagonal rule. The diagonal step `Parking.diagonal_step_curve` is proved at the positions behind the curve $k^2=(n-k)^3$ and nowhere else, so the rule can be applied to a step that is not positive only if that step is known to lie behind the curve. The curve increase, with the persistence `Parking.step_persists_iter`, shows exactly this: the column is positive from the row $j$ to the end of its first layer, so a step of the first layer that is not positive lies in a row $n<j$, and then $(n-k)^3\le(j-k-1)^3\le k^2$. This use needs the bound in the form in which it is stated. A bound that placed such a step only in a larger region, with a larger multiple of $k^2$ on the right, would leave positions at which the diagonal step is not proved.

The second use is in the persistence of the sign and in the signs beyond the first layer. For the columns $k\ge82$ both rest on a row $k+r$ with $1\le r\le27k/100$ in which the column is positive, `Parking.positive_row_early`. From it `Parking.one_sign_change` obtains, with `Parking.persist`, that the step of the column is positive from its first positive row to the row $2k-1$, and `Parking.step_persists` and `Parking.step_pos_large` follow. For $82\le k\le264$ the row $k+r$ is read from the table of two signs. For $k\ge265$ it is the row $j$ of the curve increase: $(j-k-1)^3\le k^2$ and $k\ge265$ give $100\,(j-k)\le27\,k$. This use needs far less than the bound says, because a distance $j-k$ of the order $k^{2/3}$ is well below $27k/100$ in these columns; at $k=265$ the curve increase gives $j-k\le42$, where $27k/100$ allows $71$.

So in the columns $k\ge265$ the curve increase stands behind the column rule in the first layer, behind the signs beyond the first layer, and twice behind the diagonal rule in the first layer: once directly, and once through the persistence that the diagonal rule applies. This is not a circle. The proof of the curve increase estimates the step at a single position and uses no statement on persistence; persistence is derived from the curve increase; and the diagonal rule uses both.

### What is computed

The tables are evaluations, by the kernel of the proof assistant, of closed Boolean expressions in the exact arithmetic of natural numbers, and for each table a soundness statement says what the value "true" means for the numbers $b(n,k)$. The proof uses five tables. Two of them are `Parking.Tab.small_table`, for the columns $2$ to $81$, which gives the persistence in the first layer and the nonnegative sums at the row $n_0(k)$, and `Parking.Tab.two_signs`, for the columns $9$ to $264$, of which the proof uses the positive sign in the row $k+\zeta(k)+1$. Persistence in the columns $82$ to $264$ uses in addition one check of the same kind on the list $\zeta$ alone, `Parking.Tab.ztab_27`: $100(\zeta(k)+1)\le27k$ for $82\le k\le264$, a bound on that row in which no number of parking functions occurs. The other three are tables of strict signs: `Parking.Tab.junction_table` (933 signs, in the columns $2$ to $81$), `Parking.Tab.negative_cells` (45 signs, in the columns $2$ to $9$) and `Parking.Tab.next_column_neg` (5,934 signs, in the columns $10$ to $265$). Beside the tables there is the one comparison $2A(3,0)<A(3,1)$. The junction table and the table of negative cells compute their numbers by the last-car recursion. The table of the next column computes them by a linear recursion that holds in the first layer of a column, as the table of two signs does. The largest row that any of the five tables reads is the row $264+\zeta(264)+1=300$.

### The Lean form

The statement is `theorem staircase (n : ℕ) (hn : 2 ≤ n) : ∃ p, IsPeak b n p ∧ (IsPeak b (n + 1) p ∨ IsStrictPeak b (n + 1) (p + 1))`, in which the function `Parking.b` of two natural numbers is taken as the array. In the statement above the conditions of `IsPeak b (n + 1) p` and `IsStrictPeak b (n + 1) (p + 1)` are written with what $p\le n-1$ gives: the bounds $p\le n$ and $p+1\le n$ on the positions hold, and the condition "if $p+1\le n$" on the entry after the peak of the row $n+1$ is met, so that $b(n+1,p+1)\le b(n+1,p)$ is stated without it. The difference $n-1$ is the ordinary one because $n\ge2$.

## Proof

Apply `Parking.peak_induction` to the array $f=b$. That statement says: if an array $f$ of natural numbers satisfies, for all $n\ge2$ and $1\le k\le n-1$, the implications "$f(n,k-1)<f(n,k)$ implies $f(n+1,k-1)<f(n+1,k)$" and "$f(n,k)\le f(n,k-1)$ implies $f(n+1,k+1)<f(n+1,k)$", then for every $n\ge2$ there is $p$ such that the row $n$ of $f$ has its peak at $p$ and the row $n+1$ has its peak at $p$ or a strict peak at $p+1$. For $f=b$ the first implication is `Parking.column_rule` and the second is `Parking.diagonal_rule`, each for exactly the range $n\ge2$, $1\le k\le n-1$. The conclusion for $f=b$ is the statement.
