## Description

The proved main theorem: for every integer $n\ge1$ the sequence $(b_{n,0},b_{n,1},\dots,b_{n,n-1})$ is unimodal, where $b_{n,\ell}$ is the number of parking functions of length $n$ whose maximum displacement is exactly $\ell$.

## Statement

**Definitions.** Let $n\ge1$ be an integer. A *preference sequence* of length $n$ is $\alpha=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\{1,\dots,n\}^n$. Spots $1,\dots,n$ lie in a row and are all empty at the start. Cars $1,2,\dots,n$ arrive in this order, and car $i$ looks from spot $a_i$ onward, in the direction of increasing spot numbers, and parks in the first empty spot it meets. If the spots $a_i,a_i+1,\dots,n$ are all occupied, car $i$ cannot park. If every car parks, $\alpha$ is called a *parking function*, and the set of all parking functions of length $n$ is written $\mathrm{PF}_n$.

For $\alpha\in\mathrm{PF}_n$, let $\mathrm{pos}_i(\alpha)$ be the spot in which car $i$ parks. The *displacement* of car $i$ is $d_i(\alpha)=\mathrm{pos}_i(\alpha)-a_i\ge0$, and the *maximum displacement* of $\alpha$ is $M(\alpha)=\max_{1\le i\le n}d_i(\alpha)\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}$. For $0\le\ell\le n-1$ put
$$b_{n,\ell}=\#\{\alpha\in\mathrm{PF}_n:\ M(\alpha)=\ell\}.$$

A finite sequence $(c_0,\dots,c_m)$ is *unimodal* if there is some $j^*\in\{0,\dots,m\}$ such that $c_{j-1}\le c_j$ holds for $1\le j\le j^*$ and $c_j\ge c_{j+1}$ holds for $j^*\le j\le m-1$. Equality is allowed.

**Theorem.** For every integer $n\ge1$ the sequence $(b_{n,0},b_{n,1},\dots,b_{n,n-1})$ is unimodal. That is, for every $n\ge1$ there is some $\ell^*\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ such that $b_{n,\ell-1}\le b_{n,\ell}$ for $1\le\ell\le\ell^*$ and $b_{n,\ell}\ge b_{n,\ell+1}$ for $\ell^*\le\ell\le n-2$.

## Explanation

This is the proved form of the root proposition. In the packaged library the proposition as posed is the declaration `Parking.root_unimodal`, with its proof left open, and the theorem proved in the module `Parking.Unimodal` is carried under the name `Parking.root_unimodal_proof`, with the identical Lean statement. In the source tree of the library, before packaging, that theorem is the declaration named `root_unimodal`, and no declaration named `root_unimodal_proof` exists there. The record of `Parking.root_unimodal` reads the Lean statement against the proposition, binder by binder, and that reading is not repeated here. The proof given below is short, because the theorem is assembled from a few results that carry all the content. What follows is an account of the whole argument, from the definitions to the theorem: how the theorem is reduced to statements about signs, which results it rests on and in which order, and on which steps the proof turns. The notation is that of the library: $b(n,\ell)=b_{n,\ell}$, and $A(n,k)=\sum_{\ell\le k}b(n,\ell)$ is the number of parking functions of length $n$ with maximum displacement at most $k$ (`Parking.b`, `Parking.A`).

### 1. The reduction to signs of steps

Arrange the numbers $b(n,k)$ in a table whose rows are indexed by the length $n$ and whose columns are indexed by the displacement $k$, $0\le k\le n-1$. For $1\le k\le n-1$ write
$$d_{n,k}=b(n,k)-b(n,k-1)$$
for the *step of the column $k$ in the row $n$*. The theorem is about a row. A row is unimodal as soon as no negative step is followed, further to the right, by a positive step (`Parking.unimodal_of_no_pair`, an elementary fact about finite sequences). So the theorem is the statement that $d_{n,i}<0<d_{n,j}$ is impossible for $i<j$ (`Parking.no_descent_ascent`), and everything else in the library is about the signs of the numbers $d_{n,k}$.

### 2. Why the proof studies columns

The theorem compares the entries of one row, that is, different displacements $k$ for one length $n$. The only relation between these numbers that comes from the parking process runs the other way: it relates different lengths for one displacement bound. It is the last-car recursion
$$A(n+1,k)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\min(j+1,k+1)\binom nj A(j,k)\,A(n-j,k)$$
(`Parking.A_succ`), obtained by removing the last car, which parks in the one spot that the other cars leave free and splits them into the cars on its left and the cars on its right. This recursion, the value $A(0,k)=1$ and the monotonicity of $A(n,k)$ in $k$ are all that the proof takes from the definition of a parking function. For $k\ge2$ the step is a second difference in the displacement bound,
$$d_{n,k}=A(n,k)-2A(n,k-1)+A(n,k-2)$$
(`Parking.d_eq_A`), of three sequences in $n$ each of which satisfies its own recursion. The proof therefore fixes a column $k$, studies the sign of $d_{n,k}$ as $n$ grows, and only at the end passes from the columns to the rows.

The numbers $b(n,k)$ are defined for all $n$ and $k$, with $b(n,k)=0$ for $k\ge n\ge1$, and it is convenient to let a column $k\ge2$ start at the row $n=k$, where the step $d_{k,k}=-b(k,k-1)=-k^{k-2}$ is negative (`Parking.dq_base`). The picture behind the proof is that the column is nonpositive for a while, turns positive at some row, and does not turn back. The first positive row is called the *crossing row* of the column. The proof establishes as much of this picture as the theorem needs: all of it for the columns $k\ge82$ (sections 6 and 7), and for the smaller columns the weaker statements of section 3. For the columns $k\ge9$ it shows that the crossing row is at most $2k-1$. A row $n$ thus meets the columns $k\le n/2$ after their crossing and the columns $k>n/2$ in the rows where the crossing takes place, and the row is unimodal when, among the latter, the columns that have crossed by the row $n$ come before those that have not.

### 3. Three statements about columns, and the passage to the rows

The rows $k+1\le n\le 2k-1$ are called the *chain range* of the column $k$. Three statements are proved.

- **The left half** (`Parking.left_half`). For $n\ge17$ and $k\ge1$ with $2k\le n$: $d_{n,k}\ge0$. From the row $2k$ on, the first row after its chain range, a column is nonnegative (in the rows $n\ge17$).
- **Persistence in the chain range** (`Parking.chain_column`). For $k\ge2$ and $k+1\le n\le2k-2$: if $d_{n,k}>0$ then $d_{n+1,k}>0$. Iterated (`Parking.chain_column_iter`): a column that is positive in a row $m\ge k+1$ is positive in all the rows from $m$ to $2k-1$. So inside its chain range a column changes sign at most once, from nonpositive to positive.
- **The next column** (`Parking.mono`). For $k\ge9$ and $n\ge k+2$: if $d_{j,k}\le0$ for all $k+1\le j\le n$, then $d_{n,k+1}\le0$. As long as the column $k$ has not turned positive, the column $k+1$ has not turned positive either.

The theorem follows from them in a few lines (`Parking.no_descent_ascent`, `Parking.right_tail`). Let $n\ge17$ and suppose that $d_{n,i}<0$. If $2i\le n$, this contradicts the left half. Otherwise $n\le2i-1$, hence $i\ge9$, and the row $n$ lies in the chain range of the column $i$. By persistence, the column $i$ is nonpositive in all the rows $i+1,\dots,n$, since a positive step in one of them would persist up to the row $n$. By the statement on the next column, applied in each of the rows $i+2,\dots,n$, the column $i+1$ is nonpositive in these rows; and so on, column by column. Hence $d_{n,j}\le0$ for every $j\ge i$, and no ascent follows the descent. The rows $n\le16$ are computed (`Parking.Tab.small_rows`). They have to be treated apart. The inequality of the left half is false in some small rows, for instance $d_{5,2}=377-421<0$ although $2\cdot2\le5$. And the statement on the next column begins at the column $9$; for $n\ge17$ the case $n\le2i-1$ concerns only columns $i\ge9$.

### 4. Computation for the small columns, analysis for the large ones

Each of the three statements is proved by an analytic argument for the large columns and by an exact computation for the small ones. The dividing column is $82$ for the left half and for persistence, and $265$ for the next column. The analytic arguments rest on explicit estimates, which the library carries out from these columns on, and the computations reach exactly as far as is needed to meet them. That finitely many computed numbers suffice is not obvious for all three statements, and it has a different reason in each.

- *Persistence for $2\le k\le81$* is a finite statement, about the rows $k+1,\dots,2k-1$ of each column, and it is checked position by position (`Parking.Tab.small_table`).
- *The left half for $2\le k\le81$* concerns infinitely many rows of each column. It is reduced to a finite check by the propagation theorem (`Parking.propagate`, section 7 below): if certain finitely many sums formed from the rows below $n_0$ are nonnegative, then $d_{n,k}\ge0$ for every $n\ge n_0$. For each column the table computes the rows below a listed row $n_0(k)\le2k+14$, checks these sums at $n_0(k)$, and checks the signs of the steps in the rows $\max(2k,17)\le n<n_0(k)$ (`Parking.Tab.small_table`, `Parking.small_columns`). The column $k=1$ needs no table beyond one computed comparison, $2A(3,0)\le A(3,1)$: the inequality $d_{n,1}\ge0$ for $n\ge3$ follows from it by induction on $n$, through a termwise comparison of the last-car recursions for the bounds $1$ and $0$ (`Parking.column_one`).
- *The next column for $9\le k\le264$* is a statement about all rows $n\ge k+2$, and two computed signs per column suffice (`Parking.Tab.two_signs`): a listed number $\zeta=\zeta(k)$ with $\zeta+2\le k$, $d_{k+\zeta+1,k}>0$ and $d_{k+\zeta,k+1}<0$. The first sign shows that the hypothesis of the statement can hold only for $n\le k+\zeta$. If then $d_{n,k+1}$ were positive, persistence in the chain range of the column $k+1$ would carry the positive sign to the row $k+\zeta$, against the second sign; the row $k+\zeta$ lies in that chain range because $\zeta+2\le k$. Here a table and a theorem work together: without persistence, every position $(n,k+1)$ with $n\le k+\zeta$ would have to be computed. (The records of the table write $z(k)$ for $\zeta(k)$; the letter is changed here because $z$ denotes the normalised counts below.)
- *The crossing row for $82\le k\le264$.* The analytic arguments for the columns $k\ge82$ need a row $k+r$ with $1\le r\le 27k/100$ in which the column is positive (`Parking.crossing_row`). For $k\ge265$ a theorem provides it (section 6). For $82\le k\le264$ it is the first of the two signs just mentioned, with $r=\zeta(k)+1$ (`Parking.Tab.two_signs`, `Parking.Tab.ztab_27`).

All these computations are evaluations of closed Boolean expressions by the kernel of the proof assistant, in exact arithmetic of natural numbers; the numbers $A(n,k)$ are produced by the last-car recursion or, in the chain range, by a linear recursion that follows from it, and for each table a theorem states what the value "true" means for the numbers $b(n,k)$. One further place uses explicit evaluation, of rational numbers and without a table: the monotonicity at the critical tilt for the columns $2\le k\le11$ (section 5).

### 5. What the analytic arguments are built on

**Thresholds and the free range.** Put $z^{(y)}_n=A(n,y-1)/n!$ (`Parking.zr`), the normalised count of the parking functions all of whose displacements are smaller than the *threshold* $y\ge1$. The last-car recursion becomes $(n+1)z_{n+1}=\sum_{j\le n}\min(j+1,y)\,z_jz_{n-j}$. Without a bound, the solution is $c_n=(n+1)^{n-1}/n!$, and $z^{(y)}_n=c_n$ in the *free range* $n\le y$, where the bound is no restriction (`Parking.zr_eq_cc`). The numbers $c_n$, $a_j=j^{j-1}/j!$ and $\varepsilon_m=(m-1)^{m-1}/m!$ are the explicit material of the proof. Their convolution identities are consequences of Abel's binomial identity (`Parking.abel_identity`), among them $\sum_{m=1}^{n}\varepsilon_mc_{n-m}=c_n$ for $n\ge1$ (`Parking.eps_conv`). The proof also uses the log-convexity of the $a_j$, which comes from $a_{j+1}/a_j=(1+1/j)^{j-1}$ (`Parking.cay_logconvex`), Stirling bounds, and the sum $\sum_{m\ge1}e^{-m}\varepsilon_m=1-e^{-1}$ (`Parking.hasSum_eps`). For $k\ge2$ the normalised step is $\delta_n=d_{n,k}/n!=z^{(k+1)}_n-2z^{(k)}_n+z^{(k-1)}_n$ (`Parking.dq`), so the column $k$ involves the three thresholds $k+1,k,k-1$. Write also $\beta^{(k)}_n=b(n,k)/n!$ (`Parking.bq`).

**The chain range: a linear equation.** The last-car recursion is quadratic. For $y\le n\le2y+1$ it can be replaced by a relation that is linear in the values of $z^{(y)}$,
$$z^{(y)}_n-\sum_{m=1}^{n}\varepsilon_m\,z^{(y)}_{n-m}=\frac yn\,z^{(y)}_{n-1}-c_{n-1}$$
(the single-chain formula, `Parking.IsWSol.ez`; an equivalent forward form, which expresses $c_n-z^{(y)}_n$ through the earlier values, is `Parking.IsWSol.fwd`). Read for the three thresholds $k+1,k,k-1$ it gives the *column equation* (`Parking.dq_col`): for $k\ge2$ and $k+1\le n\le2k-1$,
$$\delta_n=\frac{k+1}{n}\,\delta_{n-1}+\frac2n\,\beta^{(k-1)}_{n-1}+\sum_{m=1}^{n-k}\varepsilon_m\,\delta_{n-m},$$
with the start $\delta_k=-a_k/k<0$, which comes from the forward form. The step in the row $n$ is fed by a nonnegative source and by the earlier steps of the same column, with positive weights. In these terms the picture of section 2 reads: the negative start is fed back into the following rows, the source works against it, and the crossing row is the first row in which the source prevails. The equation also shows what has to be proved: that after the crossing the rows before it, in which the step is nonpositive and which remain in the sum, never pull the column back.

**All rows: the series of a threshold.** Beyond the chain range there is no exact linear equation. The proof passes to the generating series $Z_y=\sum_nz^{(y)}_nX^n$ of a threshold (`Parking.Zs`), for which the last-car recursion is the differential equation $Z_y'=G_yZ_y$, where $G_y$ is the series with the coefficients $\min(j+1,y)\,z^{(y)}_j$ (`Parking.Gp`, `Parking.deriv_Zs`), and to its *prime kernel* $1-1/Z_y$ (`Parking.Ks`), whose coefficients $\kappa^{(y)}_m$ are nonnegative for $y\ge2$ (`Parking.kqT_nonneg`) and equal $\varepsilon_m$ for $1\le m\le y$ (`Parking.kq_eq_eps`). One principle, that a series $F$ with $F(0)\ge0$ and $F'=GF+S$, where $G$ and $S$ have nonnegative coefficients, has nonnegative coefficients, gives two inequalities valid on every row for a column $k\ge2$ (module `Parking.Series.AllRows`). The *column inequality* `Parking.col_eq_all`, for $n\ge k+1$, is the column equation with the prime kernel $\kappa^{(k)}$ of the middle threshold $k$ in place of $\varepsilon$ and "$\ge$" in place of "$=$". The *one-tree bound* is $\sum_{t\le n}\xi_tz^{(k)}_{n-t}\le\delta_n$ (`Parking.one_tree_bound`), in which $\xi_t$ is an explicit combination of the three thresholds in the row $t-1$ (`Parking.xi`). A third inequality on all rows, the *diagonal lower bound* $\gamma_k\beta^{(k-1)}_{n-1}\le\beta^{(k)}_n$ for $n\ge1$, with $\gamma_k=(1+1/k)^{k-2}$ (`Parking.bq_diag_lower`), needs in addition a monotonicity along the diagonal, $z^{(y)}_n/c_n\le z^{(y+1)}_{n+1}/c_{n+1}$ (`Parking.zr_diag_mono`), which is proved from the last-car recursion by induction on $n$.

**Tilts.** Multiplying the $n$-th term of each sequence by $x^n$ keeps the shape of all these relations and leaves the *tilt* $x$ free to be chosen. Two choices are used throughout, and the far rows of the persistence argument use the scale $x=e^{-1}$ in addition. The *critical tilt* $x_k=(k/(k+1))^{k-1}$ (`Parking.xcrit`) is the one at which the tilted numbers $c_nx_k^n$ stop decreasing at the index $k$: they do not increase up to $n=k$ and do not decrease from $n=k-1$ on (`Parking.ccT_succ_le`, `Parking.ccT_le_succ`). The central fact is that at this tilt the tilted masses of the threshold $k$ never increase: $z^{(k)}_{N+1}x_k^{N+1}\le z^{(k)}_Nx_k^N$ for all $N$ and all $k\ge2$ (`Parking.zrT_anti_crit`). It reduces to the inequality $x_k\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_jx_k^j\le1$, which is proved by estimates for $k\ge12$ (`Parking.cayleySum_le_one`) and by evaluating ten explicit rational comparisons for $2\le k\le11$. It is used throughout the left half. The *diagonal tilt* $1/\gamma_k$ is used for the crossing row and for persistence.

### 6. The crossing row, and persistence for $k\ge82$

**The crossing row of a column $k\ge265$** (`Parking.cross_criterion`, `Parking.crossing_strip`). Let $k\ge2$ and $1\le R\le k-1$, suppose the column $k$ is still nonpositive in all the rows $k<j\le N=k+R$, and tilt by $x=1/\gamma_k$. Then the diagonal lower bound makes the tilted numbers $b(j,k-1)x^j/j!$, $k\le j\le N$, largest at $j=N$, and the column equations of the level $k-1$ and of the step, summed over the rows $k,\dots,N$, give an inequality between explicit sums in $k$ and $R$. `Parking.cross_criterion` states the contrapositive: if one of these sums is nonnegative and the opposite strict inequality holds, the column is positive in some row $k<j\le N$. For $R$ of the order $k^{2/3}$ the hypotheses of the criterion are met. With closed-form bounds for the sums (`Parking.cross_of_bounds`) the library obtains: every column $k\ge265$ is positive in some row $j>k$ with $25(j-k-1)^3\le121k^2$.

**Persistence** (`Parking.persist`, `Parking.single_crossing`). Let $k\ge82$, let $q$ be the first positive row, and suppose $q\le k+k/3$. The proof that the column stays positive up to the row $2k-1$ is an induction on the row, and it needs two different arguments. For the rows up to $k+0.47k$ the column equation at the target row is compared with the column equation at the crossing row: what the rows before the crossing contribute at the target row is controlled by what they contributed at $q$, where the outcome was positive (`Parking.two_point`, `Parking.caseA`). This needs the log-convexity of the kernel $\varepsilon$ (`Parking.eps_logconvex`) and a monotonicity property of the kernel at the diagonal tilt, namely that $(k+\rho)\,x^\rho\varepsilon_\rho$ does not increase in $\rho$, which the library proves for $1\le\rho\le0.47k$ and $k\ge42$ (`Parking.Fk_anti`). For the rows beyond, the proof uses a window of positive rows before the target row and the diagonal lower bound to bound $\delta_n$ from below by a combination of the numbers $\beta^{(k-1)}$ with the coefficients $\gamma_k\varepsilon_m-\varepsilon_{m+1}$ (`Parking.persistence_inequality`). These coefficients are nonnegative for $m+1\le3k/5$ (`Parking.eps_succ_le_gam`). Beyond the row $k+3k/5$ the combination contains later coefficients, which may be negative, and these terms are bounded through a comparison with a renewal sequence. Let $f$ be a positive log-convex kernel, $G$ its renewal sequence, and $v$ a sequence whose *innovations* $v_s-\sum_{i=1}^{s}f_iv_{s-i}$ are nonnegative. Then for nonnegative nondecreasing weights $\theta_m$ the weighted feedback $\sum_{m\ge M}\theta_mf_mv_{\rho-m}$, divided by the last value $v_\rho$, is at most the same quantity for $v=G$ (`Parking.extreme_column_le`). This rests on the theorem that the renewal sequence of a log-convex kernel is log-convex (`Parking.renewal_logConvex`). It is applied in the scale $e^{-1}$, to $v_\sigma=e^{-\sigma}\beta^{(k-1)}_{k+\sigma}$ and to the kernel $f_1=\frac32e^{-1}$, $f_i=e^{-i}\varepsilon_i$ for $i\ge2$ (`Parking.fker`), whose renewal sequence is computed in closed form (`Parking.Gres_isRenewal`). Together (`Parking.caseFar`) this gives persistence, and with the crossing row of section 4 or of the previous paragraph, `Parking.single_crossing`: every column $k\ge82$ has a row $q$ with $k+1\le q\le k+27k/100$ before which it is nonpositive and from which it is positive up to the row $2k-1$.

### 7. The left half

**Columns $2\le k\le81$: propagation** (`Parking.propagate`). Let $k\ge2$, fix a tilt $x>0$ at which the tilted masses $z^{(k)}_Nx^N$ do not increase, and suppose that at a row $n_0\ge k$ all the sums $\sum_{t=s}^{n_0}\xi_tx^t$, $k\le s\le n_0$, are nonnegative. Then $\delta_n\ge0$ for all $n\ge n_0$. The argument maintains the nonnegativity of these sums as an invariant: by the one-tree bound and a summation by parts, in which the monotonicity of the tilted masses is exactly what is needed, the invariant at $n$ gives $\delta_n\ge0$; and $\delta_n\ge0$ gives $\xi_{n+1}\ge0$, which extends the invariant to $n+1$. At the critical tilt the monotonicity is `Parking.zrT_anti_crit`, and since $x_k$ is rational the sums are a matter of integer arithmetic, which is what the table of section 4 checks.

**Columns $k\ge82$** (`Parking.big_columns`: $d_{n,k}>0$ for all $n\ge2k$). The proof starts from the single crossing of section 6 and proceeds by induction on $n\ge2k$, at the critical tilt. Write $\eta_j=\delta_jx_k^j$ for the tilted steps and $\hat\kappa_m=\kappa^{(k)}_mx_k^m$ for the tilted prime kernel of the threshold $k$. The column inequality bounds $\eta_n$ from below by a nonnegative source and by the feedback $\sum_j\hat\kappa_{n-j}\eta_j$ of the earlier steps, of which those before the crossing row are nonpositive. The kernel weights $\hat\kappa_{n-j}$ of the rows before the crossing are smaller, relative to the weight $\Psi'(j)=\max(j,2k-1)$, than those of the rows after it (`Parking.order_neg`, `Parking.order_pos`), so the feedback is bounded below by a multiple of the single sum $\sum_j\Psi'(j)\eta_j$. This sum is bounded below by $-\mathfrak D$, where $\mathfrak D$, the *weighted debt*, consists of boundary terms (`Parking.weighted_debt`, for $k\ge7$). That bound is a summation by parts of a convexity statement along the diagonal for the three thresholds: $\Psi'(N+1)\,u^{+}_{N+1}-2\Psi'(N)\,u_N+\Psi'(N-1)\,u^{-}_{N-1}\ge0$ for $N\ge k$ and $k\ge7$, where $u^{+}_N,u_N,u^{-}_N$ denote the masses $z^{(y)}_Nx_k^N$ of the thresholds $y=k+1,k,k-1$ at the critical tilt (`Parking.DeltaPsi_nonneg`). For $k\le N\le2k-2$, the *first layer*, this is the plain convexity $u^{+}_{N+1}-2u_N+u^{-}_{N-1}\ge0$ (`Parking.FirstLayer.zrT_diag_convex`); beyond the first layer it follows by an induction on the last-car recursions, which again draws on the first layer (`Parking.DeltaId_nonneg`).

The first-layer convexity is proved by an induction on the last-car recursion as well, but convexity itself does not pass through that induction: the recursion multiplies the terms by factors such as $(y+p)/(y+q)$ with $0\le p\le q$, evaluated at the three thresholds $y=k-1,k,k+1$, and such a factor does not preserve the convexity of a triple. The library therefore proves a stronger property of a triple $(w_-,w_0,w_+)$ of values at the thresholds $k-1,k,k+1$, *property K*: $2e^{\mu/k}w_0\le e^{\mu}w_++e^{-\mu}w_-$ for all $\mu\ge0$ (`Parking.FirstLayer.TK`). It is convexity at $\mu=0$, and it is preserved by sums and by these factors. In the first layer the last-car recursion splits into a part in which one of the two pieces is again a first-layer mass, and a part in which both pieces are in the free range. The closure properties carry the first part. The second part, a sum of products of the numbers $c_jx_k^j$, is not covered by them, because it carries a factor that decreases in the threshold; it is shown to have property K by a separate estimate, for $k\ge7$ (`Parking.FirstLayer.Sdir_TK`). The result is property K of the triples $(u^{-}_{N-1},u_N,u^{+}_{N+1})$, $k\le N\le2k-2$, for $k\ge7$ (`Parking.FirstLayer.kernel_TK`).

With the weighted debt in place, its boundary terms are bounded in turn by the source and by the last step, using the diagonal lower bound at the critical tilt (`Parking.Ut_diag_lower`) and a comparison of the masses of the threshold $k$ with the tilted numbers of the level $k-1$. What remains is an estimate of the size of the kernel, $n(n-1)\,\hat\kappa_{n-q+1}\le3.762\sqrt k$ with $q$ the crossing row, and arithmetic, carried out for $k\ge82$.

### 8. The next column for $k\ge265$

`Parking.mono265` proves the strict form: for $k\ge265$ and $n\ge k+2$, if the column $k$ is nonpositive in the rows $k+1,\dots,n$, then $d_{n,k+1}<0$. At a position $n=k+r$ with $1\le r\le k-1$, both steps $d_{n,k}$ and $d_{n,k+1}$ are written as "curvature minus boundary term" of one polynomial $\mathcal K(y)$, in which the threshold has become a real variable $y$: $d_{n,k}/n!=C_--B_-$ with $C_-=\mathcal K(k+1)-2\mathcal K(k)+\mathcal K(k-1)$, and $d_{n,k+1}/n!=C_+-B_+$ with $C_+=\mathcal K(k+2)-2\mathcal K(k+1)+\mathcal K(k)$ (`Parking.dq_frozen`, `Parking.Kpoly`). The boundary terms are sums over compositions of $r+1$ and of $r$ with weights $a_j$ on the parts. The curvatures are compared by $C_+\le e^{D_r/(k-1)}C_-$ (`Parking.curv_ratio`), where $D_r$ is the ratio of the third to the second factorial moment of the number of parts under the birthday law, explicitly $D_r=3((r+2)s_r-3r)/(2(r-s_r))$ with $s_r=\sum_{j=1}^{r}r(r-1)\cdots(r-j+1)/r^j$ (`Parking.Dbirth`, `Parking.sbirth`). The boundary terms are compared by two inequalities that shorten a composition by one and raise the threshold by two (`Parking.Lam_shorten`, `Parking.Lam_raise`). The quantities that occur in these comparisons are moments of the number of parts of a composition. When every cut carries the factor $1$, the total weight of the compositions of $m$ with $q$ parts is $a_m\,q\,m(m-1)\cdots(m-q+1)/m^q$ (`Parking.Tq_eq`), so that the number of parts follows the size-biased birthday law; with a common factor $h$ on the cuts it is this law reweighted by $h^{q-1}$. If $C_-\le B_-$, the chain $C_+\le e^{D_r/(k-1)}C_-\le e^{D_r/(k-1)}B_-<B_+$ gives $d_{n,k+1}<0$, for $k\ge3$ and $2\le r\le k-1$, provided an explicit function $\mathsf G(r,k)$ is nonnegative (`Parking.step_neg_of_Gfun`). This function is positive for $k\ge265$ and $2\le r$ with $25r^3\le121k^2$ (`Parking.Gfun_pos`).

### 9. The order of the argument, and the steps on which it turns

In order of dependence: the definitions; the last-car recursion; the numbers $a_j,c_n,\varepsilon_m$ and Abel's identity; the single-chain formula and the column equation; the monotonicity along the diagonal; the series of a threshold and the three inequalities on all rows; the monotonicity at the critical tilt; the tables; the crossing row; persistence in the chain range; the left half; the next column; the theorem. The three column statements are not independent. The left half for $k\ge82$ starts from the single crossing, which is persistence; the next column for $9\le k\le264$ uses persistence for the column $k+1$; and the crossing theorem for $k\ge265$ enters in two places: in the single crossing, on which both persistence and the left half rest for these columns, and directly in the next column.

The proof turns on the following steps.

*Persistence after the crossing.* The column equation keeps feeding the rows before the crossing, where the step is nonpositive, into every later row. Positivity of the source alone does not decide the sign. The library uses two arguments, one for the rows up to $k+0.47k$ and one for the rows beyond, and the second meets terms of uncontrolled sign in its last part, which are handled by the comparison with a renewal sequence.

*The rows beyond the chain range.* Here the proof has inequalities only, and the rows before the crossing have to be outweighed through a single quantity, the weighted debt. Its bound needs convexity in the threshold along the diagonal, and that convexity had to be strengthened to property K before an induction could carry it; even then one part of the recursion lies outside the closure properties and needs its own estimate.

*Two ranges that have to meet.* The function $\mathsf G$ of section 8 is proved positive where $25r^3\le121k^2$. The hypothesis of the statement on the next column says only that the column $k$ has not yet turned positive in the row $k+r$. That this confines $r$ to the range where $\mathsf G$ is known to be positive is the crossing theorem of section 6, whose bound $25(j-k-1)^3\le121k^2$ is the same range. In the same way persistence needs the crossing row within $k+k/3$ and the left half needs it within $k+27k/100$; both are supplied by that theorem and, for $82\le k\le264$, by the table.

*Where analysis and computation meet.* The estimates of the analytic arguments are carried out for $k\ge82$ and for $k\ge265$. Below these columns the statements are obtained by computation, which is possible because each of them has been reduced to finitely many exact comparisons: by the propagation theorem for the left half, and by persistence for the next column.

No part of the argument determines the crossing row or the position of the maximum of a row; the proof uses bounds for the crossing row only. The library nevertheless bounds the position of the maximum in the rows $n\ge335$, by three statements that the proof of the theorem does not use. Call the rows $k+r$ with $r\ge1$ and $25r^3\le121k^2$ the *strip* of the column $k$. A column $k\ge265$ is positive in every row beyond its strip (`Parking.beyond_strip`). In a row $k+r$ of the strip of such a column with $r\ge2$, if $d_{k+r,k}\le0$ then $d_{k+r,k+1}<0$ (`Parking.strip_step`). And in every row $n\ge335$ there is a column $p\le n-1$ with $25(n-1-p)^3\le121(p+1)^2$ such that $b(n,0)\le\dots\le b(n,81)<b(n,82)<\dots<b(n,p)\ge b(n,p+1)>b(n,p+2)>\dots>b(n,n-1)$ (`Parking.row_peak`). The inequality for $p$ bounds the distance of the maximum from the right end of the row from above, by $(121/25)^{1/3}n^{2/3}<1.692\,n^{2/3}$; it gives no lower bound for that distance, and its constant is that of the estimates of sections 6 and 8, so it does not determine the position of the maximum.

### Earlier work

The statement proved here was posed as a conjecture by Celano, Elder, Hadaway, Harris, Martin, Priestley and Udell [celano2025, Conjecture 6.5]. Their paper calls a parking function an $\ell$-interval parking function when its maximum displacement is at most $\ell$ [celano2025, Definition 2.7]. The number $b_{n,\ell}$ of parking functions of length $n$ with maximum displacement exactly $\ell$ is therefore the number of $\ell$-interval parking functions of length $n$ that are not $(\ell-1)$-interval, and the conjecture is that for every $n\ge1$ the sequence of these numbers for $\ell=0,\dots,n-1$ is unimodal. The paper tabulates the numbers for $n\le9$ [celano2025, Table 1], and it gives the two ends of each row, $b_{n,0}=n!$ and $b_{n,n-1}=n^{n-2}$ [celano2025, Example 2.8 and Section 6]. In the sentence before the conjecture it states, for all $n\ge1$ and as consequences of its enumerative results, the inequalities $b_{n,0}\le b_{n,1}$, $b_{n,1}\le b_{n,2}$ and $b_{n,n-2}\ge b_{n,n-1}$, and it then adds that experimentally the pattern seems to continue [celano2025, Section 6]. Read for all $n$ for which their terms exist, the first and the second of these three inequalities do not hold for the smallest $n$: the rows of $n=2,3,4,5$ are $(2,1)$, $(6,7,3)$, $(24,51,34,16)$ and $(120,421,377,253,125)$, so that $b_{n,0}\le b_{n,1}$ fails for $n=2$ and $b_{n,1}\le b_{n,2}$ fails for $n=3,4,5$.

The works read for this research contain, among other things, exact descriptions of the cumulative counts and a limit theorem for the maximum displacement. Write $A_{n,\ell}=b_{n,0}+\dots+b_{n,\ell}$ for the number of parking functions of length $n$ with maximum displacement at most $\ell$. One description is a sum over outcomes. The outcome of a parking function is the permutation $\sigma$ whose $i$-th entry is the number of the car that stands in spot $i$; for a permutation $\sigma$ and a spot $i$, let $\lambda_i$ be the length of the longest run of consecutive spots that ends at spot $i$, spot $i$ included, and whose cars all have numbers at most $\sigma_i$. The parking functions with outcome $\sigma$ and maximum displacement at most $\ell$ then number $\prod_{i=1}^{n}\min(\ell+1,\lambda_i)$, and $A_{n,\ell}$ is the sum of these products over all permutations $\sigma$ [celano2025, Section 4.1, (6)–(8)]. Another description is a recursion in $n$, obtained by fixing the spot of the car that arrives last: $A_{n,\ell}=\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{x}\min(x+1,\ell+1)\,A_{x,\ell}\,A_{n-1-x,\ell}$ with $A_{0,\ell}=1$ [aguilarfraga2024, Theorem 3.7]. The library proves this recursion for itself, from the definition of the parking process (`Parking.A_succ`, which states it with $n+1$ in place of $n$; section 2 of this record), and does not rely on the article for it. The limit theorem is a theorem of Petersson on hashing with linear probing, stated in the summary of his doctoral thesis. It concerns a table with $m$ cells and $m-1$ items, conditioned on its last cell remaining empty, and says that the maximum displacement $d_{\max}$ of the items satisfies $\Pr\bigl((m-d_{\max})/m^{2/3}\le z\bigr)\to1-\psi(z^{3/2})$ as $m\to\infty$, for $0<z<\infty$, where $\psi$ is a function that the theorem defines by a power series [petersson2009, Theorem 2.1]. That this series is $\psi(s)=\mathbb E\,e^{-sA}$, where $A=\int_0^1\max_{s\le t}B_s\,dt$ for a standard Brownian motion $B$, is [jansonpetersson2009, Corollary 2.4], and the thesis summary states it as its Theorem 2.9 and Corollary 2.11. For $m=n+1$ such a table is a uniform random parking function of length $n$; this identification is the research's reading of the model and is not a quotation from the thesis summary. Under it, the theorem says that all but an arbitrarily small fraction of the sum of the row of $n$ lies at a distance of order $n^{2/3}$ from the end $\ell=n-1$ of the row. The theorem is a statement of convergence in distribution, and it compares no two neighbouring terms of a row. Three further works that were read prove, each for an object other than the rows $b_{n,\ell}$, a statement on the shape of a sequence of counts, on the maximum displacement, or on the number of the members of a family of parking functions. The generating polynomial of the sum of the preferences, taken over the parking functions of length $n$, is log-concave for every $n\ge1$ [pappe2024, Theorem 3.1], and the note says that this statistic is essentially equivalent to the displacement [pappe2024, p. 1]; this concerns a sum over the cars of a parking function and not the maximum of their displacements. For non-decreasing parking functions the maximum displacement is the height of the corresponding Łukasiewicz path [seligzhu2024, Theorem 2.1], whereas for general parking functions the maximum displacement and that height differ in general [seligzhu2024, Remark 2.3 and the example given in it]; indeed sorting the entries of a parking function can change its maximum displacement, as the lists $(1,2,1)$ and $(1,1,2)$ show, whose maximum displacements are $2$ and $1$. And one preprint counts, for each $\ell$, the $\ell$-interval Fubini rankings, the Fubini rankings in which at most $\ell+1$ competitors tie at a rank [agerhart2026, Section 4 and Corollary 4.1], and recalls that all Fubini rankings are parking functions [agerhart2026, p. 3]; it does not call these rankings $\ell$-interval parking functions, and it does not speak of displacement.

Several results that the proof uses are classical. This paragraph and the next treat those for which a source is cited, and say for each what the source contains and how the library stands to the result: a declaration of the library may prove it; it may be proved only as a step inside the proof of another declaration; or the library may not prove it and take what it needs from Mathlib, the library of formalised mathematics on which it is built. Classical facts that the records name without a source, for example the weighted inequality of Chebyshev (`Parking.lr_sum_le_finset`), are not treated here. The works cited in this paragraph were read for this research. The theorem that a renewal sequence with a log-convex kernel is log-convex is Theorem 1 of de Bruijn and Erdős [debruijn1953, pp. 161–162]. In the notation of the article, where $c$ is the kernel and $f(n+1)$ is the $n$-th term of its renewal sequence, it reads: with $c_k\ge0$, $\sum_k c_k=1$, $f(1)=1$ and $f(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_kf(n-k)$, if $c_{k-1}c_{k+1}>c_k^2$ for all $k>1$ then $f(n-1)f(n+1)>f(n)^2$ for all $n>1$; the Remarks after it give the form in which both inequalities are non-strict and the consequence that $f(n+1)/f(n)$ increases, and the article says that other theorems of the same type were proved by Kaluza. The library proves the form with non-strict inequalities, for a kernel all of whose terms are positive, and without the condition $\sum_kc_k=1$, which the article assumes and which its proof of Theorem 1 does not use (`Parking.renewal_logConvex`). The two-sided bound for the factorial, $n!=\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n e^{r_n}$ with $1/(12n+1)<r_n<1/(12n)$ for $n\ge1$, is in Robbins [robbins1955, p. 26, (1) and (2)]. The library proves both bounds for $n!$, the lower one as `Parking.robbins_lt_factorial` and the upper one as `Parking.factorial_lt_robbins`; the proof of the theorem uses the upper one, through `Parking.poissonMass_ge`, a lower bound for the Poisson mass $e^{-n}n^n/n!$, and through `Parking.sbirth_le`. In place of the lower one it uses the weaker bound $\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n\le n!$, which comes from Mathlib (`Stirling.le_factorial_stirling`) and enters through `Parking.poissonMass_le`, the matching upper bound for the Poisson mass. The correlation inequality for a positive log-supermodular measure on a finite distributive lattice, under which two non-decreasing functions are non-negatively correlated, is Proposition 1 of Fortuin, Kasteleyn and Ginibre [fortuin1971, Proposition 1]. The library does not prove this inequality, and none of its declarations states it. The proof of the theorem needs one instance of it, on the lattice of the subsets of a finite set, for `Parking.Lam_first_cell`, the comparison on which the shortening inequality `Parking.Lam_shorten` of section 8 rests. The proof of `Parking.Lam_first_cell` derives this instance in an auxiliary step, which is not a declaration with a record of its own, from the four functions theorem, and that theorem comes from Mathlib (`Finset.four_functions_theorem`). For the quantity $\theta_n$ defined by $e^n/2=\sum_{i<n}n^i/i!+\theta_n\,n^n/n!$, Flajolet, Grabner, Kirschenhofer and Prodinger prove $\theta_n=\tfrac13+\tfrac{4}{135(n+k_n)}$ with $\tfrac{2}{21}\le k_n\le\tfrac{8}{45}$ for all integers $n\ge0$ [flajolet1995, Theorem 7]. The proof of the theorem uses the consequence $\theta_n\ge\tfrac13$ for $n\ge1$ and does not take it from the article: the inequality is proved, in the form $e^{-n}\sum_{i\le n}n^i/i!\le\tfrac12+\tfrac23\,e^{-n}n^n/n!$, as an auxiliary step of the proof of the bound for the mean of the birthday law (`Parking.sbirth_le`), which is not a declaration with a record of its own. The count $(n+1)^{n-1}$ of the parking functions of length $n$ is proved by Pollak's argument in Foata and Riordan [foatariordan1974, Section 2]: the street is replaced by a circle of $n+1$ spots, on which each of the $(n+1)^n$ sequences of $n$ preferences among the $n+1$ spots leaves exactly one spot empty; by symmetry every spot is left empty by equally many of these sequences, and the parking functions are the sequences that leave the spot $n+1$ empty. The library does not argue in this way. It proves $A_{n,\ell}=(n+1)^{n-1}$ for $n\le\ell+1$ (`Parking.A_eq_pow`), from the last-car recursion and from Abel's binomial identity. For these $n$ and $\ell$ the number $A_{n,\ell}$ is the count of all parking functions of length $n$, because none of them has a displacement larger than $n-1$; no declaration of the library states this last fact.

The three works cited in this paragraph, by Abel, by Aldous and Pitman, and by Konheim and Weiss, were consulted after the research, at the places given. During the research nobody read the article of Konheim and Weiss; no source was named for Abel's identity, so that Abel's note was not opened; and of the article of Aldous and Pitman only a passage other than the one cited here was read, around its formula (107). Abel's binomial identity, $\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk\,w_k(x)\,(y-k)^{n-k}=(x+y)^n$ with $w_0(x)=1$ and $w_k(x)=x\,(x+k)^{k-1}$ for $k\ge1$, is the case $\beta=-1$ of the identity that Abel states [abel1826, p. 159] and proves by an induction on $n$ that starts at $n=0$. In Abel's notation the identity that he states is $(x+\alpha)^n=\sum_{\mu=0}^{n}\binom n\mu\,\alpha\,(\alpha-\mu\beta)^{\mu-1}(x+\mu\beta)^{n-\mu}$ for arbitrary $x$, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and every positive integer $n$, the term with $\mu=0$ being $x^n$; the identity displayed first is its case $\beta=-1$ with $x$ and $y$ in the places of Abel's $\alpha$ and $x$. The library proves the identity for real $x$ and $y$ and every $n\ge0$ (`Parking.abel_identity`). The numbers $e^{-j}j^{j-1}/j!$, $j\ge1$, are the probabilities of the Borel distribution with parameter $1$, and that their sum is $1$ says that this distribution has total mass $1$. Aldous and Pitman state two facts from which this follows, and they do not write the sum out [aldouspitman1998, Section 2.2 and (10)]. The first, in their Section 2.2, is that the total progeny of a Galton–Watson process is finite with probability $1$ if and only if the mean $m$ of the offspring law is at most $1$. The second, their formula (10), is that when the offspring law is the Poisson law with mean $m$, the total progeny equals $j$ with probability $(mj)^{j-1}e^{-mj}/j!$; this is the Borel distribution with parameter $m$. The library proves the value of the sum without branching processes, by a renewal argument on partial sums (`Parking.hasSum_borel`). The count $(n+1)^{n-1}$, for which the previous paragraph cites Pollak's proof and says how the library stands to it, is also contained in the article of Konheim and Weiss [konheimweiss1966, Lemma 2 and Section 6]. Their Lemma 2 is $f(N,r)=N^{r-1}(N-r)$, written here with $N$ for the number of cells, where $f(N,r)$ is the number of ways to assign a cell to each of $r$ balls, among $N$ cells, such that the last cell stays empty when each ball in turn is placed in the first free cell at or after its assigned cell, the cells being taken cyclically. Their Section 6 gives the probability that $c$ cars all park in a street of $p$ places, each car preferring each place with probability $1/p$, as $f(p+1,c)/p^c$. By Lemma 2, $f(n+1,n)=(n+1)^{n-1}$. For $p=c=n$ the probability is therefore $(n+1)^{n-1}/n^n$, so that $(n+1)^{n-1}$ of the $n^n$ preference sequences of length $n$ are parking functions.

## Proof

Let $n\ge1$. Apply `Parking.unimodal_of_no_pair` to the sequence $f(\ell)=b_{n,\ell}$ and to $N=n-1$. That statement says: if there are no indices $1\le i<j\le N$ with $f(i)<f(i-1)$ and $f(j-1)<f(j)$, then there is $p\le N$ with $f(\ell-1)\le f(\ell)$ for $1\le\ell\le p$ and $f(\ell+1)\le f(\ell)$ for $p\le\ell\le N-1$. Its hypothesis is `Parking.no_descent_ascent`: for $1\le i<j\le n-1$ it is impossible that $b_{n,i}<b_{n,i-1}$ and $b_{n,j-1}<b_{n,j}$. Its conclusion, with $\ell^*=p$, is the theorem.

`Parking.no_descent_ascent` is in turn obtained from three results, as its record explains: `Parking.Tab.small_rows` for the rows $n\le16$; `Parking.left_half` for the rows $n\ge17$ with $n\ge2i$; and `Parking.right_tail`, which rests on `Parking.chain_column_iter` and `Parking.mono`, for the rows $n\ge17$ with $n\le2i-1$.

The hypothesis $n\ge1$ is not used in the argument. It belongs to the statement, where it makes $n-1$ the ordinary difference.
