# Unimodality of the number of parking functions by maximum displacement

This folder holds a formal proof, in Lean 4, that for every length the numbers of parking functions counted by their maximum displacement form a unimodal sequence. With the proof it holds a written record of every definition and result that the library states under a public name, a map of how the proof is built, the archived knowledge of the later of the two research runs behind it, and a history of that research. A paper presents the proof in ordinary mathematical language (see "The paper" below).

The proof was obtained with do-math, an autonomous research system in which AI sessions look for proofs, review them and formalize them in Lean. Two runs of do-math, one after the other, lie behind it.

## The result

Cars $1, \dots, n$ arrive in this order at a street with spots $1, \dots, n$. Car $i$ prefers spot $a_i$ and takes the first empty spot at or after it. The sequence $(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ is a *parking function* when every car finds a spot. The *displacement* of a car is the distance between the spot it takes and the spot it prefers, and $b_{n,\ell}$ is the number of parking functions of length $n$ whose largest displacement is exactly $\ell$.

**Theorem.** For every $n \ge 1$ the sequence $b_{n,0}, b_{n,1}, \dots, b_{n,n-1}$ is unimodal: for some $\ell^*$ it does not decrease up to $\ell^*$ and does not increase from $\ell^*$ on.

The statement was posed as Conjecture 6.5 by K. Celano, J. Elder, K. P. Hadaway, P. E. Harris, J. L. Martin, A. Priestley and G. Udell ([arXiv:2507.07243](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07243)), who tabulate the numbers for $n \le 9$.

In Lean the theorem is `Parking.root_unimodal`, in [`lean/Parking/Unimodal.lean`](lean/Parking/Unimodal.lean). As Lean prints it:

```
Parking.root_unimodal : ∀ (n : ℕ),
  1 ≤ n →
    ∃ p ≤ n - 1,
      (∀ (ℓ : ℕ), 1 ≤ ℓ → ℓ ≤ p → Parking.b n (ℓ - 1) ≤ Parking.b n ℓ) ∧
        ∀ (ℓ : ℕ), p ≤ ℓ → ℓ + 1 ≤ n - 1 → Parking.b n (ℓ + 1) ≤ Parking.b n ℓ
```

The objects it speaks of are defined in [`lean/Parking/Defs.lean`](lean/Parking/Defs.lean), directly from the parking process. After the research the library was simplified, as the section "How the proof was obtained" tells. The simplification did not touch this file: it is byte for byte the definitions file of the formal proof as the later run published it. In the archive under [`knowledge/`](knowledge/) that file has CRLF line endings, as most text files there have; with LF restored it is the same file, and [`knowledge/MANIFEST.md`](knowledge/MANIFEST.md) says why.

## What was checked, and what was not

The proof is complete in Lean 4.36.0 with Mathlib 4.36.0, and it depends on no axioms other than `propext`, `Classical.choice` and `Quot.sound`, the three axioms of Lean's standard library. Within the proof, thirty-eight statements are settled by a *table*, a finite computation in exact integer arithmetic that Lean's kernel evaluates itself (`decide +kernel`); nothing uses `native_decide`. The following checks were made. The library was changed once after it was first published in this folder on 23 November 2026, as the section "How the proof was obtained" tells, so each item says on which version it was made.

- The present library, 62 modules, was compiled from an empty folder by running `lean` on each module in the order of the imports. The compiler printed nothing for any module, neither an error nor a warning, and `Check.lean`, compiled last, printed the theorem with exactly those three axioms.
- do-math then took the present library in as its canon, the copy of the library that it keeps, and compiled it once more. The canon has two modules more than the library, one that states the theorem as posed and `Check.lean`. Of its 64 modules, the 28 that had changed or that import a changed module were compiled, with no error; the other 36 were taken from the compiled files of the first canon, whose sources and imports are the same. From the index that it built from the compiled canon, do-math counts the theorem as formally proved, resting on those three axioms and no others. The replay that the last item describes was switched off for this.
- `lake build`, the command a reader would use, was run on the library as first published, 61 modules. It compiled all of them, and `lake env lean Check.lean` printed the theorem with exactly those three axioms. It was run again on the present library on 24 November, in a copy of this folder: it compiled the modules that had changed or that import a changed module, took the others from the build of the first published version, whose sources are the same, and ended with "Build completed successfully", and `lake env lean Check.lean` printed the theorem with the same three axioms. For each import of a Mathlib module by a file of the library, `lake build` also prints a warning that Mathlib "is designed for use with the module system". The warning comes from Lake, the build tool, and not from the compiler. It is about the form of the files, none of which begins with the keyword `module`, and not about their content.
- The theorem and everything it rests on were exported and checked again by Lean's kernel outside the compiler (`lake comparator`), which accepted them. This replay was made on the library as first published. It was not repeated on the present library, which differs from that version in this: seven public declarations were added (`Parking.robbins_lt_factorial`, `Parking.near_term_le`, `Parking.strip_tlow_nonneg`, `Parking.strip_main`, and `Parking.beyond_strip`, `Parking.strip_step` and `Parking.row_peak` in the new module `Peak.lean`); `Parking.factorial_le_robbins` was replaced by the strict `Parking.factorial_lt_robbins`, and the two proofs that use it were adapted; `Parking.unimodal_of_no_pair` was generalised to values in any linearly ordered type; the proofs of `Parking.P1_le` and `Parking.crossing_strip` were rewritten; and five further lines of comments were corrected. The statement of the theorem and `Defs.lean` are unchanged. The comparator worked out the axioms afresh and replayed every exported declaration through the kernel. do-math made the check on its canon of that version, the copy of the library that it keeps, in which the proved theorem is named `Parking.root_unimodal_proof` and whose modules were otherwise byte for byte those then published. The check left one thing on trust. Lean code runs while it is compiled, and the comparator can build and export a library inside a sandbox, which confines that code. The sandbox needs Linux. On Windows, where the check was made, the comparator was given an export of modules that had been compiled beforehand and was run with its sandbox switched off. So the check showed that Lean's kernel accepts the exported proof with the three axioms; that the export is what the sources compile to rests on the compilation having done nothing but compile. [`lean/README.md`](lean/README.md) names the commands.

What these checks establish is that the Lean statement follows from the axioms. Three things in particular are not established by them.

## Outline of the proof

Picture the numbers of the theorem laid out in rows and columns: the length $n$ names the row and the displacement names the column. Write $k$ for the column, and for $1 \le k \le n-1$ write $d_{n,k} = b_{n,k} - b_{n,k-1}$ for the *step* of column $k$ in row $n$. Call the column *positive* in that row when this step is positive. A row that is not unimodal has a strict descent (a negative step) followed, further right, by a strict ascent (a positive step), so it is enough to exclude such a pair in every row.

Rows up to $n = 16$ are checked directly from a table. For a longer row the argument splits at the middle, and the two halves are settled by three statements about columns, one for the left half and two for the right. In the left half no descent occurs: for $n \ge 17$ and $2k \le n$ the step $d_{n,k}$ is nonnegative.

In the right half, two statements about columns exclude an ascent after a descent. The first says that a column $k$ which has turned positive stays positive as far as row $2k-1$: a column $k \ge 2$ that is positive in a row $n$ with $k+1 \le n \le 2k-2$ is positive in the next row. The second says that a column does not turn positive before the column to its left has: for $k \ge 9$ and $n \ge k+2$, if column $k$ is not positive in any of the rows $k+1, \dots, n$, then column $k+1$ is not positive in row $n$.

The two statements work together as follows. Take a row $n \ge 17$ with a descent at a column $k$ of its right half, so that $n \le 2k-1$ and therefore $k \ge 9$. Column $k$ cannot be positive in any of the rows $k+1, \dots, n$, because by the first statement a positive step in one of them would persist to row $n$. The second statement, applied in each of the rows from $k+2$ to $n$, then shows that column $k+1$ is not positive in those rows either. The same step leads from column $k+1$ to column $k+2$, and so on, column by column. So no step of row $n$ to the right of the descent is positive, and no ascent follows.

Everything starts from one combinatorial fact, which is proved from the parking process itself: a recursion on the spot taken by the last car, for the number of parking functions of length $n$ whose maximum displacement is at most a given bound. The research identifies it with a known theorem (Aguilar-Fraga et al., [arXiv:2311.14055](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14055), Theorem 3.7). After it the proof works with that recursion and with the series built from it. The recursion relates different lengths for one bound, whereas the theorem compares different displacements for one length. The proof therefore fixes a column, follows the sign of its step as $n$ grows, and passes from the columns to the rows only at the end.

Each of the three statements is proved by a table for the small columns and by an analytic argument for the large ones. The tools of these proofs are only named here. The record [`records/Parking/root_unimodal_proof.md`](records/Parking/root_unimodal_proof.md) sets them out in an account of the whole argument from the definitions to the theorem.

- **Left half.** Columns up to 81 are settled by a table together with a propagation argument, and columns from 82 on by an analytic argument at the critical tilt $x_k = (k/(k+1))^{k-1}$, which rests on a convexity along the diagonal for tilted masses.
- **Right half, first statement.** Columns up to 81 are settled by a table, and columns from 82 on analytically.
- **Right half, second statement.** Columns up to 264 are settled by a table, and columns from 265 on through an inequality for compositions weighted by Cayley numbers.

The analytic arguments use that every column $k \ge 265$ turns positive early: in some row $j > k$ with $25(j-k-1)^3 \le 121k^2$, that is, within a strip of width of order $k^{2/3}$. For the columns from 82 to 264 a table gives a row in which the column is positive.

The library also says where a row peaks, in statements that the proof of the theorem does not use ([`lean/Parking/Peak.lean`](lean/Parking/Peak.lean)). In every row $n \ge 335$ there is a column $p$ with $25(n-1-p)^3 \le 121(p+1)^2$ up to which the row does not decrease and from which it does not increase; it increases strictly from column 81 to column $p$ and decreases strictly from column $p+1$ on. So the maximum of such a row stands within $(121/25)^{1/3}\,n^{2/3}$ columns of its right end. This bounds the distance from above only. The constant $121/25$ is that of the strip of the previous paragraph, the range of rows in which the analytic arguments find a row where the column is positive; it comes from their estimates and has no meaning beyond them.

## How the proof was obtained

Two runs of do-math worked on the statement; the other documents in this folder call a run an *instance*. In a run, AI sessions work side by side and one after another: some look for proofs or formalize them in Lean, others check and write up what was produced, and a review session decides whether a write-up is published in the run's own repository. The changes counted below are a run's publications.

The earlier run worked from 5 to 13 November 2026 and reached a proof in words on 11 November; it published 438 changes. Sessions outside either run wrote a manuscript and a set of reports from it, and the later run was started on 19 November from those documents, and not from the records that the earlier run had published. Its proof is a different one from the earlier run's. Up to the formal proof, which it reached on 23 November, it published 120 changes; it went on running for some hours after that, and what it published then is not part of this account.

[`history/timeline.md`](history/timeline.md) tells this in detail, including what was tried and failed, and states which of its passages were compared with the sources by an independent reader and which were not. Its section 5 tells what passed from the earlier run to the later one and what did not. [`history/SOURCES.md`](history/SOURCES.md) says where the manuscript and the reports can be found.

The modules on which the theorem rests were 114 as the later run left them, with about 30,900 lines, of which about 28,000 remained once the declarations that the theorem does not use had been removed. They were then simplified to 61 modules, without changing the theorem or the definitions: results proved twice were merged, tables that checked overlapping facts were combined, detours were removed, and the modules were rearranged by subject. The number of statements settled by evaluating a table went down as well: there had been 61 of them once the declarations that the theorem does not use had been removed, and 38 remain. In the course of the simplification the library was also moved from Lean 4.32 to Lean 4.36.

After the paper was written the library was changed once more, so that every numbered statement of the paper, and every statement of its running text that had a number in the draft, is stated by a declaration of the library, with the exceptions of generality that the section "How the paper and the library correspond" names; this too was done by AI sessions. Where the paper stated more than a declaration, or stated for the numbers that the proof uses what the library proved only in general, a declaration was added or strengthened. Robbins' bounds for the factorial are now proved on both sides and strictly; the bound (12.2) of the paper and the two inequalities of its Section 9 are statements of their own; and the lemma that a sequence without a descent followed by an ascent is unimodal holds for values in any linearly ordered type. A module on where a row peaks was added, and the library now has 62 modules. A few comments in the sources were corrected as well. The theorem, the definitions and the number of statements settled by a table are as they were.

## The paper

[`paper/paper.pdf`](paper/paper.pdf) presents the proof in ordinary mathematical language: 143 pages in twenty sections, with 111 numbered statements and 19 references. It says nothing of Lean. It states the theorem and the three statements about columns, proves them as a mathematical paper does, describes the tables as exact computations with integers that a reader could repeat, and closes with the bound for the position of the maximum of a row. Its LaTeX source [`paper.tex`](paper/paper.tex) and its bibliography [`references.bib`](paper/references.bib) are beside it; it was typeset with Tectonic 0.15.

### How it was written and checked

The paper was written from the records by AI sessions, in three stages.

In the first stage do-math's paper command produced a draft. One session planned it and one session for each section wrote it. The draft was then read fourteen times: six times by a session that had the draft and nothing else, and eight times beside published research articles, by sessions that report where the draft is written otherwise than such articles are. Most readings were followed by repairs. At the end every numbered statement of the draft, 128 at that point, was compared with the declaration it states, as its record and its Lean statement give it, and all 128 were found to say the same. The command ended with fourteen findings of its last readers open.

In the second stage a further session, with agents of its own, resolved those fourteen. It cited classical results that the draft had proved in full, turned statements that follow at once from a definition into displayed formulas or sentences, replaced three lemmas stated for free parameters by what they give at the values the proof uses, stated the three statements about columns in the introduction, corrected two places where the paper's sketch of its proof disagreed with the body, and displayed a computation that one proof had only asserted. Every changed passage was read where it stands by an agent that had not written it. A script of this stage assembled the final text with the command's own code, put it through the command's mechanical checks, checked it for unresolved references and for lines that leave the text block, and typeset it. The command itself was not run on the final text, because it provides neither for a reference outside the bibliography of the records nor for a numbered statement that carries a citation in place of a proof.

In the third stage the session that brought the parts of this folder into agreement changed the paper in a few places, mostly through agents of its own. A sentence on the degree of a polynomial, which was false in a case that the proofs do not use, was corrected. The bibliography now names the second version of the preprint that poses the conjecture, which is the version that the paper cites, under the title of that version. A sentence after Proposition 1.3 states the form in which two proofs apply that proposition. And a closing subsection, announced in the Introduction, states and proves the bound for the position of the maximum. Every changed passage was read where it stands by an agent that had not written it, and the text was assembled, checked and typeset by the script of the second stage.

On 25 November 2026 the sentences of the introduction that say what four cited works contain were corrected, after the works had been opened and read at the places cited. The theorem of Pappe is about the generating polynomial of the sum of the preferences, a statistic that his note calls essentially equivalent to the displacement, and not about the total displacement itself. Ager-Hart and her coauthors count the rankings in which at most $\ell+1$ competitors tie at a rank, and do not call them a subfamily of the $\ell$-interval parking functions. The count of the parking functions follows from Lemma 2 and formula (6.1) of Konheim and Weiss and is not printed in their Section 6. And the limit theorem of Petersson is cited from the summary of his doctoral thesis, which states it and was read, where the paper had cited his journal article, which could not be opened; the theorem is now stated in the form in which the thesis states it. The section "Earlier work" of the record `records/Parking/root_unimodal_proof.md` was corrected in the same places, and there also the place at which Selig and Zhu say that maximum displacement and height can differ, their Remark 2.3. The corrections were written by one AI session and compared with what had been read in the works by another. No numbered statement changed. The paper was typeset once more, with Tectonic 0.15 as before, and a comparison of its text with the text before showed no difference outside the corrected sentences; it has 143 pages where it had 142.

### How the paper and the library correspond

[`paper/correspondence.json`](paper/correspondence.json) names, for each of the 111 numbered statements, the declaration that the statement states, and each carries the verdict that it says the same. Of the 111, 108 have exactly the text that was compared in the first stage; two received a citation in the second stage and were compared again; and one, the proposition on the position of the maximum, was added in the third stage and compared then.

Under `changes` the file lists 31 declarations: those whose standing in the paper changed after the first stage (a number lost, a statement moved, a proof replaced by a citation), and those that were added to the library after it. Eighteen had a number of their own in the draft and have none now, which is why the second stage left 110 numbered statements where the draft had 128. Of these eighteen, fourteen say the same in the running text. One, the lemma on sequences, is stated by the library for values in any linearly ordered type, and the sentence of the paper is its case of real numbers. Three are not stated in the paper in their general form: two lemmas on arbitrary real numbers, of which the paper states and proves what they give for the numbers of its Section 9, and the comparison of the bound (12.2) with an arbitrary number, which the paper makes for four numbers inside a proof. The two statements of Section 9 and the bound (12.2) have been declarations of the library since the third stage. Six further entries are the three propositions stated in the introduction and three statements of which a proof, or a step of a proof, was replaced by a citation. The last seven are declarations added to the library in the third stage: four state what the paper already stated, and three are the statements on the position of the maximum, which were added to the paper at the same time. Each of the seven says the same as the paper. The strict upper bound of Robbins, which replaced the non-strict bound of the draft in the third stage, is counted among the eighteen.

The file names declarations as the do-math canon made from the library names them. There the proved theorem is `Parking.root_unimodal_proof`; in [`lean/`](lean/) it is `Parking.root_unimodal`.

### What to know before relying on the paper

- Lean has checked the library in [`lean/`](lean/), not the paper, and no person has refereed the paper. What ties the paper to the library is the comparison of its numbered statements with declarations, which AI sessions made. A proof in the paper tells the argument of a record, and a record tells the argument of a Lean proof; neither of these two steps was verified by a machine.
- By the comparisons that `correspondence.json` records, every numbered statement of the paper is stated by a declaration of the library, and so is every statement of the running text that `changes` lists, that is, every statement that had a number in the draft and every statement for which a declaration was added since, with the one difference of generality named above for the lemma on sequences. The other sentences of the running text, among them the sentences on the maximum that accompany Proposition 20.3, were not compared with declarations one by one.
- The paper takes seven results from the literature without proving them: the recursion on the last car, Abel's binomial identity, Robbins' bounds for the factorial, the total mass of the Borel distribution, an inequality for the Poisson law ($\theta_m \ge \tfrac13$), the count $(n+1)^{n-1}$ of parking functions, and the correlation inequality of Fortuin, Kasteleyn and Ginibre. In the paper these rest on the cited sources. The Lean proof does not rest on them: the library proves the first six for itself, the fifth as a step inside another proof, and for the seventh it derives the one instance that it needs from the four functions theorem of Mathlib.
- Seven of the nineteen references were added in the second stage. Each was confirmed in a bibliographic registry and opened for what the paper attributes to it, two of them only at the pages concerned. The article of Konheim and Weiss was read in full, in a copy of JSTOR's page scan of the printed journal that stands on a university page; the publisher's own file could not be opened, so the scan was not compared with it. Four of the nineteen are not among the fifteen works that the records cite: a survey of parking functions, two articles on hashing with linear probing and a survey of unimodal sequences, which the introduction of the paper cites to place the problem. One of the nineteen changed on 25 November 2026, as said above: the summary of the doctoral thesis of Petersson took the place of his journal article.
